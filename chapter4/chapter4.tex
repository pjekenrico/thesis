\begin{tcolorbox}[abstract]

	After an introduction to the computation of hemodynamics in human brain arterial systems, we dwell on the modelling of one of the available tools to treat IA, flow-diverter stents. The chapter first provides a brief description of the stents, and their field of application and outlines the areas of remaining research questions. Then a comprehensive guide on generating virtual stents based on geometrical constraints will be given. The tool is then validated against deployments found in literature, in-vitro experiments conducted during this work and lastly against a real patient case. The last case is exploited to describe an accessible workflow to embed thin devices, such as flow-diverter stents, into unstructured tetrahedral meshing using anisotropic meshing and level-set methods. The contents of this chapter are largely based on a publication of the author, currently under production at ``Computers in Biology and Medicine''\,\cite{Jeken-Rico2024}.

\section{Flow-Diverter Stents}
Since their introduction in the early 2000s, Flow-Diverter Stents (FDSs) have exhibited a heterogeneous performance, posing challenges to overall assessment\,\cite{Dmytriw2019, Maragkos2020}. Initially, these devices were introduced as supplementary tools for neuroradiologists tackling complex IA, especially those in which other treatments were inapplicable or would most likely pose a large risk to the patient. Examples include wide-neck or fusiform aneurysms where conventional coils struggle to secure attachment and are likely to cause complications. In contrast, FDSs are braided tubular structures deployed within the parent artery to cover the IA neck thereby redirecting the flow away from it. Subsequently, the intra-aneurysmal flow is reduced, promoting thrombus formation, reducing the rupture risk and facilitating the contraction of the aneurysmal bulge. The effectivity of the device is commonly assessed through its Metal Coverage Ratio (MCR), with values around 30\% being optimal\,\cite{Jou2016, Maragkos2020}. Studies and meta-analyses indicate occlusion rates ranging from 49\% to 100\%, with around 60\% of cases experiencing bulge reabsorption (see\,\cite{Dmytriw2019, Maragkos2020, Ngoepe2018} and therein). The substantial variations in success rates across treatment centres, IA locations, and device types have spurred engineers to delve into FDs' mechanical and hemodynamic interactions to optimize their efficacy. Since the beginning of the decade, computational fluid and solid dynamics have provided valuable insights into the localized effects of FD deployment without posing additional risks to patients. While simulations offer detailed hemodynamic information compared to experimental flow estimations, their accuracy relies on precisely segmenting the patient's lumen and, crucially, a physiologically accurate representation of the FD\@.

Among the pioneering works in this field is the virtual stent deployment technique introduced by Mut \textit{et\,al.}\,(2009)\,\cite{Mut2009} and Cebral \textit{et\,al.}\,(2005)\,\cite{Cebral2005}. In this approach, the FD is modelled as an elastic membrane that deforms in the vessel to accommodate internal smoothing forces and external attractive forces. The wires are then winded regularly in the computed hull without accounting for compaction effects due to bending. Cebral \textit{et\,al.}\,(2011)\,\cite{Cebral2011} then uses this method to study several stented giant aneurysms to explore reasons for rupture occurrences. In 2012, Ma \textit{et\,al.}\,\cite{Ma2012} employed Finite Element simulations to carry out the complete FD deployment, validating their results through an in-vitro experiment with favourable outcomes. Simultaneously, Larrabide \textit{et\,al.}\,(2012)\,\cite{Larrabide2012} introduced a constrained simplex deformable model, and validated it via ink density tracking in both experimental and simulated cases, yielding satisfactory results. While highly versatile, this method's drawback lies in the modelling errors in IA neck sections as well as tortuous geometries, where determining an initial condition for stent expansion proves challenging, following the author's statements. Subsequently, Ouared \textit{et\,al.}\,(2016)\,\cite{Ouared2016} utilized this method to investigate 12 patient cases treated with FDSs, indicating that a minimal flow reduction of one-third relative to unstented hemodynamics is necessary for successful occlusion. Among the latest contributions, Zavodszky \textit{et\,al.}\,(2020)\,\cite{Zavodszky2020} modelled the stent through a system of damped springs, enabling a comprehensive application of the push-and-pull technique\,\cite{Shapiro2014a}. Contrary to most publications, this method was thoroughly validated in different deployment scenarios yielding mostly satisfying results over the whole device length excepting the neck area. Unfortunately, the kinks inherent to the spring-based discretization are problematic on a fluid-mechanical level potentially causing adverse turbulent effects.

Geometry-based approaches have also been introduced, such as the one of Janiga \textit{et\,al.}\,(2013)\,\cite{Janiga2013}, where Free-Form Deformations (FFDs) are used to model the deployed stent's hull. The stent wires are then weaved over the deformed hull following angle measurements from post-operative images. The latter method has undergone validation using patient CT images and has been employed in subsequent studies\,\cite{Berg2016, Sindeev2018}, including the illustrative review by Berg \textit{et\,al.}\,(2017)\,\cite{Berg2017}. Furthermore, Bouillot \textit{et\,al.}\,(2016)\,\cite{Bouillot2016} made a significant contribution by addressing the challenge of wire compaction due to bending, a common phenomenon observed with FDSs\,\cite{Shapiro2014a}. They derived a set of analytical equations describing this phenomenon, which exhibited robustness and demonstrated good agreement with experimental data.

In this work, we propose several enhancements to improve the accuracy of virtual FD deployment. Building on prior research and addressing identified shortcomings, our approach includes: (i) introducing a rotation-minimizing vessel parametrization, (ii) deriving and validating a new winding rule through literature examples and conducted experiments, (iii) applying Free-Form Deformations of the stent to the vessel walls, and (iv) providing a comprehensive integration into 3D volumetric meshes using accessible, open-source tools. It is important to highlight that these advancements aim to facilitate the use of embedded devices in fluid dynamics simulations, thus enhancing the reliability and effectiveness of stent deployment in clinical applications. Finally, we propose several validations first against literature examples, then in-vitro experiments, and patient data, to demonstrate the robustness and accuracy of the proposed approach.

\subsection{Vessel Parametrization}
\subsubsection*{Centerline}
The geometric description of the vasculature surrounding the aneurysm is the first component to explore since it will provide the scaffold upon which the geometrical deployment will function. Thanks to the extensive and openly available toolkit \href{http://www.vmtk.org/}{\textit{vmtk}}\,\cite{Antiga2008, Izzo2018} one can compute the centerlines, average radii, as well as many other centerline characteristics. The interface on \href{https://www.slicer.org/}{\textit{3D Slicer}}\,\cite{Kikinis2014} is recommended for its ease of use and the possibility to modify the course of the centerline at the aneurysm neck\,\cite{Fedorov2012}. To have the best possible fit with the CFD meshes, we employ the preexisting segmentation for extracting the information. The centerlines, being the core component, are retrieved by minimizing the maximal inscribed spheres along a path in the vessel. From there on, the mean radii are calculated which can help sizing the FD later on. Apart from the positioning, the centerline encodes several useful information, such as the curvature and the torsion of the vessel. The curvature is a measure of how much the centerline deviates from a straight line and is an essential requirement for the computation of the stent's deployment. To obtain this information smooth BSplines are fitted to the centerline points to generate a continuous curve. Take $\vec\gamma(t):[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^3$ as to be such a curve composed by cubic BSplines, i.e. $\gamma_{i}(t)\in C^2$. Suppose also that there exists a unique parametrization $s(t)$ such that condition\,\ref{eqn:def_arc} holds.
\begin{equation}
	\norm{\frac{d\vec{\gamma}(s)}{ds}}_2^2 = {\left(\frac{d}{ds}x(s)\right)}^2 + {\left(\frac{d}{ds}y(s)\right)}^2 + {\left(\frac{d}{ds}z(s)\right)}^2 = 1,\quad\forall t\in[0,1]
	\label{eqn:def_arc}
\end{equation}
\begin{figure}[htb]
	\centering
	\input{chapter4/Figures/Stent/CurveAnalysis.tex}
	\vspace*{-0.5cm}
	\caption[Centerline sketch]{Left: Sketch of vessel with parametrized centerline (dashed line) with the unit tangent vector $\vec{T}$ and major radius $\vec{R}$. Right: Cross-section view of vessel with mean inscribed radius $\overline{r}$ and with orthogonal basis $\vec{e}_1,\,\vec{e}_2$.}
\end{figure}
For the stent's generation, it will be necessary to compute the curve's unit tangent $\vec{T}$ and major radius $\vec{R}$ as well as the orthogonal basis $\vec{e}_1,\,\vec{e}_2$ to generate the struts. $\vec{T}$ denotes the momentaneous velocity direction of the curve and is therefore given by \autoref{eqn:tangent}.
\begin{equation}
	\vec{T}(t) = \frac{d}{dt}\vec{\gamma}(t)\norm{\frac{d}{dt}\vec{\gamma}(t)}^{-1} = \dot{\vec{\gamma}}(t)\norm{\dot{\vec{\gamma}}(t)}^{-1}
	\label{eqn:tangent}
\end{equation}
Since the parametrization in $s$ is per-definition an arc parametrization we know that the relationship holds:
\begin{equation*}
	\norm{\frac{d \vec{\gamma}(t) }{dt}}_2 = \norm{\frac{d\vec{\gamma}(s)}{ds}{\frac{ds}{dt}}}_2 = \norm{\frac{d\vec{\gamma}(s)}{ds}}_2\snorm{\frac{ds}{dt}}=\frac{ds}{dt}
\end{equation*}
The major radius is defined with the help of the Frenet-Serret equations as the second derivative in the arc parametrization $s$. The derivation with respect to $t$ is shown below.
\begin{align*}
	\vec{R}(t)             & = \frac{d\vec{T}(s)}{ds} = \frac{d\vec{T}(t)}{dt} \frac{dt}{ds}                                                                                                                                                                  \\
	\frac{d\vec{T}(t)}{dt} & = \frac{d}{dt}{\left(\frac{d}{dt}\vec{\gamma}(t)\norm{\frac{d}{dt}\vec{\gamma}(t)}^{-1}_2\right)}                                                                                                                                \\
	                       & = \norm{\frac{d}{dt}\vec{\gamma}(t)}^{-1}_2\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\vec{\gamma}(t) + \frac{d}{dt}\vec{\gamma}(t) \frac{d}{dt}\norm{\frac{d}{dt}\vec{\gamma}(t)}^{-1}_2                                                                   \\
	                       & = \norm{\frac{d}{dt}\vec{\gamma}(t)}^{-1}_2\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\vec{\gamma}(t) - \norm{\frac{d}{dt}\vec{\gamma}(t)}^{-2}_2\frac{d}{dt}\vec{\gamma}(t) \frac{d}{dt}\norm{\frac{d}{dt}\vec{\gamma}(t)}_2                               \\
	                       & = \norm{\frac{d}{dt}\vec{\gamma}(t)}^{-1}_2\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\vec{\gamma}(t) - \norm{\frac{d}{dt}\vec{\gamma}(t)}^{-3}_2\frac{d}{dt}\vec{\gamma}(t) {\left(\frac{d}{dt}\vec{\gamma}(t)\cdot\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\vec{\gamma}(t)\right)}
\end{align*}
As a result in the short-hand notation for the derivatives, the major radius may be expressed as in \autoref{eq:major_R}.
\begin{equation}
	\vec{R}(t) = \norm{\dot{\vec{\gamma}}(t)}^{-2}_2\ddot{\vec{\gamma}}(t) - \norm{\dot{\vec{\gamma}}(t)}^{-4}_2\dot{\vec{\gamma}}(t) \left\langle \dot{\vec{\gamma}}(t),\ddot{\vec{\gamma}}(t) \right\rangle
	\label{eq:major_R}
\end{equation}

With known expressions for $\vec{T}$ and $\vec{R}$, it might be tempting to use them as a reference basis on the curve together with the bi-normal vector $\vec{B} = \vec{T}\times\vec{R}$. However, this basis is not suitable for the stent deployment, as it will twist along the curve. Instead, we require a Rotation Minimizing Frame (RMF), a unique reference curve that moves along the centerline indicating one of the orthogonal directions. It is proven that for any curve $\vec{\gamma}\in C^1$ there exists a unique RMF that minimizes the rotation of the basis vectors along the curve and builds a basis with the tangent vector and the binormal\,\cite{Wang2008}. Many different approaches have been proposed to compute RMF due to their common application in computer graphics. In this project, we approximate it using the Double-Reflection method presented by Wang \textit{et\,al.}\,\cite{Wang2008}. It enjoys a fourth-order convergence based on the number of subdivisions of the curve while being computationally cheap. Algorithm~\ref{alg:double_reflection} shows the iterative rule to follow over each discrete point on the centerline. It requires the tangent vector provided by \autoref{eqn:tangent} and an initial frame $(\vec{e}_T, \vec{e}_1,\vec{e}_2)$ which can be obtained by the Frenet-Serret formulas (\autoref{eqn:frenet}). Once the entire centerline is swept, it is recommended that smooth splines be fitted to the RMF to evaluate at any point in later stages.
\begin{algorithm}
	\caption{Double-Reflection Method for RMF computation\,\cite{Wang2008}}\label{alg:double_reflection}
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]
		\Require{Points $\vec{\gamma}_i$ and associated unit tangent vectors $\vec{e}_{T,i}$, $i = 0, 1, \ldots, n$.}
		\Require{An initial frame $(\vec{e}_{T,0}, \vec{e}_{1,0}, \vec{e}_{2,0})$.}
		\State\textbf{Begin}
		\For{$i = 0$ to $n - 1$}
		\State\textbf{Begin}
		\State$\vec{v}_1 :=\vec{\gamma}_{i+1} - \vec{\gamma}_i$
		\State$c_1 :=\vec{v}_1 \cdot \vec{v}_1$
		\State$\vec{e}_{1, i}^L:= \vec{e}_1 - \frac{2}{c_1} (\vec{v}_1 \cdot \vec{e}_1)\vec{v}_1$
		\State$\vec{e}_{T,i}^L:=\vec{e}_{T,i} - \frac{2}{c_1} (\vec{v}_1 \cdot \vec{e}_{T,i})\vec{v}_1$
		\State$\vec{v}_2:= \vec{e}_{T,i+1} - \vec{e}_{T,i}^L$
		\State$c_2 := \vec{v}_2 \cdot \vec{v}_2$
		\State$\vec{e}_{1,i+1}:=\vec{e}_{1, i}^L - \frac{2}{c_2} (\vec{v}_2 \cdot \vec{e}_1^L)\vec{v}_2$
		\State$\vec{e}_{2,i+1}:=\vec{e}_{T,i+1}\times\vec{e}_{1, i+1}$
		\State\textbf{End}
		\EndFor{}
		\State\textbf{End}
	\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
A stark contrast between the RMF and the Frenet frames is illustrated in \autoref{fig:coord_system}, where a centerline composed of two opposing circle sections was used to deploy an illustrative stent. The major radius $\vec{R}$ is discontinuous at the merging point, thus leading to a sharp turn of the wires if only using the Frenet-frames (see \autoref{eqn:frenet}). Conversely, the RMF on the right is impervious to the infection point and prevents excessive spinning of the wires.

\subsubsection*{Vessel Wall}
With the obtained RMF it is possible to generate a smooth hull around the centerline that resembles the vessel's wall but excludes any formation that extends beyond the reach of the stent. The hull can, depending on the user specifications, be restricted to have a fully circular profile or to adapt itself to the local features of the walls. This permits on one hand deploying the stent later into a more realistic environment, and on the other hand generating a continuous porosity field for visual inspection, as shown in \autoref{fig:vessel_wall}. To generate the hull, radial distances between the centerline and the vessel wall are computed along equidistant positions. More precisely, for each location on the centerline, a fixed number of rays in the $\vec{e}_1, \vec{e}_2$-plane are cast to the vessel wall. Using the Moller-Trumbore algorithm\,\cite{Moller1997}, the intersection points are computed and the distance to the centerline is stored. Here too, the distances can be fed into a smooth bivariate Spline $r(t, \theta)$ to obtain a continuous representation of the vessel wall.
\begin{figure}[htb]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{chapter4/Figures/Coordinate_System/Hull.png}
	\caption[Stent hull]{Example of a stent hull at the ICA siphon, covering the PCom and a blister aneurysm. The envelope's mesh is blended in to show the structured pattern that has been employed.}\label{fig:vessel_wall}
\end{figure}

The hull, which can be seen as the future stent envelope, can be adjusted to the vessel to incorporate local deformations. When doing so, it is important to respect the limits imposed by the vessel surface, \textit{i.\,e.} that the hull does not extend beyond the vessel wall. In the work of Janiga \textit{et\,al.}\,(2013)\,\cite{Janiga2013} this is achieved through a progressive growth of the envelope from the inside until the limits of the vessel are hit. Here, we opt for a simple iterative process, in which first the radius map $r(t, \theta)$ is smoothed with either a Gaussian filter or spline smoothing, and then a new mapping is fitted with resampled points that are limited to the vessel wall. The process is repeated until the hull is sufficiently adapted to the vessel wall. At this stage it is recommended to check the angiography images to confirm that the stent is well adhered to the vessel wall, and if that is not the case, to modify the centerline accordingly.

\subsection{Stent Properties}
The stents adopt their nominal lengths and diameters under unconstrained conditions. When radially compressed, the wires bend in the axial direction, causing the stent to grow in length and its pores to grow wider, as can be seen in \autoref{fig:stent_elongation}. Then from an angle of 45$^\circ$ onwards, pores between the wires start to close, leading to a decrease in porosity. To aid physicians in choosing the correct device, producers add basic information points relating to diameter and device length, as well as some porosity estimators. This information, while being a good starting point, is not sufficient to accurately model the stent deployment, since many other factors, such as the bending of the device also play a role.

Braided flow-diverter stents have been observed not to allow sliding of the wires on each other, but rather to have a fixed position. Instead, when bending or elongating the device, the angles between the clockwise and counterclockwise wires $\alpha$ change\,\cite{Bouillot2014,Bouillot2016,Shapiro2014a}. This means, that the inter-wire distance $w$ remains constant and it determines together with the angle $\alpha$ and the wire diameter $d_w$ the porosity $\varphi$ of the stent. For its definition we use the formula provided by Bouillot \textit{et\,al.}\,\cite{Bouillot2016} (\autoref{eq:porosity}) or equivalently the one provided by Rebelo \textit{et\,al.}\,\cite{Rebelo2015} (\autoref{eq:porosity2}).
\begin{align}
	\varphi(\alpha) & = {\left( 1 - \frac{d_w}{w\sin{2\alpha}} \right)}^2, \qquad \alpha \in \left[\frac{\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{d_w}{w}\right)}{2}, \frac{\pi - \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{d_w}{w}\right)}{2}\right]
	\label{eq:porosity}                                                                                                                                                                                      \\
	\varphi(\alpha) & = {\left( 1 - \frac{N_w d_w}{4\pi r \cos{\alpha}} \right)}^2
	\label{eq:porosity2}
\end{align}
These expressions are equivalent considering the definition of the braiding angle $\alpha$ as shown in \autoref{eq:winding_angle}. The previously introduced metal coverage ratio is closely linked to porosity and is more commonly used since it is more intuitive. Its definition is found in \autoref{eq:MCR}.
\begin{equation}
	\text{MCR} = (1 - \varphi)\times 100\%
	\label{eq:MCR}
\end{equation}

Note, that all these formulas neglect the curvature of the stent surface and assume the diamond-shaped cells to be spanned in one plane. In that sense, the overlapping segments between wires are not considered, which is a common simplification in the stent modelling\,\cite{Bouillot2016, Larrabide2012}.
\begin{equation}
	\alpha = \sin^{-1}{\left(\frac{2\pi r}{wN_w}\right)}
	\label{eq:winding_angle}
\end{equation}
Since neither $w$ nor $\alpha$ are usually found in the device specifications, it is necessary to compute them from the information on the devices' elongation and contraction (see \autoref{fig:stent_elongation}). With that, we can first compute the wire length $l_w$:
\begin{equation*}
	l_w = \frac{L_1}{\cos(\alpha(r_1))} = \frac{L_2}{\cos(\alpha(r_2))}
\end{equation*}
\begin{equation}
	L_1\sqrt{1-{\left(\frac{2\pi r_2}{wN_w}\right)}^2} = L_2\sqrt{1-{\left(\frac{2\pi r_1}{wN_w}\right)}^2}
	\label{eq:clearing_stent}
\end{equation}
By clearing \autoref{eq:clearing_stent} we obtain the expressions for $l_w$ and $w$. Common values for $w$ usually lay around 0.15 to 0.25\,\si{mm}, while $l_w$ is a multiple of the stent length.
\begin{equation*}
	\begin{split}
		w &= \frac{2\pi}{N_w}\sqrt{\frac{L_1^2r_2^2 - L_2^2r_1^2}{L_1^2-L_2^2}} = \frac{2\pi}{N_w}r_e\\
		l_w &= \frac{r_e}{\sqrt{r_e^2-{r_1}^2}}L_1
	\end{split}
\end{equation*}

\subsection{Deployment}
\subsubsection*{Cylindric Deployment}
When deploying a stent in a cylinder of variable diameter, the wires evolve, as previously described with the angle $\alpha$ given by \autoref{eq:winding_angle}. Over an infinitesimal section of the stent, the angular progression in $\theta$ (see \autoref{fig:StentAnalysis}), describing the angle in the radial direction of the wires, is constant.
\begin{equation*}
	r\,d\theta = dl\sin{\alpha} = \frac{2\pi r}{wN_w}dl
\end{equation*}
Therefore, integrating it over a constant wire segment length $||\vec{dl}||=dl$ yields \autoref{eqn:theta_cyl}.
\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
		\theta_{cyl} &= \int_{\theta_0}^{\theta_{cyl}}d\theta = \int_0^l \frac{2\pi}{wN_w}\,dl + \theta_0 = 2\pi \frac{l}{wN_w} + \theta_0\\
		\theta_{cyl} &= \frac{l}{r_e}+ \theta_0
	\end{split}
	\label{eqn:theta_cyl}
\end{equation}
With this information, it is already possible to deploy a stent in a straight cylinder with varying sizes. As a first verification of this method, a deployment from Shapiro \textit{et\,al.}\,\cite{Shapiro2014a} was recreated for a 4.75\,mm PED using the described smooth stent adaptation. The confined deployment in the assembly of cylinders of varying diameter alters angles and the MCR, whose values are given in \autoref{fig:steps}. The largest cylinder ($d$\,=\,5\,mm) surpasses the nominal diameter of the device thereby pushing the computed angle beyond the limits of applicability of our model and of the porosity formula (\autoref{eq:porosity}). With this test, we have confirmed that a good approximation is possible and that the next step, the deployment of bent vessels, is ready to be tackled.

\subsubsection*{Toroidal Deployment}
If a wire segment $\vec{dl}$ is traced on a toroidal envelope, as depicted in \autoref{fig:StentAnalysis}, its progression can be expressed through:
\begin{equation*}
	\vec{dl} = \begin{pmatrix}
		dt(1-\frac{\vec{R}}{R^2}\cdot r(\vec{e}_1\cos{\theta} + \vec{e}_2\sin{\theta})) \\rd\theta
	\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}
		dt(1-\frac{\vec{R}}{R^2}\cdot \vec{r}) \\rd\theta
	\end{pmatrix}
\end{equation*}
For convenience, the wire position vector $\vec{r}$ was introduced in terms of the RMF basis $\vec{e}_1, \vec{e}_2$. The norm of $\vec{dl}$ ($dl$), \textit{i.\,e.} the distance covered by a small wire segment, is a quantity fixed by construction to be the same for all wires of a toroidal section (see \autoref{eqn:winding}).
\begin{equation}
	dl = dt\sqrt{{\left(1 - \frac{\vec{R}}{R^2}\cdot \vec{r}\right)}^2 + {\left(\frac{r\,d\theta}{dt}\right)}^2 }
	\label{eqn:winding}
\end{equation}
Given that all wires within the infinitesimal toroidal section advance along the centerline by the same distance $dt$, the square root term must also remain constant for all wires. Consistent with Bouillot \textit{et\,al.}\,\cite{Bouillot2016}, we introduce the winding factor $K$ in \autoref{eqn:winding}, which remains constant within one infinitesimal toroidal segment.
\begin{equation}
	K = \sqrt{{\left(1 - \frac{\vec{R}}{R^2}\cdot \vec{r}\right)}^2 + {\left(\frac{rd\theta}{dt}\right)}^2 }
	\label{eqn:winding_factor}
\end{equation}
The winding factor is a measure of how much the wires evolve in the radial direction to the axial one. Therefore, a high value of $K$ indicates a strong radial progression of the wires and effects like stent shortening. With $K$, the angle $\alpha$ may be written out as shown in \autoref{eqn:winding2}. The same expression can be used to integrate numerically the angles $\theta$ for deployment, although it is not recommended. Instead, a more sophisticated approach is proposed in the next section.
\begin{equation}
	\sin{\alpha} = \frac{r\,d\theta}{dl} = \sqrt{1 -{\frac{ {\left(1 -  \frac{\vec{R}}{R^2}\cdot \vec{r}\right)}^2}{{K}^2}}}
	\label{eqn:winding2}
\end{equation}
To extract the angular progression from \autoref{eqn:winding2}, it is necessary to derive a closure rule that relies on another information known \textit{a priori}. Contrary to the one provided by Bouillot \textit{et\,al.}\,\cite{Bouillot2016}, we propose here to derive it from the added radial displacements from all the wires in one cross-section segment. When adding them up, one obtains a perimeter slightly smaller than the one for a circle of the same radius.
\begin{equation}
	\sum_{i=1}^{N_w} w\sin{\alpha_i} = \frac{2\pi}{N_w}r_e \sum_{i=1}^{N_w} \sqrt{1 -{\frac{ {\left(1 -  \frac{\vec{R}}{R^2}\cdot \vec{r}_i\right)}^2}{{K}^2}}}
	\label{eqn:rad_displ}
\end{equation}
Taking the limit of $N_w$ to infinity, \textit{i.\,e.} subdividing the angular spacings into infinitesimal portions, brings us to the integral formulation. This is possible because $r_e$ is solely dependent on the winding angle and not on the wire spacing, which is instead accounted for by $2\pi/N_w$.
\begin{equation}
	\lim_{N_w\rightarrow\infty} r_e\frac{2\pi}{N_w} \sum_{i=1}^{N_w} \sqrt{1 -{\frac{ {\left(1 -  \frac{\vec{R}}{R^2}\cdot \vec{r}_i\right)}^2}{{K}^2}}} = r_e \int_0^{2\pi} \sqrt{1 -{\frac{ {\left(1 -  \frac{\vec{R}}{R^2}\cdot \vec{r}\right)}^2}{{K}^2}}}\,d\theta = 2\pi r
	\label{eqn:rad_displ2}
\end{equation}
The result of the left-hand-side of \autoref{eqn:rad_displ2} needs to equate the circumference of the stent cross-section.
The result can be reformulated into a root finding problem (see \autoref{eqn:compute_K}) and solved using Newton's method.
\begin{equation}
	f(K) = \int_{0}^{2\pi}{ \frac{r}{r_e} - \sqrt{1 -{\frac{ {\left(1 -  \frac{\vec{R}}{R^2}\cdot \vec{r}\right)}^2}{K^2}}}}\,d\theta
	\label{eqn:compute_K}
\end{equation}
A more detailed description of the winding's behaviour is seen on \autoref{fig:example_Ks}, where the same flow diverter is deployed in three configurations. There are two main trends to consider, the first being the dropping dependency of $K$ on $R$ when $R\rightarrow\infty$. This is expected since an infinite major radius corresponds to a straight cylinder. Secondly, the largest change of the winding is concentrated at deployments close to the nominal radius and above it in highly bent environments. These large differences in MCR can be expected based on the curvature direction of the vessel. The white region of the colourmap covers the section where wires at the outer rim of the torus would stretch beyond their length following the computation.

\subsubsection*{Generalized Deployment}
To extend the obtained results to arbitrary geometries, we will generate a smooth mapping from a cylindrical stent to a curved stent following the vessel walls. To maintain clarity we will enumerate the steps to follow.
\begin{enumerate}
	\item The centerline $\vec{\gamma}$ is read in and the RMF (\autoref{eqn:coordinate_sys}) and Frenet frames (\autoref{eqn:tangent} and \autoref{eq:major_R}) are computed on its entire length. For each function, smooth splines are fit to ensure that they can be evaluated for any position $t$ along the centerline.
	\item A smooth hull $r(t,\theta)$ is generated around the centerline to represent the vessel wall. For further computations, it needs to be decided if the minimal radius or a mean radius per section is used. Only minimal differences are expected between the two approaches since commonly the vessel wall will tend to become more regular under the influence of a fully expanded FD\,\cite{Bouillot2016}.
	\item A distal position $t_0$ is chosen by the user from which to start deploying the device. The wirelength is discretized into small segments of fixed length $dl$ which will be swept over the entire length of the stent.
	\item Starting from $t_0$, \autoref{eqn:compute_K} is solved. With it, $t_1$ is computed as $dl / K$ and the process is repeated on the next segments until the full length of the wires $l_w$ is reached. On the way, all values for $t$ and $K$ are stored and fed into an interpolator.
	\item The angular progression of the wires of the cylindric stent $\theta_{cyl}$ are computed with \autoref{eqn:theta_cyl}, with half of the wires evolving in positive angular direction and the others in negative. The angle offset $\theta_0$ is set to $i2\pi/N_w$ for each wire $i$.
	\item The cylindric wires $\theta_{cyl}$ are mapped onto the curved space with the transfer function\,\ref{eqn:rad_displ4} for each $\theta_{cyl}$. It contracts and expands the angular space over the stent surface to map a regular winding to the one intended by \autoref{eqn:winding2}. It can be solved numerically using Newton's method.
	\begin{equation}
		f(\theta, \theta_{cyl}) = \theta_{cyl}\frac{r}{r_e} -  \int_{0}^{\theta} \sqrt{1 -{\frac{ {\left(1 - \frac{\vec{R}}{R^2}\cdot \vec{r}\right)}^2}{K^2}}}\,d\theta
		\label{eqn:rad_displ4}
	\end{equation}
	Note, that \autoref{eqn:rad_displ4} only provides solutions for angles between $\theta_{cyl} \in [0, 2\pi]$. Thus, angles need to be adjusted to the correct range. The newly found angles for each section of the stent indicate the angular progression of the wires in the curved space, yielding an extension in the inner side of the curve and a contraction on the outer side.
	\item Lastly, the wire positions are assembled from all the obtained information:
	      \begin{equation*}
		      \vec{x}(t, \theta) = \vec{\gamma}(t) + r(t, \theta) \left[{\vec{e}_1(t) \cos{\theta} + \vec{e}_2(t) \sin{\theta}}\right]
	      \end{equation*}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Meshing and Postprocessing}
From the previous sections, it has been shown how to generate the core of the 1D wire elements. For visualization processes, it is interesting to generate more illustrative models for visual inspection. The first and most simple is the stent envelope which already has been shown in \autoref{fig:vessel_wall}. The surface representation is a helpful tool to debug code, evaluate the influence of the smoothing and also visualize relevant quantities, such as the porosity. For maximal compatibility, the surface mesh in 3D is generated with structured triangle elements. For users that are not highly versed in the complexity of the \href{https://vtk.org/}{\textit{vtk}} library, we recommend \href{https://github.com/nschloe/meshio}{\textit{meshio}}, an intuitive python package to help generate meshes from simple arrays.

The stent wires themselves can be outputted as simple 1D lines or as volume elements. The prior is efficient for later processing, since it only requires minimal processing and storage space. Using \href{https://vtk.org/}{\textit{vtk}}, the wires can be exported as \textit{vtp} files and visualized on \href{https://www.paraview.org/}{\textit{Paraview}}. For this purpose, the same line segments used on the 1D elements, are extended by prisms along the wire radius direction. These prisms can then be split into tetrahedra and their inner faces can be removed to obtain a pure surface representation of the wires. \subsection{Embedding}
To immerse the thin wires into a computational mesh of tetrahedral elements, several strategies have been proposed by the CFD community. Independently of the approach, a minimum level of refinement in the stent proximity is always needed, as the radius of the wires is typically ten times smaller than commonly adopted mesh sizes in brain hemodynamics simulations\,\cite{Jeken-Rico2023, Cebral2011, Berg2016}. One family of approaches utilizes immersed boundary methods, which involve applying numerical forcing or penalization terms onto the Navier-Stokes equations to mimic the effect of obstacles in the flow (see Mittal \textit{et\,al.}\,\cite{Mittal2005} and references therein). Recent extensions of these methods, such as the Shifted Boundary Method\,\cite{Main2018}, yield good results but require users to implement complex Finite Element routines. Other approaches push isotropic mesh refinement further at the stent and subsequently remove the cells and node inside the device\,\cite{Cebral2005}. While more straightforwardly applicable to finite volumes and less prone to user mistakes, this method does not generally provide smooth representations of the wires, thereby compromising numerical fidelity.

Exact representations of the stent surface could potentially be obtained by computing the intersection of the patient vasculature with the 3D stent geometry. However, this would require merging wire intersections, generating a large quantity of ill-posed elements that are cumbersome to handle. Secondly, computing the intersection of the resulting structure with the volume mesh has proven unviable in several test cases due to uncontrolled growth of memory requirements and difficulties with existing diminutive edges. One consistent challenge observed in all publications is combining a body-fitted mesh of the stent with boundary layers\,\cite{Larrabide2012, Larrabide2016}. These layers are crucial for accurately computing flow indicators, such as wall shear stress, which are essential for understanding the remodelling of aneurysmal tissue and are of significant interest in various hemodynamics simulations\,\cite{Meng2014}. However, due to the proximity of the device to the vessel wall, it is generally not possible to extrude structured boundary elements, presenting a significant area for improvement.

		\caption[Meshing of embedded device]{From top left to bottom right: Stages of mesh refinement used in the embedding procedure illustrated on a simple 2D mesh with an arc-section being removed and refined.}\label{fig:meshing_overview}
	\end{minipage}

To address these challenges, we introduce a hybrid approach that includes (i) an initial refinement of the immersed stent, (ii) a subsequent splitting of the mesh based on a computed distance field, and (iii) anisotropic refinement to generate boundary layers on the conformal mesh. This strategy has been chosen to avoid dependencies on the stent meshing resolution and to seamlessly merge both wire families without the previously discussed issues. The wires, represented by simple 1D elements, are immersed into the vascular domain and a minimal distance is computed for each. Then, all the distance functions are merged through point-wise minimum operations generating a joint levelset for the whole flow diverter $\phi_{FD}$. To refine the interface defined by the isosurface $\phi_{FD}(\vec{x})=d_w/2$, a diagonal metric tensor is computed, whose purpose is prescribing a small mesh size $h_{FD,\min}$ within a distance $l_{FD}$ of the flow-diverter stent (see \autoref{eqn:FD_refinement}). The tensor field is then passed to a remeshing library to optimize the existing topology, as shown on a simplified 2D case in the upper quadrants of \autoref{fig:meshing_overview}. It is recommended to redo these last steps 2--3 times depending on the initial mesh to ensure a sufficient resolution.
\begin{equation}
	M_{iso,FD} = I {\left(h_{FD,\min} 1_{(\abs{\phi_{FD}} \leq l_{FD})} + h_{iso}1_{(\abs{\phi_{FD}} > l_{FD})}\right)}^{-2}
	\label{eqn:FD_refinement}
\end{equation}
Once a satisfactory refinement of the levelset has been reached, new nodes are added and connected along the edges that cut the FD-isosurface\,\cite{dapogny2014}. The mesh is then split at the newly generated interface (see quadrant 3 in \autoref{fig:meshing_overview}). The now body-fitted mesh is altered in the last step to generate unstructured boundary layers that can cope with the complex hollow wires. For this purpose, a more complex tensor metric is computed using grading mesh sizes $h_{BL}$ in the normal direction to the walls and a fixed one $h_{iso}$ in the tangential plane\,\cite{Billon2017, Coupez2013}.
\begin{equation*}
	h_{BL} = \min{\{h_{BL,\min} + (\alpha_{BL} - 1)\phi_{wall}, \; h_{iso}\}}
\end{equation*}
The boundary layer size increases from $h_{BL,\min}$ with a growing distance to the wall $\phi_{wall}$ until reaching the set isotropic element size $h_{iso}$. The orientation of the elements in the near wall region is dictated by the normal vector field $\vec{n}$ which can be retrieved in the entire volume with the gradient of the wall distance function $\phi_{wall}$. Optionally, one may define an isotropic refinement field $h_{FD}$ to grade the mesh size in a user-defined way from the stent towards the interior of the mesh. Otherwise, $h_{FD}$ can be taken as $h_{iso}$. All of the discussed meshing steps can be performed by the open-source \href{https://www.mmgtools.org/}{\textit{mmg}} platform.
\begin{equation*}
	M = \frac{\vec{n}\otimes\vec{n}^T}{\min{\{h_{BL}^2,\,h_{FD}^2\}}} + \frac{I-\vec{n}\otimes\vec{n}^T}{h_{FD}^2}, \quad \text{with} \quad \vec{n} = \frac{\nabla \phi_{wall}}{\norm{\nabla \phi_{wall}}}
\end{equation*}

\subsection{Immersion Test}
The latter case has been further processed to demonstrate the proposed 3D meshing pipeline. For that the lumen was cut and extruded following the steps of a previous work\,\cite{Jeken-Rico2023}. The generated stent was then limited to the area of interest, \textit{i.\,e.} the portion ranging from the proximal end of the aneurysm neck to the distal end of the covered anterior cerebral artery. The meshing pipeline was then launched with the meshing parameters $h_{iso}$\,=\,0.3\,mm, $h_{FD, \min}$\,=\,0.015\,mm, $l_{FD}$\,=\,0.25\,mm and $h_{BL}$\,=\,0.02\,mm. The resulting mesh, containing approximately 13\,M elements, can be seen in \autoref{fig:circle_and_zoom}. For a further test, the mesh has been used to simulate the fluid mechanics with generalized pulsatile inflow conditions and zero-pressure outlets as described in\,\cite{Jeken-Rico2023} using an in-house finite element stabilized solver\,\cite{Hachem2010}. The resulting velocity profiles at peak systole are shown on the right half of \autoref{fig:circle_and_zoom}. With a mean porosity of 71.4\% at the aneurysm neck, we achieved a mean spatiotemporal bulge velocity reduction\,\cite{Larrabide2016} of 59.5\%, which is in line with the literature. Indeed, reductions of 50--80\% have been reported for simulated flow-diverters with similar (70--80\%) porosities\,\cite{Suzuki999}. This has also been confirmed in an in-silico study involving a large panel of 82 anatomies\,\cite{Sarrami-Foroushani2021}, where devices of porosity 73.2$\pm$3.8\% have been shown to typically result in mean velocity reductions of 40--60\%. Several other publications from diverse groups report similar flow reduction scores but do not disclose the porosity of their devices\,\cite{Janiga2015, Sindeev2018}.

\section{Discussion}
Flow-diverter stents provide a useful tool for clinicians to treat otherwise untreatable fusiform or wide-necked intracranial aneurysms, saving countless lives. Sadly, in some cases, the devices do not trigger the desired effects that contribute to the healing process,\cite{Maragkos2020, Dandapat2021}. To better understand the root causes of the failed cases, it is important to find statistically relevant predictors for a positive outcome so that manufacturers and neuroradiologists can improve the design and adjust the deployment accordingly. Large-scale computational studies,\cite{Sarrami-Foroushani2021} have been carried out to assess the capabilities of computational fluid dynamics to evaluate the performance of flow diverter stents, laying the groundwork for in-silico trials. These efforts need to be extended towards stented IAs in critical locations, such as the anterior communicating artery or the posterior circulation, where rupture rates are notoriously higher\,\cite{Brown2014}. In these cases, simulations rely on a robust deployment method that can cope with high tortuosities and incorporate the effects of bending on the stent porosity. With these constraints in mind, we developed a fast deployment tool and analyzed it with a strong emphasis on validation.

The comparisons with literature, though limited to simple and partly unconstrained deployments, provide a useful initial verification tool. In both the toroidal sections and the step-wise growing cylinder, the virtual deployment accurately matched the MCR and angular measurements. The latter experiment highlighted the model's limitations, particularly when a stent is significantly undersized relative to the parent vessel. Under these conditions, the MCR values are overestimated due to the assumption of constant wire distances and the limits of porosity computation. However, undersizing is uncommon in clinical practice, as it leads to poor wall apposition and a higher risk of ischemic events due to occluded side branches, as demonstrated in animal studies by Berg \textit{et\,al.}\,\cite{Berg2016}. In the cylindrical deployment, the device's adaptation to its surroundings was effectively illustrated through a smooth representation of its envelope. While the FFD overlook some established numerical relationships, the resulting stent profile aligns well with image data\,\cite{Shapiro2014a} and avoids unphysiological folds at the steps. This is a minor change for deployment purposes if only sizing and porosity estimations are considered. However, it can have a significant impact on hemodynamics simulations, where poor wall apposition can distort remodelling indicators, disrupt downstream flow profiles, and affect endothelization estimates\,\cite{Janiga2013, Ravindran2019}.

The more realistic resin model, stented and analyzed using CT images, combines a gradually decreasing vessel diameter with both bent and straight segments. This provides a constructive continuation of the previous cases. The radius and angular measurements of the repeated deployments yielded errors well within the margins reported in the literature\,\cite{Zavodszky2020, Janiga2013, Larrabide2012, Bouillot2016}. Unlike some other studies\,\cite{Janiga2013, Bouillot2016, Larrabide2012}, we did not use micro-CT despite the mismatch between wire size and imaging resolutions. This choice was motivated by the work of Janiga \textit{et\,al.}\,\cite{Janiga2013}, who found no significant deviations in the guide wires' positions between high-fidelity micro-CT and regular CT images. Applying the same procedure to a patient case allowed us to draw conclusions on the deployment in a realistic scenario, considering the mechanical resistances of pulsating blood and the friction exerted by human arteries. Both the radius and the angles of the deployed device matched well with predictions, although angles were underestimated at the inner side of the curve at the neck. These errors partly stem from locally poor segmentation and the push-pull technique used by the clinician during deployment. This technique poses a challenge to all virtual deployment algorithms, even when mechanics are considered, as it is difficult for a clinician to exert or record the exact force during an operation. Therefore, any deviation from a regular deployment requires fitting in the virtual model\,\cite{Janiga2013}. Complex mechanical models, such as those employed by Ma \textit{et\,al.}\,\cite{Ma2012}, can achieve nearly perfect deployment results when all parameters are known and quantified. However, this is rarely the case, even in controlled in-vitro experiments\,\cite{Zavodszky2020, Larrabide2012}, where deployment conditions are better tracked than in clinical interventions. For this reason and their robustness, geometric deployment tools are commonly used in the literature\,\cite{Janiga2013, Berg2017, Sindeev2018}.

The patient case was used to demonstrate the embedding technique and outline some simulation results. We obtained a flow reduction in the bulge consistent with the literature\,\cite{Suzuki999, Sindeev2018}. Moreover, we recall that a hematocrit-dependent shear-thinning viscosity model was used\,\cite{Suzuki2021}. Indeed, blood rheology is frequently assumed to be Newtonian in large calibre vessels, such as the aorta, despite recent evidence against it\,\cite{Lynch2022}. In the case of stented intracranial aneurysms, shear rates are substantially lower than in arteries due to the dampening effect of the metal grid. Consequently, erythrocytes agglomerate more, raising the apparent viscosity in the continuum. This is an important concern to address before concluding the effects of flow diversion on IA or any other flow-altering device. Albeit not considered in this simulation, compliant wall modelling has been shown to interact strongly with an embedded FD\@. In a concurrent study applying the proposed method\,\cite{Goetz2024}, the wall movement was found to counteract the flow reduction of the FD, potentially hindering or delaying thrombosis. Similar analyses could be conducted on other devices since the developed embedding workflow is not limited to flow diverters but can handle various prostheses, including coils and WEB devices.

Lastly, we would like to propose our method as a complementary tool in simulations of flow diverters as porous media\,\cite{Augsburger2011, Abdehkakha2021}, as it provides a continuous, geometry-informed estimation of local porosity. Instead of relying on a constant estimation, this approach can refine existing methods by merging the benefits of fast virtual stenting with optimized porous media computations. This integration could enhance the accuracy and efficiency of simulations, making it a valuable addition to the toolkit for researchers and clinicians.

\subsubsection*{Limitations}
Although mostly included in the discussion, we highlight here the main limitations to this study. The simulations are based on common choices from literature and lack patient data for imposing boundary conditions. Ultimately, this prevents us from concluding which of the models is the most physiologically accurate. The resolution of the MRA images had some local defects (unclear structure where the flow was in-plane) that sometimes could not be corrected with DSA images, since they were on the contralateral ICA or the BA\@. For that reason the segmentation quality of those vessels is less trustworthy. Futhermore, we could not carry out a study of Murray's law on the segmentation quality, although it might play an important role. This should certainly be studied, since tapering of the vessels is readily present in the brain and it can alter the imposed BCs.\\
