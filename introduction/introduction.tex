\chapter{Introduction}
\minitoc\newpage\label{introduction}

In this initial chapter, we introduce intracranial aneurysms from a biomedical perspective. After providing a general overview of the disease, its epidemiology, and treatment options, we focus on the study of blood flow in and around aneurysms. We briefly discuss methods for obtaining flow profiles, with a particular emphasis on Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD), and provide examples of CFD applications in the biomedical field. Lastly, we address the shortcomings of current methodologies and present the motivation for this work.

\section{Intracranial Aneurysms}\label{intro:sec1}

\subsection{Pathology}
Intracranial aneurysms (IAs) are abnormal dilations of the cerebral arteries found in an estimated 3.2\%\,\cite{Tawk2021, Vlak2011} to 4\%\,\cite{Rinkel1998} of the adult population. They are often asymptomatic and discovered incidentally during imaging studies conducted for other reasons. When IAs are symptomatic, patients may experience headaches, visual disturbances, and cranial nerve palsies caused by the compression of surrounding brain tissue. The rupture of an IA, which can occur independently of its size and symptoms, leads to a subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), a severe condition with a 50\% mortality rate\,\cite{Rinkel1998}.

During a SAH, blood spills into the subarachnoid space—the area between the arachnoid membrane and the pia mater surrounding the brain. This presence of blood sets off a chain of events, commonly leading to insufficient perfusion\footnote{Perfusion: The passage of blood through vessels or other natural channels in an organ or tissue.} of the brain. This deficit in blood flow causes neurological damage, with less than 25\% of patients surviving unharmed\,\cite{Molyneux2005}, highlighting the severity of the condition. Fortunately, IA rupture and subsequent SAH are rare events. In fact, 50--80\% of IAs are estimated to never rupture\,\cite{Tawk2021}.

\subsection{Epidemiology}
The root causes of intracranial aneurysms are still not thoroughly understood, although many studies consider alterations in local blood flow, genetics, and other environmental factors to be responsible. One initial step towards understanding the root cause of the disease has been through epidemiology, \ie\,tracing who has IA and under which circumstances. When discussing Prevalence Ratios (PR), studies often fix an overall PR and then state how a characteristic, \eg,gender or age, modifies it multiplicatively. The base value, as previously mentioned, is around 3.2\% for a 50-year-old person\,\cite{Vlak2011}. Thus, PR,=,2 would indicate a twofold likelihood relative to the base PR.

One of the first apparent observations is that IA is not equally present across different ages and genders, with women being approximately 1.5 to 3 times more likely to have IA than men. Gender not only influences the presence of the disease but also the location of the IA, although the clear causes have not yet been established\,\cite{Rinkel1998, Brown2014}. IA incidence is relatively provenance-independent, \ie\,unruptured IA are roughly equally present in different countries. However, rupture rates vary, as seen, for instance, in Finland and Japan, where SAH is 2 to 3 times more likely\,\cite{Etminan2019}. Genetics also seem to play a role in the PR. The presence of an IA in a direct family member raises prevalence to 9.5\%, approximately three times the base occurrence rate. Other inherited diseases, such as Autosomal Dominant Polycystic Kidney Disease, are known to raise the risks (PR\,=\,6.9)\,\cite{Vlak2011}.

As with many other pathologies, smoking (PR  ) and other harmful habits increase the risk of IA and SAH\,\cite{Ji2016, Thompson2015}. Atherosclerosis\footnote{Atherosclerosis: Disease of the arteries characterized by the deposition of fatty material on their inner walls.} and hypertension, often accompanying these habits, generally alter human hemodynamics and arterial health. Atherosclerosis causes plaque formation in healthy vessels, compromising blood flow, and similar depositions have been found in IA, creating a link between both diseases. Hypertension, as the reader may know, implies increased blood pressure in the cardiovascular system, surpassing 140 mmHg at systole and 90 mmHg at diastole. The increased pressure exerted by the heart raises peak flow velocities and pulsatility of arterial flow.

\subsection{Pathogenesis}
\subsubsection*{Healthy arteries}
A healthy brain artery consists of multiple layers, each with specific structural and functional roles. The innermost layer, known as the endothelium, is a single layer of Endothelial Cells (ECs) responsible for biochemical signaling, metabolic exchange, and other regulatory processes\,\cite{Liebeskind2016,Dessalles2021}. This layer perceives tensile stresses and pressure fluctuations, initiating remodeling cascades to determine the development of subsequent layers. Following the endothelium is the basement membrane (BM) and the Internal Elastic Lamina (IEL), a fenestrated layer of elastin fibers. The IEL is crucial for maintaining the elasticity and structural integrity of the vessel wall, especially at arterial bifurcations. In healthy tissue, this layer is well-preserved, ensuring proper function and resilience against stress\,\cite{Etminan2019,Liebeskind2016}. The tunica media, the muscular layer of the artery, is primarily composed of Smooth Muscle Cells (SMCs). These cells regulate blood flow distribution through their ability to contract and relax, a function particularly important in the brain, where arterioles lack the regulatory components found in other body regions. This layer provides the necessary force to manage cerebral perfusion effectively and is particularly well developed in major brain arteries compared to other parts of the body\,\cite{Liebeskind2016}. The outermost layer, the tunica adventitia, consists of collagenous and elastic fibers. These are produced and decomposed  by fibroblasts (FBs), who reside in this last layer. In brain arteries, the adventitia is sparser and contains a lower proportion of elastic fibers compared to extracranial arteries. Instead of connective tissue, brain arteries are mostly embedded in  cerebrospinal fluid, a clear, colorless body liquid found within the tissue that surrounds the brain and spinal cord.

\subsubsection*{Initiation}
IA initiation is thought to be caused by abherrant hemodynamics acting on arteries that are weakened, due to environmental or genetic factors. The brain arteries, as pointed out before, are embedded in a less rigid environment than regular vessels and contain pronounced bifurcations. At these locations, the the jet from the parent vessel impacts on a small area, inducing elevated levels of shear stress onto the IEL and the endothelium. By shearing, we understand formally the Wall Shear Stress (WSS), an expression of the friction perceived by an object in contact with a flowing viscous fluid. In cases of underdeveloped side branches and incomplete arterial configurations the hemodynamics differ from the standarized ones due to a smaller collateral flow. In these cases, it has been shown that IA formation is more likely to occur\,\cite{Hindenes2023}, agreeing with the flow induced IA initiation theory. In \autoref{fig:etminan1}, an example of an underdeveloped A1 segment of an anterior cerebral artery is taken which is commonly found when an aneurysm is situated on the so-called anterior communicating artery\,\cite{Etminan2016}. Note that a detailed anatomical decription of the brain arteries will be developed in Chapter 3. When the IEL and the endothelium are damaged, as shown in (ii) of \autoref{fig:etminan1}, the artery loses some of its strength, causing an bulge to form. The bulge itself commences to experience altered hemodynamics, since the impingement angles of the flow and the targetted area change due to the tissue deformation process. The result can be seen for instance in the cycle shown in \autoref{fig:etminan1}, where an aneurysm is shown in different stages of its development, accompanied by a scheme of the wall constitution.

\subsubsection*{Growth}
After the aneurysm initiation, there are three possible scenarios occurring. In the first and most favourable one, the growth stops and homeostasis\footnote{Homeostasis: State of equilibrium in a physiological process.} is established. IAs in which homeostasis has been confirmed have a risk of rupture 30 times smaller than when growing\,\cite{Brinjikji2016, Tawk2021}. Medical follow-ups are nevertheless carried out to ensure that the formation is stable. This precaution is important due to the particular growth behaviour of IA. As described by Etminan and Rinkel\,\cite{Etminan2016}, IA rarely go through a continuous change. Instead, growth takes place during short periods separated by long intervals of apparent stasis (see \autoref{fig:etminan2} i). The reasons for this rather unintiutive behaviour have so far been mostly addressed through hemodynamics studies. There, researches stumbled upon seemlingly contradicting theories. Studies on one side have highlighted high WSS to be related to the rupture of IA, particularly in the anterior communication\,\cite{Castro2009}. Others\,\cite{Xiang2011} found low and oscillatory shearing in growing or ruptured formations.

Given these clashing principles, Meng \etal\,\cite{Meng2014} formulated a well known hypothesis in the field joining both observed indicators. Once an initial bulge is found, one of two phenomena is possible. In the first case, the shearing remains high despite the tissue deformation. The smooth muscle cells start generating inflammatory agents that trigger the remodeling of the arterial wall. In healthy tissue, this remodeling adjusts the structure of the vessel to cope with increased stress. In diseased tissue, per-contrary, it results in a sped-up cell apoptosis\footnote{Apoptosis: The process of programmed cell death.} without the desired reinforcement, leading to a hypocellular aneurysm. The thin, scarsly populated walls of such an aneurysm type can be seen on \autoref{fig:meng} marked with an A.

The second option is that the overall WSS levels drop in the bulge due to the balooning of the IA. In this case, where oscillatory components of the shearing become more noticeable, a similar chain of events as in atherosclerosis begins. The oscillatory flow, formally quantified through the Oscillatory Shear Index (OSI), disrupts the junctions between endothelial cells and elicits a pro-inflammatory behaviour\,\cite{Dessalles2021}. The first consequence of this phenomenon is increased permeability, allowing WBC to traverse the endothelium and penetrate the artery. WBCs then spark an inflammation of the tissue, much like in a lesion, which degrades the extracellular matrix and promotes the accumulation of plaque and lipoproteins\,\cite{Meng2014}. The IA walls become thicker and attain a white appearance as in the case of aneurysm D in \autoref{fig:meng}. Both remodeling pathways can coexist, with some regions of an IA being subject to different external stresses. A possible outcome of a combined remodelling could be reflected on aneurysms like B and C on \autoref{fig:meng}, showing an inhomogeneous wall constitution.

These mechanisms may also lead to different growth dynamics. In the most worrying case, the IA develops daughter sacs or blebs. These compromise even more so the structural integrity of the IA, pushing clinicians in most cases towards an intervention\,\cite{Meng2014}.

\subsection{Treatment}
Neuroradiologists wage benefits and risks of treatment against the threat of an aneurysm rupture. Indicators for a probable rupture are as mentioned growth and the presence of daughter sacs. The size ponders also into the decision, since large (10--20\,mm) and giant aneurysms ($\geq$ 20\,mm) are more likely to be symptomatic and thus need to be treated. In general however, the correlation between size and rupture risk remains controversial. Until recently, the rupture risk compared to medium (7--10\,mm) and small IA ($\leq$ 7\,mm) was assumed to be smaller\,\cite{Bijlenga2017}. Studies analyzing ruptured IAs concluded that if size was mostly used for assessment, 78\% of ruptured cases would not have been classified as at risk\,\cite{Pagiola2020}. It is also important to consider that most IAs belong to the ``small'' category and that conditional dependence is crucial when defining risk metrics. The age of the patient also plays a decisive role. While the yearly rupture risk is higher for older patients ($\geq$ 70 years)\,\cite{Bijlenga2017}, the cumulative risk for younger individuals is larger due to their longer life expectancy.

The list of considerations that clinicians must take into account when deciding on a treatment is long and ultimately depends on the patients will. The motivation behind these careful considerations is that any intervention entails a non-negligible risk for the patient, and furthermore, not all treatments meet their intended purpose. A recent work\,\cite{Bijlenga2017}, for instance, states that a conservative approach is justified in patients for whom the risk of treatment complications is higher than the 5-year rupture risk. We have seen that rupture risks are diverse, but so are the ones related to the possible operations. A short overview of the history of IA treatment will help us understand the current state of the art, potentials and shortcomings of the available methods.

Since the 18th century, doctors have attempted to treat IA with various methods, primarily by cutting the flow in the parent artery and inducing thrombosis of the aneurysm. Unsurprisingly, these initial operations often resulted in the immediate death of the patient. This was not necessarily due to the chosen method but rather the unhygienic conditions under which these early interventions were carried out. Nowadays, the embolization\footnote{Embolization: Blockage of a vessel by natural or surgical means, such as coils.} of a parent vessel can be considered in cases where other treatments are not applicable, provided sufficient peripheral flow reaches the distal sections past the aneurysms.

In 1937, Walter Edward Dandy performed the first surgical clipping operation. In this procedure, after opening the patient's skull, the aneurysm neck is ligated with a metallic clip, isolating the bulge from blood flow and preventing rupture. At the time, this method revolutionized IA treatment and remains one of the most common treatments today in some parts of the world. However, its disadvantages are considerable. First, open brain surgery is risky due to the delicate environment and the risk of infections. Secondly, the use of a clip is only feasible when the aneurysm neck is sufficiently narrow to maintain the structure, substantially reducing the range of applicability. Other secondary motivations, such as the increased costs of patient care during the longer recovery time, make this approach less attractive in Western countries.

The introduction of endovascular techniques in the 1990s led to a shift in the treatment paradigm. Unlike surgical approaches that require a craniotomy\footnote{Craniotomy: The surgical removal of part of the bone from the skull to expose the brain.}, treatments performed through the catheterized parent vessel bear a considerably smaller risk of complications\,\cite{Molyneux2005}. More precisely, during endovascular treatment, the patient is put under general anesthesia, and a catheter\footnote{Catheter: A flexible tube inserted through a narrow opening into a body cavity.} is inserted into the arterial system, commonly through the femoral artery. Apart from being a large, accessible vessel, the femoral artery is situated far from the brain, reducing the risk of critical infections and shortening recovery times. The catheter is then navigated upstream through the aorta into either the carotid or vertebral arteries until reaching the aneurysm. In the first proposed endovascular treatment, coiling, detachable platinum wires are inserted into the aneurysm sac, slowing down the intrasaccular flow to a point where a stable thrombus can form. The thrombus then seals the aneurysm from the parent vessel, preventing rupture. This method, when applicable, is considered the gold standard in IA treatment, even after rupture\,\cite{Pineda2024}. Unfortunately, its applicability is not universally guaranteed. Coils need to be safely introduced into the aneurysmal sac without the risk of detachment, as a loose wire in the bloodstream would have fatal consequences. Therefore, the aneurysm neck must be sufficiently narrow to maintain the structure, excluding wide-necked and fusiform aneurysms from treatment. The latter IA phenotype, with a 10\% prevalence, is substantially less frequent than its saccular counterpart and less well-studied\,\cite{Keedy2006}. To extend intrasaccular treatment, new devices have been proposed, such as the Woven EndoBridge (WEB). This device is built with a set of interwoven metallic wires that are fixed at both ends, forming a foldable cylindrical device. It is deployed, similarly to coils, through a transluminal catheter into the aneurysm sac. The design of this device makes it more suited for aneurysms situated at vessel bifurcations. Both of the intrasaccular techniques have a potential risk of aneurysm recurrence and neck remnants, necessitating a second intervention in such cases.

However, many IAs remained outside the treatability range of coils and needed to be managed by other means. One supporting tool that aims to facilitate deployment is braided stents. These tube-like devices are inserted into the parent vessel, covering the neck of the IA. Then, the coils are deployed through the stent into the vessel, where, thanks to the supporting scaffold, they find a better hold. While extending the range of applicability, this method still is not enough to handle all untreatable cases\,\cite{Shin2020}. In some instances, clinicians deployed stents alone, fearing perforation of extremely weakened aneurysm tissue with the coils. They observed that a minor redirection of flow sometimes sufficed to improve the patient's condition. This observation led to the development of flow diverter stents, closely related to intracranial stents, whose main distinguishing feature is a high wire density with reduced mechanical strength\,\cite{Dandapat2021}. Since their approval in the early 2010s, Flow Diverter Stents (FDS) have provided clinicians with a dedicated tool for treating complex, fusiform IAs. When used, the clinician carefully measures the parent vessel and selects the proximal\footnote{Proximal: In the medical context, the side closest to the heart. In that sense, the opposite to distal.} and distal landing zones. According to the measurements, an FDS is selected and deployed by advancing a catheter past the aneurysm and unsheathing the device while pulling the catheter back. The FDS is considerably softer than a stent and expands automatically when pushed out of its envelope. Once in place, the wires redirect flow away from the bulge, allowing a stable thrombus to form inside the cavity. After a successful intervention, the thrombus is slowly converted into collagen, leading to the reabsorption of the IA. Like all previously mentioned methods, this one also has downsides. Due to the presence of metallic wires in the bloodstream, patients experience an increased risk of blood clot formation inside the artery. These clots can grow, travel downstream, and cause a stroke if not prevented. For this reason, patients carrying stents and FDS need to follow dual antiplatelet medication for the rest of their lives\,\cite{Dmytriw2019, Maragkos2020, Dandapat2021}. Additionally, these devices can lead to complications when deployed across bifurcating vessels, as they can either clog them or cause a thrombus to form inside them. This clash between potential benefits and risks has led to heterogeneous use of FDS in the medical community, reflected in widely scattered success rates across different centers and practitioners\,\cite{Maragkos2020}. In the latter review study, success rates ranged from 49\% to 100\%, raising numerous questions about how and under which circumstances these devices are used. It is also clear that the function of FDS is not fully understood by the community, a fact that is surely related to the complexity of aneurysms and their formation, growth, and rupture mechanisms.

\section{Hemodynamics of IA}
Numerous questions risen from the IA growth and treatment call out for a deeper analysis beyond statistical outcomes after treatment. As we have seen, the disease and the treatment options depend largely on the flow dynamics of blood, also called hemodynamics\footnote{Hemodynamics: The study of blood flow and the forces involved in circulating blood through the cardiovascular system.}. To obtain an image of the arterial hemodynamics of an IA there are different families of methods available, \textit{in-vivo} measurements, \textit{in-vitro} experiments and lastly \textit{in-silico} simulations.

\subsection{Why simulations?}
Patient data is overall the most valuable form of information, since it stems directly from the patients and thus carries the smallest amount of bias. Nevertheless, it comes at costs and is not generally available. First, some measurements, such as Computed Tomography (CT), expose patients to radiation  that however harmless it seems, needs to be waged against their possible benefits of the targetted study. This is mostly the case when considering health subjects for reference populations, since they have no medical reason to undergo those checks. Another difficulty is the applicability of certain methods, \eg\, Doppler-Ultrasound. This latter technique is non-invasive and can measure the flow velocities, but is limited to areas that are not covered by bones, a problematic limitation when considering the brain. Experimental and computational methods come into play at this stage, by attempting to bridge and combine the scarse recordings to obtain the full picture. \textit{in-vitro} setups usually use realistic artificial models in which blood-like substances, such as glycerol, are pumped through in regular pulses. In methods like Particle Image Velocimetry (PIV), the fluid is seeded by small fluorescent particles that can be tracked to obtain a 3D map of the flow velocity. PIV has been long used to study the flow in diverse environments and to validate and calibrate simulation models. Compared to simulations, experiments manage to avoid certain modelling steps, such as solving expensive numerical problems, and provide valuable insights in scenarios where the physics of the problem are not well understood\cite{Sindeev2018}. There are however two main reasons, why \textit{in-vitro} experiments are not the go-to method for IA studies. First, experimental setups are time consuming for researchers to carry out, as they require a lot of preparation, post-processing and repetitions, to ensure the results are reliable. Secondly, the results are limited to the setup and the used materials, which can be a problem when trying to generalize the findings. In the case of IA, for instance, blood cannot be used due to its low opacity and thrombogenic behaviour outside a living organism. Similarly to simulations, experiments also relie on knowledge about boundary conditions and material properties, providing no further insight into these complex aspects.

\subsection{Computational Fluid Dynamics}
Boosted by the improving performances of computing systems, CFD has become a fundamental tool in research and development. Its industrial and scientific applications range over designing wind turbines to the simulation of magma streams in earth's crust. In industry, simulations permit testing in parallel arrays of product designs for optimization purposes, saving time and resources for prototyping. In science, CFD can recreate plenty of natural phenomena and provides a usefull tool for hypothesis testing. It can also be used to prognose the intensity of catastrophic phenomena, such as tsunamis, supporting the decision-making process. In biomedical applications, CFD are exploited to recreate the hemodynamics in narrow, confined spaces, elucidating in detail the flow patterns following imposed boundary conditions.

Broadly spearking, in CFD a fluid domain is considered as an assembly of small, discrete elements. On these, the governing equations of fluid dynamics are simplified and solved considering all neighbouring elements. Then, the solution can be reassembled from all of the small contribution, providing a macroscopic view of the flow. The dynamics of the system are usually driven by the Navier-Stokes equations, which describe the conservation of momentum and mass in a fluid, as well as energy in the case of compressibility. The users of CFD prescribe a set of boundary conditions and material properties following the known system characteristics. Naturally, each system is subject to different levels of complexity, like the presence of turbulence, compressibility, or multiphasic flow. These impact the choice of the solver and the computational resources needed to solve the problem in feasible time. Finding a set of suitable simplifications is not an easy task and requires a careful consideration. Therefore, Chapter 2 will be entirely dedicatedn to the description of the governing equations and the numerical methods used in CFD.

\subsection{CFD in Biomechanics}
So far we have shown the interest of learning more about the hemodynamics of intracranial aneurysms and designated CFD as the tool to provide us with the sought insights. This procedure has been already applied to a number of applications in the human body with highly satisfactory outcomes. Cardiovascular research commonly profits from hemodynamics analysis, as for instance in Taylor \etal(2013)\cite{Taylor2013}, where a better estimation of the pressure drop following coronary stenosis was found with CFD than with traditional invasive methods. As one of the most prominent examples, this finding has proven that \textit{in-silico} tests can avoid risks and complications for patients first-hand. Other research groups have successfully related plaque formation to altered hemodynamics across different locations in the arterial system. These findings, summarized in\,\cite{Morbiducci2016}, help understanding how in principal it could be possible to predict the progression of the diseas and help clinical decision making. Many other applications devote their time to simulating and optimizing medical devices, from heart valves\,\cite{LURAGHI201745} to dialysis machines (see\,\cite{BEHBAHANI2009} and therein).

All the accomplishments in the field come after exhaustive, often multi-centered analysis. This includes iterations of hypothesis being formulated and several checks until a validated consensus is found. Motivated by these advancements, the IA research community has been trying to find out why some aneurysms seemingly do not fall under the general rules. Whether this is a result of an incomplete knowledge of the growth and remodelling pathways or inconsistent simulation methods is a question that many scientists have considered. To shed light into this matter, several known researchers of the field have organized computational challenges, organizing teams to simulate the same aneurysm and compare the results. In the initial question posed onto the community was whether the computing frameworks (\eg, solver type, mesh resolutions and time stepping) were in sufficient accordance to not jeopardize the conclusion of the teams. To this end, Steinman\,\cite{Steinman2013} and Berg\,\cite{Berg2015} examined solutions of the same aneurysms and concluded that overall the scatter of results was not large enough to cause confounding results. However, another related study by Janiga\,\cite{Janiga2015} found that the interpretation is not necessarily unified. Although more than 80\% of teams identified the ruptured aneurysm from the two proposed in the study, all failed to identify the correct rupture site. The aneurysm was later said to be located at a zone of low Time-Averaged WSS (TAWSS) and under large OSI. A later study by Valen-Sendstad\,\cite{Valen-Sendstad2018} extended the search for dissent and considered the full pipeline of processing \ie\,including all steps from the segmentation of the medical images. Teams reported all of the employed modelling choices necessary to carry out the simulations and were categorized into low to highly experience teams following their own statements. Interestingly, the study reflected a noticeable scatter, roughly of 30\%, on most of the quantifyable results. Even among the experienced participants, the disagreement in methods and results was substantial. One of the few consentual choices, was the inclusion of the parent ICA into the computations. The study suggests in the end that for better comparisons, the WSS in the aneurysms should be normalized to the parent vessel, as the absolute values are not significant across simulations. A last set of challenges was carried out by Berg and Vo{\ss} \etal\cite{Berg2018, voss2019, Berg2019} that unified all of the previously set goals, that is, assessing the segmentation based on imaging scores, computing the scatter of the results due to the segmentation errors and lastly predicting which of the five considered IA did rupture in the end. The segmentation challenge poses particular difficulties in the domain of IA for two main reasons. First, aneurysms are highly irregular malformations and do not adhere generally to one shape. Thus, little prior knowledge is available to guide the segmentation process. Their common non-convex shape makes it difficult to apply conventient methods, such as \href{https://simvascular.github.io/}{Simvascular}. Secondly, the imaging techniques used to obtain the data are not standardized and can vary greatly in quality and resolution, leaving overall much room for interpretation. The challenge displayed notable differences in the segmentations which pondered considerably in some cases into the simulation results. Particularly, cases with excessive or very reduced smoothing deviated strongly from the expected disclosed high resolution data. Likewise, omitting vessels in the aneurysm vecinity turned out fatal in the simulation sense. The challenge concluded on the note that the segmentation is a highly complex yet decisive step and deserves more attention in the future. In the last stage of the study, one set of geometries was distributed to assess the rupture risk and choose one of the 5 geometries as the one that would rupture. The results were not as clear as expected, with only 4 out of 17 teams choosing the correct aneurysm. The successful teams were not particularly characterized by high resolution simulations or complex models. All however considered transient dynamics. This rather presents an unsettling finding, many questions remain open and the community is still far from a unified understanding of the disease.

\section{Motivation}

With the observed literature we deem it as very important to carry out carefully considered simulations. The thesis will therefore commence with a detailed description of the governing equations and the numerical methods used in CFD, including a novel stabilization parameter tensor, and 2D and 3D validation cases.

We will introduce an overview of the brain arterial fluid dynamics, with special attention on the general flow conditions, such as boundary conditions and material properties. As a result of the phd, we will analyze the effect of differing simulation domain sizes on the outcome of the simulations as a new sensitivity point to the previously cited computtational challenges.

Then a geometry-based stent deployment is derived and extensively validated against literature examples, in-vitro and in-vivo experiments.

The methods studied in this thesis will be applied to a patient case featuring a ruptured giant aneurysm. The patient, whose pre- and post-treatment images are available, sadly passed away due to the rupture, even after being treated. In the given scenario, we will attempt to understand the hemodynamics of the aneurysm and the effect of the treatment on the flow. The results will be compared to the patient's outcome and the literature to assess the validity of the simulations.
