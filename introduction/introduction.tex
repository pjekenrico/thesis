\section{Intracranial Aneurysms}\label{intro:sec1}

\noindent Intracranial Aneurysms (IAs) are abnormal bulging or ballooning in the walls of blood vessels within the brain that can be found in an estimated 2\% of adults~\cite{Rinkel1998}. These vascular anomalies can develop in any part of the brain's arterial network, most commonly found at arterial bifurcations where vessels branch out. Although some aneurysms remain asymptomatic and may go unnoticed, they hold the potential to cause serious health complications.\\

\noindent The primary concern associated with IAs is the risk of rupture. When an aneurysm ruptures, it leads to a condition known as SubArachnoid Hemorrhage (SAH), which involves bleeding into the subarachnoid space — the area between the brain and the thin tissues that cover it. The sudden appearance of blood in this region can cause an array of life-threatening conditions, such as cerebral ischemia, leading in 50\% of the cases to the patient's death~\cite{Keedy2006}. In half of the remaining cases, permanent disabilities are observed after surviving an SAH.\@ Thankfully, the incidence of IA rupture remains relatively low, with an estimated yearly occurrence of 0.7\%~\cite{Rinkel1998}.\\

\noindent IAs pose a very important research subject due to their high incidence and potential implications. While some of the initiation and growth mechanisms are known, much remains to be learned. To better understand the matter of the subject, it is necessary to characterize from a physiological point of view this disease.\\

\noindent Brain arteries, vessels at which the pathology can occur, transport 15\% of a human's nutrients and oxygen-laden blood to our most delicate organ. This substantial part of the cardiac output is guided mainly through three vessels in the brain, the left and right Internal Carotid Arteries (ICAs) and the Basilar Artery (BA). In a regular case, all these vessels join in a loop of arteries, called the Circle of Willis (CoW, see Figure). This structure provides redundancy that in the event of a blocked supplier, could allow other suppliers to reach all the regions of the brain. Only 11.9\% of adults exhibit a regular or textbook-type CoW, while most people miss at least one communicating vessel in the CoW~\cite{Hindenes2020}, underlining how patient-dependent the environment can be. 



\section{Treatment}\label{intro:sec2}
Advancements in medical imaging and an increased awareness of cerebrovascular diseases have led to more IAs being detected during routine checkups and diagnostic screenings in hospitals.  Early detection and monitoring of these aneurysms play a critical role in preventing potential ruptures and allowing timely assessment to reduce the associated risks. In common clinical practice, the decision to treat an IA is based among other factors on:

\begin{itemize}
    \setlength\itemsep{0.25cm}
    \item \textbf{Size}: Larger aneurysms ($>$7mm), especially when generating symptoms, generally have been associated with a higher risk of rupture than smaller ones. Some studies however highlight that smaller ones are similarly dangerous~\cite{Pagiola2020}.
    \item \textbf{Growth Rate}: The growth rate of the aneurysm is an essential indicator of its stability. Rapidly growing aneurysms may necessitate more immediate intervention.
    \item \textbf{Morphology}: IA that feature secondary blebs, also called daughter sacks, imply an additional risk.
    \item \textbf{Location}: Certain regions, for instance, the Anterior communicating artery (Acom), may pose a higher IA rupture risk than others~\cite{Orz2015}.
    \item \textbf{Age and Health Status}: Younger patients may accumulate a larger risk of rupture over their lives and may handle a clinical intervention better than an elderly patient.
    \item \textbf{Origin and Precedents}: A family history of IAs or a genetic predisposition of certain populations can be taken into account, as it may influence the risk of rupture~\cite{Bijlenga2017}.
\end{itemize}


\noindent These factors are partly incorporated into a scoring system that links risk points with specific criteria. When a certain threshold of points is met, it is recommended to proceed with the surgery for the patient. However, some studies have raised concerns about the limited specificity of this risk estimation method, underlining the demand for incorporating more specific risk factors~\cite{Bijlenga2017, Pagiola2020}. A further aspect not covered by the study is the counterposition with the treatment, i.e., weighing the risks and benefits of treatment for a patient. An inherent difficulty plaguing IA is their inhomogeneous shapes and locations, which make the use of some treatments impossible in some cases.\\

\noindent Overall, treatments are divided into surgical clipping and endovascular IA embolization. In the former, the surgeons access the brain and insert a metallic clip at the aneurysm neck, blocking the flow into the IA directly. Although regarded as highly effective once in place, it is rarely used on unruptured IA due to its high intra- and postoperative morbidity and mortality rates. Endovascular treatment, in comparison, is a minimally invasive technique that aims to treat the IA from the inside. Generally, interventional neuroradiologists navigate a microcatheter through the patient's arterial system, typically from a femoral artery in the groin to the aneurysm location within the cerebral vasculature. This precise navigation relies on real-time Computed Tomography (CT) images and advanced 3D mapping. Once the microcatheter reaches the aneurysm site, several treatment options are available. One common technique used is the deployment of detachable metallic coils within the aneurysm sac. These coils promote clot formation and occlude the IA, effectively sealing it off from the circulation. Since the first application in the 90s, this technique has both decreased mortality and morbidity rates with respect to prior techniques and opened up a whole range of new treatment options to physicians. Coiling however is not universally available, failing in cases where the aneurysm neck does not provide enough support for the metal wires to ensure that they stay in place. Sometimes, a stent may be deployed at the aneurysm to assist in the positioning of the coils.\\

\noindent In case of a wide-necked aneurysm or one in which the IA develops around a full segment of the artery, i.e.\.a fusiform aneurysm, flow diverter stents are usually used. In this case, a braided metallic tube is deployed at the parent vessel, covering the IA and directing the blood flow away from it. The elastic wires and the braiding of the stent allow the physician to regulate the wall adhesion and porosity of the device by pushing and pulling during the delivery. In the optimal case, the stent reduces the flow in the sac so far, that a thrombus forms in its interior, thereby sealing it. \noindent Lastly, there is a family of braided devices that have been developed in the last decades to target some of the shortcomings of the previously described techniques. Notable mentions are the Woven Endobridge (WEB), an intrasaccular device to treat wide-neck bifurcation aneurysms and the contour device, an umbrella-like structure that also targets the same kind of IA.\@
