\section{Intracranial Aneurysms}\label{intro:sec1}

\noindent Intracranial Aneurysms (IAs) are abnormal bulging or ballooning in the walls of blood vessels within the brain that can be found in an estimated 2\% of adults~\cite{Rinkel1998}. These vascular anomalies can develop in any part of the brain's arterial network, most commonly found at arterial bifurcations where vessels branch out. Although some aneurysms remain asymptomatic and may go unnoticed, they hold the potential to cause serious health complications.\\

\noindent The primary concern associated with IAs is the risk of rupture. When an aneurysm ruptures, it leads to a condition known as SubArachnoid Hemorrhage (SAH), which involves bleeding into the subarachnoid space — the area between the brain and the thin tissues that cover it. The sudden increase in pressure from the bleeding can compress and damage nearby brain tissue, leading in 50\% of the cases to the patient's death~\cite{Keedy2006}. In half of the remaining cases permanent disabilities are observed after surviving a SAH.\\

\noindent Thankfully, the incidence of IA rupture remains relatively low, with an estimated yearly occurrence of 0.7\%~\cite{Rinkel1998}. Also, advancements in medical imaging and increased awareness of cerebrovascular diseases have led to more IAs being detected during routine checkups and diagnostic screenings in hospitals. Early detection and monitoring of these aneurysms play a critical role in preventing potential ruptures and allowing timely assessment to reduce the associated risks. In common clinical practice, the decision of treating an IA is based among other factors on:

\begin{itemize}
    \setlength\itemsep{0.25cm}
    \item \textbf{Size}: Larger aneurysms ($>$7mm), especially when generating symptoms, generally have been associated with a higher risk of rupture than smaller ones. Some studies however highlight that smaller ones are similarly dangerous~\cite{Pagiola2020}.
    \item \textbf{Growth Rate}: The growth rate of the aneurysm is an essential indicator of its stability. Rapidly growing aneurysms may necessitate more immediate intervention.
    \item \textbf{Morphology}: IA that feature secondary blebs, also called daughter sacks, imply an additional risk.
    \item \textbf{Location}: Certain regions, for instance, the Anterior communicating artery (Acom), may pose a higher IA rupture risk than others~\cite{Orz2015}.
    \item \textbf{Age and Health Status}: Younger patients may accumulate a larger risk of rupture over their life and may handle a clinical intervention better than an elderly patient.
    \item \textbf{Origin and Precedents}: A family history of IAs or a genetic predisposition of certain populations can be taken into account, as it may influence the risk of rupture~\cite{Bijlenga2017}.
\end{itemize}

Based on these factors, doctors outweigh the implications of an intervention against a continued observation of the aneurysm. Naturally, all treatment options bear a risk of surgical complications and can potentially not have the desired outcome on the aneurysm. Among the three most known options are surgical clipping, coiling and stenting. The former, as the name suggests, involves a direct intervention at the brain site in which a clip is deployed to isolate the aneurysm from the blood circulation. While highly effective once in place, it is rarely used due to its prevalence of (post-) operative complications. Stenting and coiling, on the other side, are done in an endovascular procedure i.e.\ by making a section in the femoral artery and inserting a catheter. The device is then navigated through the aorta into the brain region, where then the operator can effectuate the deployment.
