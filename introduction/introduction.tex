\section{Intracranial Aneurysms}\label{intro:sec1}
Intracranial aneurysms (IA) are abnormal dilations of the cerebral arteries that can be found on an estimated 4\%\,\cite{Rinkel1998} to 3.2\%\,\cite{Tawk2021} of adult population. They are often asymptomatic and discovered incidentally during imaging studies for other reasons. In cases where the IA are symptomatic, the most common symptoms are headaches, visual disturbances, and cranial nerve palsies, caused by the compression of the surrounding brain tissue. When an IA is sufficiently large to cause these adverse effects, the risk of rupture is also increased. The rupture of an IA can lead to a subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), a severe condition that results in the death of the patient in 50\% of the cases\,\cite{Rinkel1998}. During a SAH, blood spills into the subarachnoid space, the area between the arachnoid membrane and the pia mater surrounding the brain. The presence of blood in this area unsets a chain of events, commonly leading to an insufficient perfusion\footnote{Perfusion: The passage of blood through vessels or other natural channels in an organ or tissue.} of the brain. This decific in blood flow causes neurologic damage which less than 25\% of patients survive asymptomatically\,\cite{Molyneux2005}, underlining the severity of the phenomenon.

Luckily, IA rupture and a subsequent SAH remain seldom event. In fact, 50--80\% of IA are estimated to never rupture\,\cite{Tawk2021}. When patients are diagnosed with an IA, clinicians assess the risk of rupture based on several morphological, topological and categorical factors. The morphology of the aneurysm can be a good indicator of the risk when pronounced daughter sacks are present. These secondary aneurysms have been correlated with a pronounced risk of rupture and pose an imminent threat\,\cite{Meng2014}. Similarly, when in follow-up scans the IA is shown to be growing or multiple aneurysms are found, treatment is strongly encouraged. The correlation of size with rupture risk on the other hand is a controversial factor. Until recent years, large (10--20\,mm) and giant aneurysms ($\geq$ 20\,mm) were related to an increased rupture risk compared to middle (7--10\,mm) and small ones ($\leq$ 7\,mm)\,\cite{Bijlenga2017}. Studies analyzing ruptured IA concluded that if treatment decisions were to be largely decided by IA size, 78\% of ruptured cases would not have been classified as in risk\,\cite{Pagiola2020}. At the same time, it is important to consider, that most IA are classified as small and that conditional dependance is important to consider when risk metrics are defined. The age of the patient plays also a decisive role. While the yearly rupture risk is higher for older patients ($leq$ 70 years) \,\cite{Bijlenga2017}, the cumulative risk for younger individuals is larger due to the longer life expectancy. Apart from the mentioned categories, there is a number of other considerations that might be taken into account differently by the treating clinician.

The motivation behind these careful considerations is that any intervention entails a non-negligible risk for the patient, and furthermore, not all treatments meet their intended purpose. Ever since the 18$^\textit{th}$ century, doctors have trying to treat IA with various methods, first and foremost, by cutting the flow in the parent artery and inducing a thrombosis of the aneurysm. As can be expected these first operations more than rarely led to the immediate death of the patient. This is not necessarily attributed to the chosen method, but rather the hygenic conditions under which first interventions were carried out. Nowadays, the embolization\footnote{Embolization: Blockage of vessel by natural or surgical means, such as coils.} of a parent vessel can be considered in cases where other treatments are not applicable and enough periferal flow reaches the distal sections past the aneurysms. In 1937, Walter Edward Dandy carried out the first surgical clipping operation, a procedure in which after opening the skull of the patient the aneurysm neck is ligated with a metallic clip, impeding a secluding the bulge from flow and preventing rupture. At the time, this method revolutionized the treatment of IA and is still the most common treatment today in parts of the world. The introduction of endovascular techniques in the 1990s, however, has led to a shift in the treatment paradigm. Contrary to the surgical approaches that require a craniotomy\footnote{Craniotomy: The surgical removal of part of the bone from the skull to expose the brain.}, treatments carried out through the catheterized parent vessel bear a considerable smaller risk of complications\cite{Molyneux2005}. The first endovascular treatment, coiling, consists of the deployment of platinum wires in the aneurysm sack, whose main purpose is slowing down the intrasaccular flow to a point where a stable thrombus can form. The thrombus then seals the aneurysm from the parent vessel, preventing rupture. This method, when applicable, is seen as the golden standart in IA treatment, even after the case of rupture\cite{Pineda2024}. Unfortunately, its applicability is not generally guaranteed. Coils need to be safely introduced into the aneurysmal sack without the risk of detachament, as a loose wire in the blood stream would have fatal consequences. Therefore, the aneurysm neck needs to be sufficiently narrow to maintain the structure, excluding wide-necked and fusiform aneurysms from treatment. The latter IA phenotype is with a 10\% prevalence substantially less frequent than its saccular counterpart and less well studied\cite{Keedy2006}. To extend the intrasaccular treatment, new devices have been proposed, like for instance the Woven EndoBridge (WEB). This device is build with a set of intervowen metallic wires that are fixed at both ends forming a foldable cylindric device. It is deployed, similarly to coils, through a transluminar catheter into the aneurysm sack. The design of this device makes its use more suited for aneurysms situated at vessel bifurcations.
