\chapter{Introduction}

\begin{tcolorbox}[abstract]

	Intracranial aneurysms are complex malformations of the cerebral arteries that remain poorly understood despite their high prevalence. This thesis aims to advance our understanding of these conditions by modelling them in a virtual environment. The first chapter provides a comprehensive overview of aneurysms from a biomedical perspective, summarizing key literature on their formation and growth. We discuss how fluid dynamics simulations, which offer a non-invasive method for studying blood flow associated with these formations, complement findings from autopsies and surgical examinations. Despite their promise, simulations have not yet resolved many open questions in the field. This thesis specifically addresses two critical aspects: (i) delimiting an adecuate computational domain and (ii) assessing the effects of flow diverter stents, which, despite occasional failures, have been instrumental in treating previously untreatable aneurysms.


	\tcblower

	Les anévrismes intracrâniens, bien que fréquents, restent des malformations complexes des artères cérébrales dont la compréhension est encore limitée. Cette thèse cherche à approfondir notre connaissance de ces pathologies à travers la simulation numérique. Ce premier chapitre présente une introduction des anévrismes d'un point de vue biomédical, en passant en revue les principales recherches sur leur formation et leur croissance. Nous présentons comment les simulations de dynamique des fluides, en tant que méthode non invasive pour étudier les flux sanguins, complètent les découvertes issues des autopsies et des interventions chirurgicales. Bien que ces simulations offrent des perspectives prometteuses, elles laissent encore plusieurs questions en suspens. Cette thèse se concentre sur deux points clés: (i) l'impact de la taille du domaine de simulation sur le flux sanguin dans les anévrismes et (ii) les effets des systèmes de diversion de flux, qui, malgré quelques échecs occasionnels, ont permis de traiter des anévrismes apriori considérés comme incurables.

\section{Medical Background}
\subsection{Pathology}
Intracranial aneurysms (IAs) are abnormal dilations of the cerebral arteries found in an estimated 3.2\%\,\cite{Tawk2021, Vlak2011} to 4\%\,\cite{Rinkel1998} of the adult population. They are often asymptomatic and discovered incidentally during imaging studies conducted for other reasons. When IAs are symptomatic, patients may experience headaches, visual disturbances, and cranial nerve palsies caused by the compression of surrounding brain tissue. The rupture of an IA, which can occur independently of its size and symptoms, leads to a Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (SAH), a severe condition with a 50\% mortality rate\,\cite{Rinkel1998}.

During an SAH, blood spills into the subarachnoid space—the area between the arachnoid membrane and the pia mater surrounding the brain. This presence of blood sets off a chain of events, commonly leading to insufficient perfusion\footnote{Perfusion: The passage of blood through vessels or other natural channels in an organ or tissue.} of the brain. This deficit in blood flow causes neurological damage, with less than 25\% of patients surviving unharmed\,\cite{Molyneux2005}, highlighting the severity of the condition. Fortunately, IA rupture and subsequent SAH are rare events. In fact, 50--80\% of IAs are estimated to never rupture\,\cite{Tawk2021}.

\subsection{Epidemiology}
The root causes of intracranial aneurysms are still not thoroughly understood, although many studies consider alterations in local blood flow, genetics, and other environmental factors to be responsible. One step towards understanding the root cause of the disease has been through epidemiology, \textit{i.e.}\,tracing who has IAs and under which circumstances. When discussing Prevalence Ratios (PR), studies often fix an overall PR and then state how a characteristic, \textit{e.g.}\,gender or age, modifies it multiplicatively. The base value, as previously mentioned, is around 3.2\% for a 50-year-old person\,\cite{Vlak2011}. Thus, PR\,=\,2 would indicate a two-fold likelihood, or a 6.4\% prevalence probability.

One of the first apparent observations is that IAs are not equally present across different ages and genders, with women being approximately 1.5 to 3 times more likely to have IAs than men. Gender not only influences the presence of the disease but also where IAs spawn, although clear causes have not yet been established\,\cite{Rinkel1998, Brown2014}. IA incidence is relatively provenance-independent, \textit{i.e.}\,unruptured IAs are roughly equally present in different countries. However, rupture rates vary, as seen in Finland and Japan, where SAH is 2 to 3 times more likely\,\cite{Etminan2019}. Genetics also seem to play a role in the PR\@. The presence of an IA in a direct family member raises prevalence to 9.5\%, approximately three times the base occurrence rate. Other inherited conditions, such as Autosomal Dominant Polycystic Kidney Disease, are known to raise the risks (PR\,=\,6.9)\,\cite{Vlak2011}.

As with many other pathologies, smoking increases the risk of IAs (PR\,=\,2.2--3.9) and SAH\,\cite{Etminan2016, Ji2016, Thompson2015}. Atherosclerosis\footnote{Atherosclerosis: Disease of the arteries characterized by the deposition of fatty material on their inner walls.} and hypertension, often accompanying unhealthy habits, generally alter hemodynamics\footnote{Hemodynamics: The study of blood flow and the forces involved in its circulation through the cardiovascular system.} and vascular health. Atherosclerosis causes plaque formation in vessels, compromising blood flow. Similar depositions have been found in IAs, creating a link between both diseases. Hypertension, as the reader may know, implies increased blood pressure in the cardiovascular system, surpassing 140\,mmHg at systole and 90\,mmHg at diastole. The increased pressure exerted by the heart raises peak flow velocities and pulsatility of arterial flow. Extended exposure to these aberrant conditions is thought to unsettle the balance in the regeneration process of arterial walls, increasing the chances of developing aneurysms\,\cite{Rinkel1998, Etminan2016,Etminan2019}.

\subsection{Pathogenesis}
\subsubsection*{Healthy arteries}
A healthy brain artery consists of multiple layers, each with specific structural and functional roles. The innermost layer, known as the endothelium, is a single layer of endothelial cells responsible for biochemical signalling, metabolic exchange, and other regulatory processes\,\cite{Liebeskind2016, Dessalles2021}. This layer perceives tensile stresses and pressure fluctuations, initiating remodelling cascades to determine the development of subsequent layers. Following the endothelium are the basement membrane and the Internal Elastic Lamina (IEL), a fenestrated layer of elastin fibres. The IEL is crucial for maintaining the elasticity and structural integrity of the vessel wall, especially at arterial bifurcations. In healthy tissue, this layer is well-preserved, ensuring proper function and resilience against stress\,\cite{Etminan2016, Liebeskind2016}. The tunica media, the muscular layer of the artery, is primarily composed of smooth muscle cells. These cells regulate blood flow distribution through their ability to contract and relax, a function particularly important in the brain, where capillaries lack the regulatory components found in other body regions. This layer provides the necessary force to manage cerebral perfusion effectively and is particularly well developed in major brain arteries compared to other parts of the body\,\cite{Liebeskind2016}. The outermost layer, the tunica adventitia, consists of collagenous and elastic fibres. These are produced and decomposed by cells called fibroblasts, which reside in this last layer. In brain arteries, the adventitia is sparser and contains a lower proportion of elastic fibres compared to extracranial arteries. Instead of connective tissue, brain arteries are mostly embedded in cerebrospinal fluid, a clear, colourless body liquid found within the tissue that surrounds the brain and spinal cord.

\subsubsection*{Initiation}
Initiation is thought to be caused by aberrant hemodynamics acting on weakened arteries. A common site for aneurysms is bifurcations\,\cite{Brown2014}. At these locations, the jet from the parent vessel impacts on a small area, inducing elevated levels of shear stress onto the IEL and the endothelium. By shearing, we understand formally the Wall Shear Stress (WSS), an expression of the friction perceived by an object in contact with a flowing viscous fluid. In zones of high WSS and a positive gradient, \textit{i.e.}\,a progressive growth of the WSS in the flow direction, the arterial wall is observed to lose the IEL, causing an initial dent in the vessel\,\cite{Etminan2016, Meng2014}. Extended exposure to these hemodynamic conditions can dilate the lesion further (\autoref{fig:etminan1}, b, c). During this process, tissue cells in and around the artery attempt to adapt their structure to withstand the strain caused by the flow. An example of this process can be seen in \autoref{fig:etminan1}, where one can observe an IA forming on the anterior communicating artery. This particular case has been chosen by the figure's authors\,\cite{Etminan2016} to highlight the growth of IAs conditioned by an underdeveloped side branch. The small vessel left of the aneurysm is usually larger and carries flow from the other internal carotid artery to the anterior cerebral arteries. The vessels of both brain sides are thereby connected in a circle which helps balance out the flow, called the Circle of Willis. When some of the vessels in this circle are underdeveloped, like here, flow velocities can be elevated and lead to an increased prevalence of IAs\,\cite{Hindenes2023}.

\subsubsection*{Growth}
After initiation, there are three possible scenarios occurring. In the first and most favourable one, the growth stops and homeostasis\footnote{Homeostasis: State of equilibrium in a physiological process.} establishes. IAs in homeostasis are statistically 30 times less likely to rupture than growing ones\,\cite{Brinjikji2016, Tawk2021}. Medical follow-ups are still carried out to ensure that the formation is stable. This precaution is important due to the particular growth behaviour of IAs\@. As described by Etminan and Rinkel\,\cite{Etminan2016}, IAs rarely go through a continuous change. Instead, growth takes place during short periods separated by long intervals of apparent stasis (see Box i on \autoref{fig:etminan2}). The reasons for this behaviour have, so far, been mostly addressed through hemodynamic studies\,\cite{Meng2014, Etminan2016}. There, researchers stumbled upon seemingly contradicting theories, highlighting high WSS to be related to IA rupture\,\cite{Castro2009}, while others\,\cite{Xiang2011} found low and oscillatory stress in growing or ruptured formations.

Given these clashing principles, Meng \textit{et\,al.}\,\cite{Meng2014} formulated a well-known hypothesis joining both pathways. Once an initial bulge is created through high shearing, one of two courses is possible. In the first case, the shearing remains high despite the tissue deformation. Smooth muscle cells start generating inflammatory agents that trigger the remodelling of the arterial wall. In healthy tissue, this remodelling adjusts the vessel's structure to cope with increased stress. In diseased tissue, it results in an accelerated cell apoptosis\footnote{Apoptosis: The process of programmed cell death.} without the desired reinforcement, leading to a hypocellular aneurysm. The thin, sparsely populated walls of such an aneurysm type can be seen on \autoref{fig:meng} marked with an A.

In the second scenario, the mean WSS levels drop as a consequence of the aneurysm's ballooning, as displayed in \autoref{fig:etminan2}. Frequently, the drop in WSS is accompanied by oscillatory shearing, which is common in atherosclerosis\,\cite{Meng2014}. The oscillatory flow, formally quantified through the Oscillatory Shear Index (OSI), disrupts the junctions between endothelial cells and elicits a pro-inflammatory behaviour\,\cite{Dessalles2021}. The first consequence is increased permeability, allowing white blood cells to traverse the endothelium and penetrate into the artery (see \autoref{fig:etminan2}, iii). There, they spark inflammation, much like in a lesion, degrading the extracellular matrix and promoting the accumulation of plaque and lipoproteins\,\cite{Meng2014}. As a result, the IA walls become thicker and attain a white appearance, like in aneurysm D of \autoref{fig:meng}. Both remodelling pathways can coexist, with some regions of an IA being subject to different external stresses. A possible outcome of a combined remodelling could be reflected in aneurysms like B and C in \autoref{fig:meng}\,\cite{Meng2014}, showing an inhomogeneous wall constitution. These mechanisms may lead to different growth dynamics. In the most worrying case, the IA develops daughter sacs,further compromising its structural integrity\,\cite{Meng2014}.

\subsection{Treatment}
Neuroradiologists wage benefits and risks of treatment against the threat of an aneurysm rupture. Indicators for a probable rupture are as mentioned growth and the presence of daughter sacs. The size ponders also into the decision, since large (11--24\,mm) and giant aneurysms ($\geq$ 25\,mm) are more likely to be symptomatic and thus need to be treated. In general, the correlation between size and rupture risk remains controversial. Until recently, the rupture risk compared to medium (7--10\,mm) and small IA ($<$\,7\,mm) was assumed to be smaller\,\cite{Bijlenga2017}. Studies analyzing ruptured IAs concluded that if the size was mostly used for assessment, 78\% of ruptured cases would not have been classified as at risk\,\cite{Pagiola2020}. It is also important to consider that most IAs belong to the ``small'' category and that conditional dependence is crucial when defining risk metrics. The age of the patient also plays a decisive role. While the yearly rupture risk is higher for older patients ($\geq$ 70 years)\,\cite{Bijlenga2017}, the cumulative risk for younger individuals is larger due to their longer life expectancy.

The list of considerations that clinicians must take into account when deciding on a treatment is long and ultimately depends on the patient's will. The motivation behind these careful considerations is that any intervention entails a non-negligible risk for the patient, and not all treatments meet their intended purpose. Bijlenga \textit{et\,al.}\,\cite{Bijlenga2017} state that a conservative approach is justified in patients for whom the risk of treatment complications is higher than the 5-year rupture risk. In regards to this rule, we have seen that rupture risks are inhomogeneous and depend on many (unknown) factors. The risk of treatment is likewise hard to estimate and usually depends on the employed strategy. To put the reader into context we provide a short overview of the history of IA treatment.

Since the 19th century, doctors have attempted to treat IA with various methods, primarily by cutting the flow in the parent artery and inducing thrombosis of the aneurysm. Unsurprisingly, these initial operations often resulted in the immediate death of the patient\,\cite{Smith1994}. This was not necessarily due to the chosen method but rather the unhygienic conditions under which these early interventions were carried out. Nowadays, the embolization\footnote{Embolization: Blockage of a vessel by natural or surgical means, such as coils.} of a parent vessel can be considered in cases where other treatments are not applicable, provided sufficient peripheral flow reaches the distal sections past the aneurysms.

In 1937, Walter Edward Dandy performed the first surgical clipping operation\,\cite{Laurent2022}. In this procedure, after opening the patient's skull, the aneurysm neck is ligated with a metallic clip, isolating the bulge from blood flow and preventing rupture (see \autoref{fig:treatments}). At the time, this method revolutionized IA treatment and remains one of the most common treatments today in some parts of the world. However, its disadvantages are considerable. First, open brain surgery is risky due to the delicate environment and the risk of infections. Secondly, the use of a clip is only feasible when the aneurysm neck is sufficiently narrow to maintain the structure, substantially reducing the applicability. Other secondary motivations, such as the increased costs of patient care during the longer recovery time make this approach less attractive in Western countries.

The introduction of endovascular techniques in the 1990s led to a shift in the treatment paradigm. Unlike surgical approaches requiring a craniotomy\footnote{Craniotomy: The surgical removal of part of the bone from the skull to expose the brain.}, treatments performed through the catheterized parent vessel bear a considerably smaller risk of complications\,\cite{Molyneux2005}. More precisely, during endovascular treatment, the patient is put under general anaesthesia, and a catheter\footnote{Catheter: A flexible tube inserted through a narrow opening into a body cavity.} is inserted into the arterial system, commonly through the femoral artery. Apart from being a large, accessible vessel, the femoral artery is situated far from the brain, reducing the risk of critical infections and shortening recovery times. The catheter is then navigated upstream through the aorta into either the carotid or vertebral arteries until reaching the aneurysm. In the first proposed endovascular treatment, coiling, detachable platinum wires are inserted into the aneurysm sac, slowing down the intrasaccular flow to a point where a stable thrombus can form (see the left of \autoref{fig:treatments}). The thrombus then seals the aneurysm from the parent vessel, preventing rupture. This method, when applicable, is considered the gold standard in IA treatment, even after rupture\,\cite{Pineda2024}. Unfortunately, its applicability is not universally guaranteed. Coils need to be safely introduced into the aneurysmal sac without the risk of detachment, as loose wires in the bloodstream would have fatal consequences. Therefore, the aneurysm neck must be sufficiently narrow to maintain the structure, excluding wide-necked and fusiform\footnote{Fusiform: A spindle-like shape similar to one of a Zeppelin.} aneurysms from treatment. The latter IA phenotype, with a 10\% prevalence, is substantially less frequent than its saccular counterpart and less well-studied\,\cite{Keedy2006}. To extend intrasaccular treatment, new devices have been proposed, such as the Woven EndoBridge (WEB). This device is built with a set of interwoven metallic wires that are fixed at both ends, forming a foldable cylindrical device. It is deployed, similarly to coils, through a transluminal catheter into the aneurysm sac. The design of this device makes it more suited for aneurysms situated at vessel bifurcations\,\cite{KABBASCH2019}. Both of coils and the WEB device have a potential risk of aneurysm recurrence and neck remnants, in which case a second intervention is needed.

Despite these numerous approaches, many IAs remained outside the treatability range and required supportive means. One tool that facilitates coil deployment is braided stents. Their normal intended use is, similarly to coronary stents, widening stenosis and improving the blood flow. Here, however, the stent is deployed over the neck to provide a scaffold to coils in the sack. While extending the range of applicability, this method still is not enough to handle all untreatable cases\,\cite{Shin2020}. In some instances, clinicians employed stents alone, to avoid perforating the weakened aneurysm tissue while deploying the coils. They observed that a minor flow redirection sometimes sufficed to improve the patient's condition. This led to the development of flow diverter stents, closely related to intracranial stents, whose main distinguishing feature is a high wire density with reduced mechanical strength\,\cite{Dandapat2021}. These properties follow from using 2--3 times more wires with only half of their thickness approximately\,\cite{Bouillot2014, Dmytriw2019, Maragkos2020}. Since their approval in the early 2010s, Flow-Diverter Stents (FDS) have enabled clinicians to treat complex, fusiform IAs without sacrificing the parent vessel or resorting to surgery. When used, the clinician measures the parent vessel and selects the proximal\footnote{Proximal: In the medical context, the side closest to the heart. In that sense, the opposite of distal.} and distal landing zones. The selected FDS is deployed by advancing a catheter past the aneurysm and unsheathing the device while pulling the catheter back. Once in place, the wires redirect flow away from the bulge, allowing a stable thrombus to form inside the cavity. After a successful intervention, the thrombus is slowly converted into collagen, leading to the reabsorption of the IA\@. Like all previously mentioned methods, this one also has downsides. Due to the presence of metallic wires in the bloodstream, patients experience an increased risk of blood clot formation inside the artery. These clots can grow, travel downstream, and cause a stroke if not prevented. For this reason, patients carrying stents and FDS are prescribed with (dual) antiplatelet medication for the rest of their lives\,\cite{Dmytriw2019, Maragkos2020, Dandapat2021}. Additionally, these devices can lead to complications when covering bifurcating vessels, as they can either clog them or cause a thrombus to form inside them. This clash between potential benefits and risks has led to a heterogeneous use of FDS in the medical community, reflected also in widely scattered success rates across studies\,\cite{Maragkos2020}. In the latter review study, success rates ranged from 49\% to 100\%, raising numerous questions about how and under which circumstances these devices are used. It is also clear that the function of FDS is not fully understood by the community, a fact that is surely related to the complexity of aneurysms and their formation, growth, and rupture mechanisms.

\section{Engineering in Medical Research}
Numerous questions arising from the IA growth and treatment call for a deeper analysis beyond statistical outcomes after treatment. As we have seen, the disease and the treatment options depend largely on the hemodynamics. There are several ways of obtaining a picture of the aneurysmal flow: in-vivo measurements, in-vitro experiments and lastly in-silico simulations. We will briefly discuss these below and work on some measurement modalities in Chapter 3.

\subsection{Why Simulations Matter}
Patient data is overall the most valuable form of information since it stems directly from the patients and thus carries the smallest amount of bias. Nevertheless, it comes at a costs and is not generally available. First, some measurement techniques, such as Computed Tomography (CT), expose patients to radiation that however harmless it seems, needs to be waged against the possible benefits of the targetted study. This is mostly the case when considering health subjects for reference populations since they have no medical reason to undergo those checks. Another difficulty is the applicability. Doppler-Ultrasound, for instance, is non-invasive and can measure the flow velocities, but is limited to areas that are not covered by bones-a problematic limitation when considering the brain. Experimental and computational methods come into play at this stage, by attempting to bridge and combine the scarce recordings to obtain the full picture. In-vitro setups can use realistic silicone models in which blood-like substances, such as glycerol, are pumped through in regular pulses. In methods like Particle Image Velocimetry, the fluid is seeded by small fluorescent particles that can be tracked to obtain a 3D map of the flow velocity. This technique has been long used to study the flow in diverse environments and to validate and calibrate simulation models. Compared to simulations, experiments manage to avoid certain modelling steps, such as solving expensive numerical problems and providing valuable insights where the physics are not well understood\cite{Sindeev2018}. There are however two main reasons, why in-vitro experiments are not the go-to method for IA studies. First, experimental setups are time-consuming for researchers to carry out, as they require lots of preparation, post-processing and repetitions to ensure the results are reliable. Secondly, results are limited to the setup and the used materials, which can be a problem when trying to generalize the findings. In the case of IA, for instance, blood cannot be used due to its low opacity and thrombogenic behaviour outside a living organism. Similarly to simulations, experiments also rely on knowledge about boundary conditions and material properties, providing no further insight into these complex aspects.

\subsection{Computational Fluid Dynamics}
Boosted by the improving performances of computing systems, CFD has become a fundamental tool in research and development. In industry, simulations permit testing in parallel arrays of product designs for optimization purposes, saving time and resources for prototyping. In research, CFD can recreate plenty of natural phenomena and provides a useful tool for hypothesis testing. It can also be used to prognose the intensity of catastrophic phenomena, such as tsunamis, supporting the decision-making process. In biomedical applications, CFD are exploited to recreate the hemodynamics in narrow, confined spaces, elucidating in detail the flow patterns following imposed boundary conditions.

In CFD simulations a fluid domain is considered as an assembly of small, discrete elements on which the solution to some governing equations are approximated. Then, the solutions are reassembled from the small contributions, providing a macroscopic view of the flow. The dynamics of the system are usually driven by the Navier-Stokes equations, which describe the conservation of momentum, mass, and energy in the case of compressibility. The users of CFD prescribe a set of boundary conditions and material properties following the known system characteristics. Naturally, each system is subject to different levels of complexity, like the presence of turbulence, compressibility, or multiphasic flow. These impact the choice of the solver and the computational resources needed to solve the problem in a feasible time. Finding a set of suitable simplifications is not an easy task and requires careful consideration. Therefore, Chapter 2 will be entirely dedicated to the description of the governing equations and the numerical methods used in CFD\@.

\subsection{Motivation}
\subsubsection*{Current Achievements}
So far we have shown the interest of learning more about the hemodynamics of intracranial aneurysms and designated CFD as the tool to provide us with the sought insights. This procedure has been already applied to several applications in the human body with highly satisfactory outcomes. Cardiovascular research commonly profits from hemodynamics analysis, for instance in Taylor \textit{et\,al.}\,(2013)\,\cite{Taylor2013}, where a better estimation of the pressure drop following coronary stenosis was found with CFD than with traditional invasive methods. As one of the most prominent examples, this finding has proven that in-silico tests can avoid risks and complications for patients. Other research groups have successfully related plaque formation to altered hemodynamics across different locations in the arterial system\,\cite{Morbiducci2016}. These findings help us understand how in principle it could be possible to predict the progression of the disease and help clinical decision making. Many other applications devote their time to simulating and optimizing medical devices, from heart valves\,\cite{LURAGHI201745} to dialysis machines (see\,\cite{BEHBAHANI2009} and therein). All of these accomplishments came after exhaustive, often multi-centered analysis. This includes iterations of hypotheses and several checks until a validated consensus is found.

\subsubsection*{Current Research}
Computational Fluid Dynamics has proven to be a valuable tool in advancing research and development in various diseases. Intracranial aneurysm research, in particular, has benefited from this discipline, helping to clarify some of the underlying disease mechanisms\,\cite{Meng2014}. However, there remains significant room for improvement and validation of the proposed theories, as discussed earlier. The two primary areas of focus are the study of the pathology and its treatments. Regarding pathology, advances in imaging and measurement technologies have enabled larger studies that incorporate high-fidelity data with statistical and methodological significance. These advances allow for the identification of correlations that can enhance our understanding of the disease.

The second major research area for CFD and solid mechanics in the context of IA is the optimization of treatment strategies. Beyond the obvious benefits of refining treatment methods, this research also enhances our understanding of the hemodynamic changes that contribute to positive disease outcomes. For ``conventional'' saccular aneurysms, guidelines already exist for coil packing, stent porosity, and placement\,\cite{Dandapat2021}. However, less common aneurysms, such as blister-like, fusiform, and giant aneurysms, are not specifically addressed. These types of aneurysms are associated with higher morbidity and mortality rates\,\cite{Peschillo2016, Kulcsar2011} and have limited treatment options available. Flow diverters are increasingly being used in these cases due to their versatility and ability to conform to the shape of the aneurysm\,\cite{Mokin2018}. However, in the absence of established treatment guidelines, clinicians often rely on intuition, sometimes deploying multiple stents, flow-diverter stents, or even adding coils to achieve the desired occlusion of the aneurysm. To improve outcomes and define what constitutes an optimal result, large studies could be conducted to closely examine these cases. This would include simulations of fluid dynamics and stent deployment to determine which flow reductions and stent porosities yield the best outcomes.

\subsubsection*{Current Challenges}
Currently, the progress in this field is throttled by two factors, (i) an inconsistent choice of techniques and approximations when carrying out the simulations and (ii) data availability. The first issue was identified by researchers like Steinman, Berg and Valen-Sendstad\,\cite{Berg2015, Steinman2013, Valen-Sendstad2018} when coming across conflicting theories about IA growth. They hypothesized that research teams were employing modelling assumptions so different from each other that their results would lead them to other interpretations. They tested this hypothesis by opening computing challenges in which participants would simulate the same cases and report their results. With those, the organizers could analyze the deviations and assess the research question. Berg and Vo\ss\,\textit{et\,al.}\,\cite{Berg2018, voss2019} isolated perhaps the largest source of dissent, the segmentations. Due to the complex and per-definition irregular shape of aneurysms, this task is subject to variability even when carried out by clinicians. In many other cases, they found that the community chooses inconsistently their approximations\,\cite{Berg2019}. Interestingly, when it came to selecting aneurysms that would likely rupture, no clear strategy prevailed over the other.

The reason for this turmoil lies ultimately in the lack of measurement data that forces the use of approximations. Luckily, imaging technology is advancing in recent times introducing more potent Magnetic Resonance Tomography and Computed Tomography scanners, among others. These will likely improve data availability thereby alleviating data-related issues.

The second factor, data availability, is conditioned by the relatively slow exchange of medical and the less frequently appearing aneurysm phenotypes. Due to the sensitive nature of medical data, it is important to ensure a responsible handling of patient information. Additionally, to obtain a statistically relevant cohort size for studies, researchers are required to interview many medical institutions, repeating the weary process several times. As a consequence, less attention has been given to giant and blister-like aneurysms, despite their relatively high propensity to complications, 20--25\%\,\cite{Parkinson2006, Gruber1999} and 38\%\,\cite{Peschillo2016}, respectively.

\subsubsection*{Structure of the Thesis}
Based on the discussion, we have identified a critical need for further research into aneurysms, especially complex types like blood-blister and giant aneurysms, to improve their treatment options. To address this, our goal is to develop a high-fidelity framework for simulating aneurysm hemodynamics in both preoperative and postoperative states. Our work will specifically focus on the use of flow diverters as a treatment option, deploying them through a new algorithm based on geometrical constraints.

This thesis is organized into four chapters, each outlining the essential steps in the development process. Chapter 2 revisits fluid dynamics theory, beginning with the constitutive equations and advancing to discretized systems. This chapter also covers rheology modelling and introduces a novel method in stabilized Finite Elements, which is verified using two classic benchmarks from CFD literature.

In Chapter 3, we investigate the fundamental properties of brain blood flow, with a focus on mesh generation and boundary conditions. We also examine the impact of the simulated domain size on aneurysm hemodynamics, using three patient-specific arterial systems as case studies.

Chapter 4 presents a geometry-based flow diverter deployment model, offering a robust and efficient tool for predicting device sizes, and porosities, and generating wire meshes. The algorithm is thoroughly validated against examples from the literature, in-vitro experiments, and an in-vivo case.

Chapter 5 applies the developed methods to three patient cases involving ruptured giant, blister-like, and wide-neck aneurysms. The giant aneurysm case is studied concerning delayed rupture phenomena after treatment, including a fluid-structure interaction simulation. The other two cases focus on post-rupture treatment, highlighting the changes in hemodynamics from an unstented state to the use of two telescopic stents.
