\section{Intracranial Aneurysms}\label{intro:sec1}

\subsection{Disease description}
Intracranial aneurysms (IA) are abnormal dilations of the cerebral arteries that can be found on an estimated 4\%\,\cite{Rinkel1998} to 3.2\%\,\cite{Tawk2021} of adult population. They are often asymptomatic and discovered incidentally during imaging studies for other reasons. In cases where the IA are symptomatic, the most common symptoms are headaches, visual disturbances, and cranial nerve palsies, caused by the compression of the surrounding brain tissue. When an IA is sufficiently large to cause these adverse effects, the risk of rupture is also increased. The rupture of an IA can lead to a subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), a severe condition that results in the death of the patient in 50\% of the cases\,\cite{Rinkel1998}. During a SAH, blood spills into the subarachnoid space, the area between the arachnoid membrane and the pia mater surrounding the brain. The presence of blood in this area unsets a chain of events, commonly leading to an insufficient perfusion\footnote{Perfusion: The passage of blood through vessels or other natural channels in an organ or tissue.} of the brain. This decific in blood flow causes neurologic damage which less than 25\% of patients survive asymptomatically\,\cite{Molyneux2005}, underlining the severity of the phenomenon.

Luckily, IA rupture and a subsequent SAH remain seldom event. In fact, 50--80\% of IA are estimated to never rupture\,\cite{Tawk2021}. When patients are diagnosed with an IA, clinicians assess the risk of rupture based on several morphological, topological and categorical factors. The morphology of the aneurysm can be a good indicator of the risk when pronounced daughter sacks are present. These secondary aneurysms have been correlated with a pronounced risk of rupture and pose an imminent threat\,\cite{Meng2014}. Similarly, when in follow-up scans the IA is shown to be growing or multiple aneurysms are found, treatment is strongly encouraged. The correlation of size with rupture risk on the other hand is a controversial factor. Until recent years, large (10--20\,mm) and giant aneurysms ($\geq$ 20\,mm) were related to an increased rupture risk compared to middle (7--10\,mm) and small ones ($\leq$ 7\,mm)\,\cite{Bijlenga2017}. Studies analyzing ruptured IA concluded that if treatment decisions were to be largely decided by IA size, 78\% of ruptured cases would not have been classified as in risk\,\cite{Pagiola2020}. At the same time, it is important to consider, that most IA are classified as small and that conditional dependance is important to consider when risk metrics are defined. The age of the patient plays also a decisive role. While the yearly rupture risk is higher for older patients ($\leq$ 70 years) \,\cite{Bijlenga2017}, the cumulative risk for younger individuals is larger due to the longer life expectancy. Apart from the mentioned categories, there is a number of other considerations that might be taken into account differently by the treating clinician.

\subsection{Treatment}
The motivation behind these careful considerations is that any intervention entails a non-negligible risk for the patient, and furthermore, not all treatments meet their intended purpose. Ever since the 18$^\textit{th}$ century, doctors have trying to treat IA with various methods, first and foremost, by cutting the flow in the parent artery and inducing a thrombosis of the aneurysm. As can be expected these first operations more than rarely led to the immediate death of the patient. This is not necessarily attributed to the chosen method, but rather the hygenic conditions under which first interventions were carried out. Nowadays, the embolization\footnote{Embolization: Blockage of vessel by natural or surgical means, such as coils.} of a parent vessel can be considered in cases where other treatments are not applicable and enough periferal flow reaches the distal sections past the aneurysms. In 1937, Walter Edward Dandy carried out the first surgical clipping operation, a procedure in which after opening the skull of the patient the aneurysm neck is ligated with a metallic clip, impeding a secluding the bulge from flow and preventing rupture. At the time, this method revolutionized the treatment of IA and is still one of the most common treatments today in parts of the world. Nevertheless, its disadvantages are also considerable. First, open brain surgery is a risky procedure due to the delicate environment and the risk of infections. Secondly, the use of a clip is only possible when the aneurysm neck is sufficiently narrow to maintain the structure, substantially redusing the range of applicability. Other secondary motivations, such as the increased costs of patient care during the longer recovery time, make this approach less attractive in western countries.


The introduction of endovascular techniques in the 1990s, however, has led to a shift in the treatment paradigm. Contrary to the surgical approaches that require a craniotomy\footnote{Craniotomy: The surgical removal of part of the bone from the skull to expose the brain.}, treatments carried out through the catheterized parent vessel bear a considerable smaller risk of complications\cite{Molyneux2005}. More precisely, during endovascular treatment the patient is put under general anesthesia and a catheter\footnote{Catheter: A flexible tube inserted through a narrow opening into a body cavity.} is inserted into the arterial system, commonly through the femoral artery. Apart from being a large, accessible vessel, the femoral artery is situated far from the brain, reducing the risk of critical infections and reducing recovery times. The catheter is then navigated upstream through the aorta into either the carotid or vertebral arteries until reaching the aneurysm. In the first proposed endovascular treatment, coiling, detachable platinum wires are inserted into the aneurysm sack, slowing down the intrasaccular flow to a point where a stable thrombus can form. The thrombus then seals the aneurysm from the parent vessel, preventing rupture. This method, when applicable, is claimed to be the golden standart in IA treatment, even after the case of rupture\cite{Pineda2024}. Unfortunately, its applicability is not generally guaranteed. Coils need to be safely introduced into the aneurysmal sack without the risk of detachament, as a loose wire in the blood stream would have fatal consequences. Therefore, the aneurysm neck needs to be sufficiently narrow to maintain the structure, excluding wide-necked and fusiform aneurysms from treatment. The latter IA phenotype is with a 10\% prevalence substantially less frequent than its saccular counterpart and less well studied\cite{Keedy2006}. To extend the intrasaccular treatment, new devices have been proposed, like for instance the Woven EndoBridge (WEB). This device is build with a set of intervowen metallic wires that are fixed at both ends forming a foldable cylindric device. It is deployed, similarly to coils, through a transluminar catheter into the aneurysm sack. The design of this device makes its use more suited for aneurysms situated at vessel bifurcations. Both of the intrasaccular techniques have a potential risk of aneurysm reocurrence and neck remanants, in which case a second intervention is necessary.

However, many IA remained outside the range of treatability of coils and needed to be dealt with by other means. One supporting tool that aims to facilitate the deployment are braided stents. These tube-like devices are inserted into the parent vessel covering the neck of the IA. Then, the coils are deployed through the stent into the vessel, where, thanks to the supporting scaffold, they find a better hold. While extending the range of applicabilitty, this method still is not enough to bridge handle all untreatable cases\cite{Shin2020}. In some cases, clinicians deployed stents alone fearing a perforation of extremely weakened aneurysm tissue with the coils. They noticed that a minor redirection of flow was sometimes sufficient to improve the patient's condition. This observation lead to the development of flow diverter stents, a close relative to intracranial stents, whose main differing feature is a high wire density with reduced mechanical strength\cite{Dandapat2021}. Ever since their approval in the beginning of the 2010s decade, Flow Diverter Stents (FDS) have provided clinicians with a dedicated tool for treating complex, fusiform IA. When chosen to be used, the clinician carefully measures the parent vessel and selects the proximal\footnote{Proximal: In the medical context, the side closest to the heart. In that sense the opposit to distal.} and distal landing zones. According to the measurements, a FDS is selected and deployed by advancing a catheter past the aneurysm and unsheathing the device while pulling the catheter back. The FDS is considerably softer than its stent and expands automatically when pushed out of its envelop. Once in place, the wires redirect flow away from the bulge, allowing a stable thrombus to form inside the cavity. After a successful intervention, the thrombus is slowly converted into collagen leading to the reabsorbtion of the IA. As all the previously mentioned methods, this one does too have its downsides. Due to the presence of metallic wires in the blood stream, patients experience an increased risk of blood clot formation inside the artery. These can grow, travel downstream and lead to a stroke, if not prevented. For this reason, patients carrying stents and FDS need to follow a dual anti-platelet medication for the rest of their lives\cite{Dmytriw2019, Maragkos2020, Dandapat2021}. Also, these devices can lead to complications when deployed across bifurcating vessels, as they can either clog them or cause a thrombus to form inside them. These clash between potential benefits and risks has lead to a heterogeneous used of FDS in the medical community which also reflects on widely scattered success rates across different centers and practitioners\cite{Maragkos2020}. In the latter review study, success rates from 49\% all the way to 100\% were reported, raising numerous questions about how and under which circumstances these devices are used. It is also clear that the function of FDS is not fully understood by the community, a fact that is surely related to the complexity of aneurysms, their formation, growth and rupture mechanisms.

\subsection{Pathology}
The root causes of IA are still not thoroughly understood, albeit many works consider alterations of the local blood flow, genetics as well as other envirnmental factors responsible. IA are not equally present among ages and gender, with women being approximately 1.5 times more likely to have IA than men\cite{Rinkel1998}, and children being rarely affected by the disease. Other influencial factor were observed to be genetic. The presence of IA in family members was found to raise the prevalence of IA to 9.5\% (approximately 3 times the base ocurrence rate). Diseases, such as artherosclerosis and autosomal polycistic kindney disease were likewise correlated with an increased risk of harbouring IA and a rupture\cite{Rinkel1998}.

Among the external factors we find the commonly found increased risk for smokers\cite{Ji2016, Thompson2015} and other factors that contribute to arterial stiffening and hypertension. These factors have lead to think that the origin of IA could be related to the interaction between the brain arteries and disturbed blood flow. In the case of hypertension for instance, the heart generates exerts larger forces to push blood through the body. Consequently, pulsatility and blood pressures rise incresing the fluid stress on arteries. The first component of arteries perceiving flow changes is the endothelium, a single layer of cells covering the inner-most section of the vessels that is responsible for biochemical signaing, metabolic exchange as well as other regulatory processes\cite{Dessalles2021}. In healthy tissue, this cell layer perceives tensile stresses and pressure fluctuations and, accordingly, triggers remodelling cascades to adapt the growth and remodelling of the subsequent layers.
