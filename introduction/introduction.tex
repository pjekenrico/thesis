\section{Intracranial Aneurysms}\label{intro:sec1}

\subsection{Pathology}
Intracranial aneurysms (IA) are abnormal dilations of the cerebral arteries found in an estimated 3.2\%\,\cite{Tawk2021, Vlak2011} to 4\%\,\cite{Rinkel1998} of the adult population. They are often asymptomatic and discovered incidentally during imaging studies conducted for other reasons. When IAs are symptomatic, the patients can experience headaches, visual disturbances, and cranial nerve palsies, caused by the compression of surrounding brain tissue. The rupture of an IA, which can happen independently on the size and symptoms, leads to a subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), a severe condition with a 50\% mortality rate\,\cite{Rinkel1998}. During a SAH, blood spills into the subarachnoid space—the area between the arachnoid membrane and the pia mater surrounding the brain. This presence of blood sets off a chain of events, commonly leading to insufficient perfusion\footnote{Perfusion: The passage of blood through vessels or other natural channels in an organ or tissue.} of the brain. This deficit in blood flow causes neurological damage, with less than 25\% of patients surviving unharmed\,\cite{Molyneux2005}, highlighting the severity of the condition.

Fortunately, IA rupture and subsequent SAH are rare events. In fact, 50--80\% of IAs are estimated to never rupture\,\cite{Tawk2021}. When patients are diagnosed with an IA, clinicians assess the rupture risk based on several morphological, topological, and categorical factors. The morphology of the aneurysm can indicate the risk, especially when pronounced daughter sacs are present. These secondary aneurysms correlate with a significantly increased risk of rupture and pose an imminent threat\,\cite{Meng2014}. Similarly, when follow-up scans show the IA growing or multiple aneurysms are found, treatment is strongly encouraged. The correlation of size with rupture risk, however, remains controversial. Until recent years, large (10--20\,mm) and giant aneurysms ($\geq$ 20\,mm) were believed to have a higher rupture risk compared to medium (7--10\,mm) and small ones ($\leq$ 7\,mm)\,\cite{Bijlenga2017}. Studies analyzing ruptured IAs concluded that if treatment decisions were largely based on IA size, 78\% of ruptured cases would not have been classified as at risk\,\cite{Pagiola2020}. It is also important to consider that most IAs are classified as small and that conditional dependence is crucial when defining risk metrics. The age of the patient also plays a decisive role. While the yearly rupture risk is higher for older patients ($\geq$ 70 years)\,\cite{Bijlenga2017}, the cumulative risk for younger individuals is larger due to their longer life expectancy. Apart from the mentioned categories, there are numerous other considerations that might be taken into account differently by the treating clinician.

\subsection{Treatment}
The motivation behind these careful considerations is that any intervention entails a non-negligible risk for the patient, and furthermore, not all treatments meet their intended purpose. Since the 18th century, doctors have attempted to treat IA with various methods, primarily by cutting the flow in the parent artery and inducing thrombosis of the aneurysm. Unsurprisingly, these initial operations often resulted in the immediate death of the patient. This was not necessarily due to the chosen method but rather the unhygienic conditions under which these early interventions were carried out. Nowadays, the embolization\footnote{Embolization: Blockage of a vessel by natural or surgical means, such as coils.} of a parent vessel can be considered in cases where other treatments are not applicable, provided sufficient peripheral flow reaches the distal sections past the aneurysms.

In 1937, Walter Edward Dandy performed the first surgical clipping operation. In this procedure, after opening the patient's skull, the aneurysm neck is ligated with a metallic clip, isolating the bulge from blood flow and preventing rupture. At the time, this method revolutionized IA treatment and remains one of the most common treatments today in some parts of the world. However, its disadvantages are considerable. First, open brain surgery is risky due to the delicate environment and the risk of infections. Secondly, the use of a clip is only feasible when the aneurysm neck is sufficiently narrow to maintain the structure, substantially reducing the range of applicability. Other secondary motivations, such as the increased costs of patient care during the longer recovery time, make this approach less attractive in Western countries.

The introduction of endovascular techniques in the 1990s led to a shift in the treatment paradigm. Unlike surgical approaches that require a craniotomy\footnote{Craniotomy: The surgical removal of part of the bone from the skull to expose the brain.}, treatments performed through the catheterized parent vessel bear a considerably smaller risk of complications\,\cite{Molyneux2005}. More precisely, during endovascular treatment, the patient is put under general anesthesia, and a catheter\footnote{Catheter: A flexible tube inserted through a narrow opening into a body cavity.} is inserted into the arterial system, commonly through the femoral artery. Apart from being a large, accessible vessel, the femoral artery is situated far from the brain, reducing the risk of critical infections and shortening recovery times. The catheter is then navigated upstream through the aorta into either the carotid or vertebral arteries until reaching the aneurysm. In the first proposed endovascular treatment, coiling, detachable platinum wires are inserted into the aneurysm sac, slowing down the intrasaccular flow to a point where a stable thrombus can form. The thrombus then seals the aneurysm from the parent vessel, preventing rupture. This method, when applicable, is considered the gold standard in IA treatment, even after rupture\,\cite{Pineda2024}. Unfortunately, its applicability is not universally guaranteed. Coils need to be safely introduced into the aneurysmal sac without the risk of detachment, as a loose wire in the bloodstream would have fatal consequences. Therefore, the aneurysm neck must be sufficiently narrow to maintain the structure, excluding wide-necked and fusiform aneurysms from treatment. The latter IA phenotype, with a 10\% prevalence, is substantially less frequent than its saccular counterpart and less well-studied\,\cite{Keedy2006}. To extend intrasaccular treatment, new devices have been proposed, such as the Woven EndoBridge (WEB). This device is built with a set of interwoven metallic wires that are fixed at both ends, forming a foldable cylindrical device. It is deployed, similarly to coils, through a transluminal catheter into the aneurysm sac. The design of this device makes it more suited for aneurysms situated at vessel bifurcations. Both of the intrasaccular techniques have a potential risk of aneurysm recurrence and neck remnants, necessitating a second intervention in such cases.

However, many IAs remained outside the treatability range of coils and needed to be managed by other means. One supporting tool that aims to facilitate deployment is braided stents. These tube-like devices are inserted into the parent vessel, covering the neck of the IA. Then, the coils are deployed through the stent into the vessel, where, thanks to the supporting scaffold, they find a better hold. While extending the range of applicability, this method still is not enough to handle all untreatable cases\,\cite{Shin2020}. In some instances, clinicians deployed stents alone, fearing perforation of extremely weakened aneurysm tissue with the coils. They observed that a minor redirection of flow sometimes sufficed to improve the patient's condition. This observation led to the development of flow diverter stents, closely related to intracranial stents, whose main distinguishing feature is a high wire density with reduced mechanical strength\,\cite{Dandapat2021}. Since their approval in the early 2010s, Flow Diverter Stents (FDS) have provided clinicians with a dedicated tool for treating complex, fusiform IAs. When used, the clinician carefully measures the parent vessel and selects the proximal\footnote{Proximal: In the medical context, the side closest to the heart. In that sense, the opposite to distal.} and distal landing zones. According to the measurements, an FDS is selected and deployed by advancing a catheter past the aneurysm and unsheathing the device while pulling the catheter back. The FDS is considerably softer than a stent and expands automatically when pushed out of its envelope. Once in place, the wires redirect flow away from the bulge, allowing a stable thrombus to form inside the cavity. After a successful intervention, the thrombus is slowly converted into collagen, leading to the reabsorption of the IA. Like all previously mentioned methods, this one also has downsides. Due to the presence of metallic wires in the bloodstream, patients experience an increased risk of blood clot formation inside the artery. These clots can grow, travel downstream, and cause a stroke if not prevented. For this reason, patients carrying stents and FDS need to follow dual antiplatelet medication for the rest of their lives\,\cite{Dmytriw2019, Maragkos2020, Dandapat2021}. Additionally, these devices can lead to complications when deployed across bifurcating vessels, as they can either clog them or cause a thrombus to form inside them. This clash between potential benefits and risks has led to heterogeneous use of FDS in the medical community, reflected in widely scattered success rates across different centers and practitioners\,\cite{Maragkos2020}. In the latter review study, success rates ranged from 49\% to 100\%, raising numerous questions about how and under which circumstances these devices are used. It is also clear that the function of FDS is not fully understood by the community, a fact that is surely related to the complexity of aneurysms and their formation, growth, and rupture mechanisms.

\subsection{Epidemiology}
The root causes of IA are still not thoroughly understood, although many works consider alterations of local blood flow, genetics, and other environmental factors to be responsible. One initial step towards understanding the root cause of the disease is considering who and under which circumstances contracts it. When discussing Prevalence Ratios (PR), studies oftentimes fix an overall PR and then state based on that value how a characteristic, \eg\,gender or age, modifies it multiplicatively. The base value, as stated before, hovers around 3.2\% for a 50 year-old person\cite{Vlak2011}. Thus, a PR of two would yield a twofold likelyhood with respect to the base PR\@.

One of the first apparent observations is that IA are not equally present across different ages and genders, with women being approximately 1.5 to 3 times more likely to have IA than men. Gender does not just condition the presence of the disease, but also the location of the IA, although clear causes have not been established yet\,\cite{Rinkel1998, Brown2014}. IA incidence is relatively provenence-independent, \ie\,unruptured IA are roughly equally present in different counteries. However, the rupture rates are not, as seen, for instance, in Finnland and Japan with SAH being to 2 and 3 times more likely\,\cite{Etminan2019}. Genetics overall seem to play part of a role in the PR, since the presence of an IA in the direct family raises prevalence to 9.5\% (approximately three times the base occurrence rate). Few other diseases that are inherited, such as Autosomal Dominant Polycystic Kidney Disease, are known to raise the risks (PR\,=\,6.9)\,\cite{Vlak2011}.

As for many other pathologies, bad habits such as smoking raise the risk of IA and SAH\,\cite{Ji2016, Thompson2015}. Atherosclerosis\footnote{Atherosclerosis: Disease of the arteries characterized by the deposition of fatty material on their inner walls.} and hypertension that often accompany these perpetual habits alter in general the human's hemodynamics and the health of the arteries. Artherosclerosis on one side causes plaque formation in healthy vessels, compromising blood flow. The same depositions have been found in IA, creating a link between both diseases. Hypertension, as the reader probably knows, implies an increased blood pressure in the cardiovascular system surpassing 140 mmHg at systole and 90 mmHg at diastole. The increased pressure exerted by the heart raises also peak flow velocities and pulsatility of flow profiles. Oftentimes, it is a response from the body to arterial stiffening, caused among others, by the previously mentioned bad habits.

\subsection{Pathogenesis}
These factors suggest that the origin of IA could be related to the interaction between the arterial walls and disturbed blood flow in people with a certain predisposition. The first component of arteries perceiving flow changes is the endothelium, a single layer of cells covering the innermost section of the vessels, responsible for biochemical signaling, metabolic exchange, and other regulatory processes\,\cite{Liebeskind2016, Dessalles2021}. In healthy tissue, this cell layer perceives tensile stresses and pressure fluctuations, triggering remodeling cascades to determine the development of the subsequent layers. Situated after the endothelium is the subendothelium and the internal elastic lamina, a fenestrated layer of tissue generally damaged or missing in IA\,\cite{Meng2014}. The elastic lamina separates the internal layer (tunica intima) from a muscular structure that constitutes most of the tunica media. This layer is populated by smooth muscle cells whose main purpose is regulating the flow distribution in the brain through contraction and relaxation. Unlike other arteries in the human body, brain arteries play a larger role in perfusion since arterioles, the smaller vessels following arteries, lack regulatory components found elsewhere. The muscular layer is found to be likewise damaged in most saccular aneurysms\,\cite{Meng2014}, giving some formations an almost translucent appearance. The final layer, the adventitia, is made of collagen and elastic components that carry a large portion of the structural load.

\subsubsection*{Initiation}
Aneurysm initiation is thought to occur after an extended exposure of a patch of artery to a strong impinging jet. The affected zone, which might already be weakened through the aforementioned medical conditions, experiences degradation of the endothelium and the elastic lamina due to the strong shearing from blood flow\,\cite{Meng2014}. Eventually, this leads to the formation of a bulge. While the initiation is agreed upon, the growth process and rupture risk assessment remain subjects of debate. In a 2014 paper, Meng \etal\,formulated a unifying hypothesis for the otherwise opposing assumptions on high or low shearing-induced changes. By shearing, we understand formally the Wall Shear Stress (WSS), an expression of the friction perceived by an object in contact with a flowing viscous fluid.

\subsubsection*{Growth}
Studies on one side have highlighted high WSS to be related to the rupture of IA, particularly in the anterior communication\,\cite{Castro2009}. Others\,\cite{Xiang2011} found low and oscillatory shearing in growing or ruptured formations. Given these clashing principles, Meng hypothesized that once an initial bulge is found, one of two phenomena is possible. In the first case, the shearing remains high despite the tissue deformation. The smooth muscle cells start generating inflammatory agents that trigger the remodeling of the arterial wall. In healthy tissue, this remodeling adjusts the constitution of the vessel to cope with increased stress. Here, per-contrary, it results in a sped-up cell apoptosis\footnote{Apoptosis: The process of programmed cell death.} without the desired reinforcement, leading to a hypocellular aneurysm (see aneurysm A in \autoref{fig:meng}).

The second option is that the overall WSS levels drop in the sac due to the spread of the impinging jet over a larger curved area and possibly a redirection of the flow. In this case, where oscillatory components of the shearing become more noticeable, a similar chain of events as in atherosclerosis begins. The oscillatory flow, formally quantified through the Oscillatory Shear Index (OSI), disrupts the junctions between endothelial cells and elicits a pro-inflammatory behavior of the cells,\cite{Dessalles2021}. The first consequence of this phenomenon is increased permeability, allowing leukocytes to traverse the endothelium. The immune cells then spark an inflammation of the tissue, which degrades the extracellular matrix and promotes the accumulation of plaque and lipoproteins,\cite{Meng2014}. When this is the case, the IA attains a white appearance, much like in aneurysm D in \autoref{fig:meng}. Both remodeling pathways can coexist, with some regions of an IA being subject to different external stresses. A possible outcome of a combined remodelling could be reflected on aneurysms like B and C on \autoref{fig:meng}, showing an inhomogeneous wall constitution. 

The remodeling can have either of two outcomes for an IA. The first, and most common, is that at some point, a balance is struck and the bulge stops growing. This stage is called homeostasis and poses a relief for patients and clinicians. IAs in which homeostasis has been confirmed have a risk of rupture 30 times smaller than when growing\,\cite{Brinjikji2016, Tawk2021}. Continued growth of the IA, particularly in the formation of daughter sacs or blebs, is, on the other hand, a worrying indicator. Despite extensive analysis, the unifying hypothesis remains a hypothesis due to the sheer complexity of the biomechanical interactions that shape the process. To aid in finding new clues, the scientific community has started employing simulations of the blood flow in and around IA to get a better grasp of the fluid dynamics phenomena taking place in the brain. With this information, it is hoped that statistical evidence might be found to support or disclaim existing theory, and ideally learn about the working mechanisms of treatment devices.

\section{Hemodynamics of IA}
Hemodynamics\footnote{Hemodynamics: The study of blood flow and the forces involved in circulating blood through the cardiovascular system.} can be investigated \textit{in-vivo} through measurements, \textit{in-vitro} through experimental setups and lastly \textit{in-silico} utilizing computer simulations.

\subsection{Why simulations?}
Patient data is overall the most valuable form of information, grating a direct view of a phenomenon taking place in a real scenario. Unfortunately, this type of data is seldomly available for a number of reasons. First, based on the used technique, a good justification has to be present to expose subject to any risks during measurements. This can be the case, for instance, when performing a Computed Tomography (CT) using contrast agents. Both the exposure to radiation and the injection of intravenous ink are factors, that however harmless thwey seem, need to be waged against their possible benefits. Another difficulty is the applicability of certain methods, \eg\, Doppler-Ultrasound. This latter technique is non-invasive  and can measure the flow velocities, but is limited to areas that are not covered by bones, a problematic limitation when considering the brain. Experimental and computational methods come in play at this stage, by attempting to bridge and combine the scarse recordings to obtain the full picture. \textit{in-vitro} setups usually use realistic silicone models in which blood-like substances, such as glycerol, are pumped through in regular pulses. In methods like Particle Image Velocimetry (PIV), the fluid is seeded by small fluorescent particles that can be tracked to obtain a 3D map of the flow velocity. Compared to computer simulations, experiments manage to avoid certain modelling steps, such as solving expensive numerical problems, and provide valuable insights in scenarios where the physics of the problem are not well understood\cite{Sindeev2018}. Their dominant use in our field however seems limited to validating certain numerical models, which could be explained by their high demands on time. At the same time, experiments also relie on many modelling assumptions related to the used materials (arterial walls and blood), boundary conditions and imaging resolutions. These have motivated the community to explore IAs using Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) simulations.

Boosted by the improving performances of computing systems, CFD has become a fundamental tool in research and development. Its industrial and scientific applications range over design optimization of wind turbines to the simulation of magma streams in earth's crust, limited only by the applicability of the fundamental equations and the size of the simulation. In CFD, an object or a domain is considered as an assembly of small, discrete elements. By approximating some equations in each of these elements and exchanging information with its neighbours, a solution can be obtained.
