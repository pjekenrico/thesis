\chapter{Patient-specific analysis with flow-diverter treatment}\minitoc\newpage\label{chap.5}
\begin{tcolorbox}[abstract]

	In the previous chapters, we have worked on the numerical framework of VMS in fluid simulations, studied the specifics of brain arterial hemodynamics and developed a tool to model Flow-Diverter Stents (FDS) based on geometrical constraints. With the obtained experience and tools, we now move towards putting the framework to use on 3 patient cases displaying two variations of intracranial aneurysms, each posing very unique challenges to the clinicians. These showcase some situations in which FDS treatment is considered the most promising tool, even though fatality rates are still high. First, we will deal with the giant aneurysm ($>$\,25\,mm) that has been already shown in the previous chapter. The case will be thoroughly discussed considering medical and anatomical aspects before diving into the simulation of pre- and postoperative hemodynamics. The second application case involves blister-like aneurysms, formations barely seen on CT imaging characterized by critically thin and fragile walls. Both disease types are poorly understood and require further research to improve the prospective treatment of those in need of it. With the run simulations, we put on display the framework, but most importantly, we provide hemodynamics results on scarsely documented aneurysms and relate them to existing literature.

\section{Delayed rupture of giant aneurysms}\label{chap5:sec1}
Giant aneurysms bear several added difficulties compared to their smaller counterparts. Due to their size, they are often symptomatic, causing cerebral palsies, headaches and other neurological disturbances. Therefore, treating them is often a necessity, albeit operations are more complex and are more prone to complications\,\cite{Rouchaud2016}. Most giant aneurysms contain a sizable portion of the parent vessel and lack a well-defined neck. As a consequence, clipping them surgically is often inviable. Even the deployment of coils is rarely feasible since they require a sufficient hold to remain firmly in place. Furthermore, coils suffer from an increased risk of recanalization due to accentuated material fatigue and material compaction in these cases\,\cite{Dandapat2021}. Even the use stent-assisted coiling has not been found to improve the odds of positive outcome\,\cite{Hirato2023,Rouchaud2016}. Flow-diverter stents, although sometimes difficult to deploy in giant aneurysms, manage to avoid the aforementioned issues. Despite their overall good performance\,\cite{Kulcsar2011}, giant aneurysms still pose a non-negligible risk of delayed rupture\,\cite{Kulcsar2011, Li2019,Rouchaud2016}. Since the first observed cases in the 2010 decade, research teams have proposed several contributing factors and registered their observations. In the following section, we will conduct a detailed analysis of the patient case who sadly experienced this dramatic outcome.


\subsection{Patient medical record}
The patient is a woman born in January 1963, with no history of smoking, hypertension, or family history of intracranial aneurysms. Vertigo along with anxiety and a strange sensation lead her to seek consultation with a neurologist. After a first consultation, she was appointed to do an MRI examination of the crane, revealing a giant intracranial aneurysm compressing the left temporal lobe\footnote{Temporal lobes: Regions of the brain situated on both halves above the ears.}. Her neurologist referred her to a neuro-interventionalist in the same month for further evaluation. A cerebral arteriography confirmed the existence of a giant aneurysm in the left supra-clinoid ICA, between the ophthalmic and communicating segments. The englobing of the parent artery by the aneurysm caused a delayed opacification of the left middle cerebral artery. The small anterior communicating artery was visible during the compression of the left ICA\@.

Based on the presence of symptoms and a menace of rupture, endovascular treatment was proposed to the patient, to which she agreed during the following medical consultation. One week before the procedure, she began taking aspirin (75 mg/day) and prasugrel\footnote{Prasugrel: Platelet aggregation inhibitor medication.} (10 mg/day) to reduce the risk of blood clots. The procedure started with a balloon test occlusion, where a balloon was temporarily inflated in the artery to block blood flow. A guiding catheter was placed in the right and left internal carotid arteries. A Scepter XC 4--11 balloon was positioned in the left ICA upstream of the aneurysm and inflated to stop blood flow. Contrast injection via the right catheter showed a delay of over one second in the opacification of the left cerebral veins compared to the right, indicating that the left carotid artery could not be safely occluded. Therefore, preserving and reconstructing the left ICA with a flow-diverting stent was necessary. A $3$\,mm FRED Jr flow-diverter was deployed from the left MCA (M1 segment) to the left ICA upstream of the aneurysm, immediately causing contrast stasis in the aneurysm sac. Upon waking up, the patient showed no neurological deficits and continued antiplatelet therapy. At her follow-up 1.5 months later, her clinical exam returned normal results. Six months after the procedure, a follow-up cerebral arteriography showed a patent stent\footnote{Patent (stent): Maintaining the openness and integrity of the vessel or structure where it has been placed.} with unchanged opacification of the aneurysm sac. Continued dual antiplatelet therapy was advised, with a repeat arteriography scheduled for ten months post-procedure.

Eight months after the intervention, the patient reported a sudden headache. A CT scan revealed spontaneous hyperdensity in the aneurysm, suggesting thrombosis. A week later, she suffered a sudden coma, with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 4, indicating severe brain injury. A CT scan revealed subarachnoid haemorrhage, intraventricular haemorrhage, and a left temporal hematoma\footnote{Hematoma: A solid swelling of clotted blood within the tissues.}. Cerebral arteriography showed persistent aneurysm opacification, a patent stent, and active contrast leakage from the aneurysm sac's posteroinferior part. A microcatheter was placed in the aneurysm sac through the stent mesh, and the sac was occluded with coils, halting the bleeding and completely occluding the aneurysm. Sadly, the patient passed away five days after this second intervention.

\subsection{Artery topography}\label{chap5:sec1:subsec2}
\subsubsection*{Circle of Willis}
An MRA scan confirmed an incomplete Circle of Willis (CoW), missing the posterior communication ipsilateral to the aneurysm. The contralateral one, which appears to have a fetal nature, contributes most of the blood to the left posterior circulation. The anterior communication, although present, has a small size which was confirmed during a clamp test. According to these features, the structure of the CoW is categorized as a type PCP, a variant found in approximately 7\% of the population\,\cite{Hindenes2023}. Although the CoW configuration carries a 2.3-fold risk of aneurysm formation, it is unclear how it affects the rupture risk.

The aneurysm on the communicating (C7) segment of the supra-clinoidal ICA, 4\,mm before its terminal bifurcation. The Anterior Choroidal Artery (AChA) emanates from it, however, its flow is barely noticeable on DSA scans, as seen on the left of \autoref{fig:stenosis}.

\subsubsection*{Tapering}
An important factor considering this case is the presence of Tapering (T) and vessel deformation around the aneurysm. The cavernous ICA has a diameter of 4.16\,mm falling in line with reported values\,\cite{Baz2021}. It experiences continuous tapering over the ophthalmic (C6) and communicating (C7) segments leading to a nearly elliptical vessel at the proximal aneurysm neck. This atypical cross-section translates into a 32\% tapering to the diameter of the cavernous ICA (see \autoref{tab:pat_geo}). Past the aneurysm, the terminal section narrows down to 2.15\,mm, equivalently to a 48\% stenosis. This value falls outside the standard deviation of the recorded cases by Baz \etal\,\cite{Baz2021}.

After deployment, the proximal vessel sections remain largely unchanged. The clinoidal ICA however shrinks with diameters of 3.33\,mm, which is a 20\% tapering to the cavernous ICA\@. The sections at the aneurysm have become nearly identical in diameter, attaining commonly found sizes. Although it is not verifiable from the imaging, these effects are likely caused by the mechanical forces exerted by the stent and the progressive remodelling of the artery.

\subsection{Aneurysm}\label{chap5:sec1:subsec3}

\subsubsection*{Morphology}
The aneurysm contained its parent vessel prior to the operation. It has a nearly round shape with a diameter of approximately 25\,mm and a volume of 7170\,mm$^3$. In the course of the eight months after the intervention, the aneurysm and the vasculature underwent changes. Most notably, it shrunk on its lower and left sides, which rendered its shape heart-like. The FDS reshaped the parent vessel, transforming it into a circular artery with the aneurysm neck being now clearly visible (instead of the vessel traversing the aneurysm). The next rotational angiographic images stem from the hours after rupture, showing a small blood leakage at the tip of the aneurysm, as shown in \autoref{fig:stenosis2}. The rupture site is adjacent to the lateral ventricle\footnote{Brain ventricles: A communicating network of cavities filled with cerebrospinal fluid.}, which permits extensive movement of the aneurysm at this site.

\subsubsection*{Movement}
The displacement of the aneurysm wall induced by the cardiac pulse could only be seen after rupture. The aneurysm movement resembled an inflating and deflating balloon, with no noticeable displacement at the neck and a progressively bigger movement towards the tip. The quantitative displacement was however difficult to measure due to the rotating video angiography (see the left of \autoref{fig:wall_movement} for reference).

During the segmentation work, it was seen that the post-rupture images were not as nitid as the preoperative ones, particularly due to the absence of contrast ink in some essential vessels. This can be seen in \autoref{fig:stenosis2}, where the MCA is nearly hollow and only the marker wires indicate the location and size of the vessel. Inspection of the videos taken during injection showed a remarkable response of the aneurysm to the rise in pressure caused by the ink bolus being flushed through the system. The latter is injected following medical protocol at a rate of 3\,mL/s (60\% of the mean flow rate of the ICA) for 7\,s. At systole, the ink penetrates the aneurysm without visible reflux of fluids leaving the bulge. Right after systole, the aneurysm deflates, flushing out blood that turns the MCA transparent (see the right of \autoref{fig:wall_movement}). Despite being an effect only seen during the injection and therefore under increased pressure, this underscores the potential volume variation of the aneurysm and its pulsatility. When the ink injection is stopped, the reflux of the aneurysm is no longer visible, indicating a different type of fluid dynamics at play.

\subsection{Simulations}\label{chap5:sec2}
The medical images have revealed important information about the case including the topology, morphology and some of the dynamics. This informations is now enriched by hemodynamics simulations, which will help to understand flow structures, the pressure evolution and wall shearing in the aneurysm. These factors are potentially decisive in the remodelling and rupture dynamics as already discussed in previous publications on similar aneurysms\,\cite{Cebral2011, Hirato2023, Li2019}.

\subsubsection*{CFD setup}
For the CFD study, two 3D angiography images are available, taken before the stent deployment and after rupture. Both DSA images were segmented following the guidelines described in the previous chapter and meshed with a mesh size of $h$\,=\,0.25\,mm. Due to the increased flow velocities at the stenosis, the preoperative case was run implicitly with a timestep of $\Delta t$\,=\,0.5\,ms. The convection-related instabilities at peak systole that motivate this strategy were not observed in the stented case, where semi-implicit time stepping was sufficient.

The inflow conditions were imposed through parabolic profiles according to averaged waveforms for people in the same age group as the patient\,\cite{Tanaka2006}. We assumed the inflow conditions to remain the same pre- and postoperatively\,\cite{Cebral2011}, but we acknowledge this to be a potential source of bias as already discussed by Cebral \etal\,\cite{Cebral2011}. Outlets were modelled using linear resistances as described in Chapter 3. The targeted flow was chosen to depend on the cube of the MCA and ACA diameters, meeting Murray's Law. Blood rheology was imposed through the modified Casson model presented in the second chapter. The hematocrit was measured at every visit resulting in an average of 34.1\% that was used in all computations.

\subsubsection*{FSI setup}
Since the aneurysm demonstrated clear pulsatility, we investigated the implications of considering rigid arterial walls by comparing our results to fully coupled FSI simulations of the case. These were carried out in cooperation with Aurèle Goetz, another PhD candidate involved in the project.
The fluid dynamics are solved as described in previous chapters, with the nuance that an Arbitrary Lagrangian-Eulerian (ALE) description is employed. The mesh is adapted to follow the wall motions by solving a diffusion equation as done in the work of\,\cite{HABCHI2013306,Sha20}.
Arterial tissue is modelled as hyperelastic using a Neo-Hookean formulation with a Simo-Taylor volumetric model\,\cite{NeoH, SimoTaylor}.
The solid dynamics equations use a mixed formulation in displacement and pressure, and are also solved with a VMS-type method\,\cite{Aurele}.
The coupling between the two solvers is organized in a partitioned way with an under-relaxed fixed-point algorithm\,\cite{FELIPPA198061}.
Fluid stresses are applied as Neumann conditions on the solid interface, and solid dynamics yield displacement updates for the fluid.
Iterations continue until convergence is achieved, with a tolerance set to $10^{-5}\,$mm on the average solid displacement.
An Aitken $\Delta^2$ scheme is used for momentum acceleration, enhancing computational efficiency and convergence\,\cite{Kuttler, BREUER2012107}.

The aneurysm wall is computed as an outward extrusion of the segmented lumen with a fixed thickness of X\,$\mu$m. The material properties are fitted to obtain a movement in agreement with the observed displacements. For reasons of computational feasibility, the FSI case was meshed with a lower spatial and temporal resolution as shown in \autoref{tab:comp_times}.


\subsubsection*{Computation times}
The simulation jobs have been executed on 64 cores of the previously mentioned computing facility (see Chapter 3). The computation times with the different integration modalities are shown in \autoref{tab:comp_times}. The preoperative case caused a longer computation time due to the 2--5 subiterations per time step required for convergence. The postoperative case computed as a CFD simulation was faster, at approximately half the time per cardiac cycle. The FSI simulation of the ruptured aneurysm took, 50h per cycle, the longest time despite its lower spatial and temporal resolutions. The explicit coupling between the solid and fluid solvers, together with slower convergence rates for the solid solver, are the main reasons for this increase in computation time.

\subsubsection*{Pre-operative hemodynamics}\label{chap5:sec3:subsec1}
The aneurysm hemodynamics before the insertion of the FDS are characterized by a high-velocity jet (1.8\,m/s) at the stenosis entering and rolling through the formation around a horizontal axis (see the first quadrant of \autoref{fig:hemodynamics} and \autoref{fig:hemodynamics2}). It then spreads out over the aneurysm surface, generating a large stable vortex in the bulge. As a consequence, high OSI values form on the ``eyes'' of the vortex, as seen on \autoref{fig:hemodynamics}. The WSS in these regions displays persistently expansive properties with high divergences of the normalized WSS vector field. A pouch at the aneurysm's inner side stands out with high OSI and small TAWSS values, due to its irregular perfusion (see \autoref{fig:hemodynamics}).

\subsubsection*{Post-operative hemodynamics}
In the postoperative state, the FDS induces changes of the parent artery, which is now circular and no longer traverses the aneurysm sack. Consequently, the impinging jet at systole is slowed down to half of its preoperative value (0.8\,m/s). The volumetric flow traversing the stent at this same instant is 38\% of the ICA's flow rate. The weakened inflow no longer holds a stable vortex, but one that travels from near the inflow towards the aneurysm's tip. The OSI experiences an increase on its path, as can be seen on the second quadrant of \autoref{fig:hemodynamics}. The flow structure of the FSI simulation resembles largely the ones from the CFD simulations. The OSI, similarly to reported observations\,\cite{Aurele}, increases moderately when simulating the effect of compliance. Other changes involving flow patterns are seen, but not discussed here due to their minor impacts on the discussion. For more details, we refer to the thesis of Aurèle Goetz.

The Pulsatility Index (PI) in the postoperative state was computed at 1.24, resembling common literature values for giant ICA aneurysms\,\cite{Larrabide2016}. The PI belongs to the rather lower spectrum of values found, which is tied to two main factors. On one side, the impingement angle of the flow and size of the IA permit the formation of large vortices that retain a large proportion of their momentum throughout the cycle. The mean bulge-averaged velocity $\bar{u}$ therefore remains high, and so does also its minimum value. The second factor is the chosen patient waveform. The averaged curve presents two closely followed peaks at systole, instead of one more pronounced one. This leads to a more regular flow pattern and thus a lower PI\@.

\subsubsection*{Pressure changes}
Considered separately due to its alleged importance is the alteration of the bulge pressures between the preoperative and postoperative (ruptured) cases. To that end, we have extracted the pressure along the centerline of the ICA in all simulated cases, including the FSI computation. Figure\,\ref{fig:ctrl_p} shows the pressure drops with a colour-coded inflow volume rate. The pressures have been offset to have zero pressure at diastole in the distal section of the MCAs.

In the CFD simulations, the profiles overall vary almost linearly for the lower flow rates excepting some fluctuations at systole. Notably, the pressure drops in the later ICA sections are remarkably high with respect to the human pressure levels. For comparison, 5\,kPa corresponds to the pressure drop between 80--120\,mmHg. The question of whether these are meaningful values or if there is something erroneous in the modelling remains unaddressed despite being commonly observed\,\cite{Cebral2011, Kizhisseri2023}. After estimating the pressure drop with linear Poiseuille resistances, we found that the obtained results indeed are expected. Most of it is the product of the considerable tapering that the ICA experiences and possibly the fact of not modelling the flow through the smaller vessels and perforators\footnote{Perforator: Small artery penetrating or traversing muscles or bones}. We also confirm through the FSI simulation, that the large capacitance of the aneurysm leads to a reduction of the maximal pressure by 1.5\,kPa (see \autoref{fig:ctrl_p}).

A search in the anatomy of the human brain revealed other possible factors that can be related to the stronger pressure drop in the main arteries. Liebeskind \etal\,\cite{Liebeskind2016} elaborates on the special mechanisms that the brain has to regulate its blood flow. The brain arterioles lack capillary sphincters that are elsewhere strong pressure regulators. This shifts the task towards upstream arteries, which need to adapt seamlessly to changing blood flow conditions, \eg\,during physical exercise or sleep. For this purpose, brain arteries have a well-developed layer of smooth muscle cells that can widen or narrow down the vessels, thereby adjusting the flow resistance\,\cite{Liebeskind2016}. The extent of their capabilities is showcased during vasospasm, a critical form of autoregulation that can onset in during haemorrhages. There, the entire ICA may be shut by the medial contraction\,\cite{Branko2014}, posing life-threatening conditions. It could thus be possible that the brain arteries have a higher resistance to flow than other arteries, as part of their ``standby'' autoregulation capabilities. The writer underscores that this is merely a hypothesis and needs to be further investigated.

Returning to the matter at hand, the pressures over the cardiac cycle show mostly a linear relationship with the flow rate, excepting the FSI simulation, where a dephase of pressure and inflow is apparent. For a clearer representation, consider the centerline pressure during systole alone displayed in \autoref{fig:ctrl_p_systole}. There, we have equalized the pressures at the inflow according to the downstream autoregulation hypothesis of Cebral \etal\,\cite{Cebral2011}. In the cavernous sections, pressure drops minimally until halfway into the clinoidal section. There, the tapering of the ICA begins, leading to a fall of 200\,Pa. At the aneurysm, the pre-treatment case sees a flat pressure profile, whereas the post-treatment case continues its nearly linear decay. At the distal end of the aneurysm, the tables turn, causing a sharp drop in the preoperative pressure of nearly 26\,mmHg due to the distal stenosis and the sudden contraction of the flow. The postoperative FSI case displays an identical behaviour to its rigid counterpart until reaching the aneurysm. At that point, a surplus of flow enters the bulge causing it to inflate. Consequently, the resistance in the vessel diminishes yielding a pressure plateau.

\subsection{Discussion}\label{chap5:sec4}
With the observed hemodynamics and the patient case description, we can attempt to relate the rupture to the proposed theories from the literature. By doing so, we can at least see if the present case fits into the general picture and if so, support one of the claims with this one case. The proposed theories relie on pressure-induced\,\cite{Cebral2011}, haemorrhage-induced \,\cite{Kulcsar2011} and lastly flow structure-induced rupture\,\cite{Li2019}.


\subsubsection*{Pressure induced rupture}
In a widely known computational study in the year 2010, Cebral \etal\,\cite{Cebral2011} analyzed the hemodynamics of 7 FDS-treated intracranial aneurysms located at the upper ICA sections. Of those, 3 ruptured within a week of the operation, while the remaining 4 occluded during the following months. The healed aneurysms were all middle-sized excepting one giant aneurysm, whereas all ruptured belonged to the latter category. In the study, the cases were computed in preoperative and post-rupture states following similar steps as here. The pressures were levelled at the inlets of all simulations to be normotensive. They observed that under those conditions two of the three cases resulted in a pressure increase in the IA. The deployment of the FDS had opened narrow passages of the parent artery located upstream of the lesion, reducing the resistance of the flow and thus carrying higher pressures farther. In the other ruptured case, the stenosis was located past the aneurysm and thus no noticeable alteration of the pressure was reported.

While this hypothesis might be a lead in some cases\,\cite{Hirato2023}, it seems overall unsuited here. Firstly, the pressure decreases post-treatment due to the location of the stenosis, similar to other works on this subject\,\cite{Li2019}. In that sense, this aneurysm resembles the giant one that did not rupture in their study. Furthermore, we have observed a strong pulsatility of the aneurysm, which translates into a capacitance that spreads out the pressure waveform. Inevitably, the wall movement introduces further mean pressure losses due to dissipation of energy into the surrounding tissue. Lastly, the timescale of the present rupture exceeds those of Cebral \etal\,\cite{Cebral2011} by months, pointing towards other long-term remodelling phenomena.


\subsubsection*{Thrombus mediated rupture}
Just months after the previous publication, Kulscar \etal\,\cite{Kulcsar2011} proposed a different mechanism for the delayed rupture of aneurysms. In their study, 13 patients with ruptured intracranial aneurysms were studied post-mortem through imaging and autopsies. The patients harboured giant-saccular and fusiform aneurysms that were treated with FDS, and in one case with stent-assisted coiling. 4 out of the 13 patients reported strong headaches prior to the rupture event, similar to our case. The ruptures were split into two categories, early ($n$\,=\,10) and late ($n$\,=\,3), with the latter taking place at least 3 months after the intervention. The latest instance of a rupture took place after 5 months, which falls short of the 8 months of our case. There, however, double anti-platelet therapy was reduced to single after three months, whereas here the patient was kept on the same medication during the whole time.

One common feature among all cases was the high degree of thrombosis in the aneurysm. Interestingly, thrombosis is generally considered as the treatment goal of FD and thus a positively perceived event. The two dissected aneurysms displayed a large degree of autolysis\footnote{Autolysis: Self-digestion of tissue.} and subsequent necrosis of the tissue. This was expressed in a strong mural thinning and the loss of smooth muscle cells and fibrous tissue. Based on these observations, the authors suggest that thrombosis might have disturbed the balance of the aneurysm wall, leading to a sped-up rupture. The size and aspect ratios ($>$\,1.6) played in thus far a role as that the thrombus forming more quickly within these aneurysms.

In the present giant aneurysm no thrombus was observed one week before the rupture. However, what would later become the rupture zone showed for the first time a persistent stagnation of flow in CT scans. We cannot tell whether in the timespan of a week this hyperdensity can spark autolysis or not. It is nevertheless promising to consider this factor as a possible suspect. In Kulcsar \etal\,\cite{Kulcsar2011}, they report one particular case in the late rupture group that after 3 months showed full patency of the aneurysm with no other apparent changes. 20 days after switching to single anti-platelet therapy the aneurysm ruptured and was largely thrombosed. If autolysis was the process leading to rupture in that case, it is clear that only little time is needed to degrade the wall to the point of rupture.

\subsubsection*{Flow structure-mediated rupture}
The last proposed theory that will be discussed is presented by Li \etal\cite{Li2019}. In their work, 10 patients with delayed aneurysm rupture were compared to 20 control patients, whose aneurysms remained stable. Simulations of the hemodynamics were carried out for both groups, including the simulations of the stents and porosity modelling of coils, when present. A large number of flow indicators, including TAWSS and OSI, were compared. Among the indicators, there was one categorical property related to flow stability. The latter was described as, ``flow patterns that persist (do not move or change) during the cardiac cycle.'' according to a prior work from Cebral \etal\,\cite{Cebral_Mut_Weir_Putman_2010}. When a stable flow was maintained after stenting, the aneurysms were less likely to have ruptured. The authors proposed that the structure of the flow could likewise impact the thrombus formation. By doing so, their theory could be related to the previously presented thrombus-mediated rupture, although evidence was not provided.

In regards to our case, we can confirm to have transitioned from a ``stable'' vortex preoperatively to a more irregular flow postoperatively (shown in \autoref{fig:hemodynamics2}). This transition can also be observed qualitatively in Cebral's paper\,\cite{Cebral2011}. There is however a certain degree of vagueness in the determination of stability that hovers menacingly over the interpretation. Here, newer flow structure identifiers introduced on the analysis of the topological skeleton\,\cite{Morbiducci2020} could provide a better quantifiable measure of the flow stability. Although first efforts are being made to apply the topological skeleton to intracranial aneurysms\cite{Mazzi2020}, clear guidelines for analysis are not yet available.

In the discussion regarding ``stability'', we have observed the ``unstable'' flow to be a result of the change of shape of the vasculature and not to result from the embedded stent. Since the work of Li \etal\,\cite{Li2019}, seems to consider only the postoperative state, it is difficult to compare the results to our case. Likewise, the timescales of the ruptures are not provided, making a relationship with the present case somewhat impossible. A final remark from the studied publication is formulated in regard to the addition of coils. Both experimentally and clinically, it has not been proven that adding coils reduces the delayed rupture risk\,\cite{Li2019, Hirato2023}.

\subsubsection*{Limitations}
In the simulation of the case, no patient-specific inflowrate was available. To test the sensitivity, the preoperative case was simulated using flowrates scaled by $\pm$25\%. Although the flow patterns remained similar, we acknowledge that the nature of the waveform can impact the oscillatory components of the flow and needs to be tested further. Due to the poor quality of the postoperative images at the outlets, segmenting them was difficult and could feed back into the outflow boundary conditions. Lastly, it is important to point out that the mesh resolution at the stent was chosen following suggestions from literature\,\cite{Janiga2015,Larrabide2016} instead of convergence studies. This should be included in further work, to safely confirm the parameter choices. Furthermore, it would be interesting to study giant aneurysms using minimally dissipative solvers, such as the ones proposed by Valen-Sendstad\,\cite{Valen-Sendstad2014}, to assess solver sensitivity.


\begin{tcolorbox}[conclusions]

	After conducting a thorough analysis of the flow dynamics of the present giant aneurysm, we contrasted the observations with hypotheses on the rupture pathways found in literature. We have confirmed that under the same modelling assumptions as in the pressure-mediated rupture, no pressure increase could be observed. Thrombus-induced autolysis cannot be excluded, but certainly requires more supporting imaging to be addressed in depth. Methodically, we have seen that FSI can play a crucial role in these cases, where uncertainties around the wall constitution can be overcome through imaging data. The alteration of the pressure dynamics particularly can have a strong impact on the conclusions and should be addressed when observed. Further, we point out some shortcommings of literature in this field, particularly the use of clearly defined indicators for (un-) ``stable'' flows and also the modelling of thrombus formation, to obtain a better grasp of the temporal progression of the wall weakening. With these careful considerations, researchers might ultimately provide helpful indications to clinicians, who so far, have to relie mostly on intuition when dealing with these intrincate cases.
\end{tcolorbox}



\section{Blister Aneurysms}
A further application to flow diverter stents is the research around blister aneurysms, also known as Blood Blister-like Aneurysms (BBAs). These formations are uncommon, highly dangerous and poorly understood\,\cite{Meling2017, Peschillo2016}. In the following section, we will provide a brief introduction to the specific properties of these aneurysms and present two patient cases that have been followed for some months at the cooperating medical institution. The aim of the study is to provide a hemodynamic analysis of the aneurysms and to discuss the potential implications of the flow diverter stent on the aneurysm's progression.


\subsection{Pathology}
Blister aneurysms are rare but distinct cerebral vascular lesions. They constitute less than 2\% of all intracranial aneurysms and are typically found on internal carotid artery, although can also be found in other locations such as the anterior communicating artery or basilar trunk\,\cite{Nasra2021, Papasilekas2020}. Blister aneurysms are usually small, shallow, broad-based, or semi-fusiform protrusions on the arterial wall. The aneurysm wall is markedly weak, predisposing these lesions to rapid growth, rerupture, and catastrophic consequences. They are thought to result from a tear in the arterial inner wall, leading to an intramural haemorrhage and a protrusion covered only by the adventitial layer. Some studies have shown signs of dissection, such as a double lumen or arterial narrowing. An alternative theory suggests that blister aneurysms are pseudoaneurysms, essentially representing a focal defect in the arterial wall covered by an organized blood clot\,\cite{Papasilekas2020}. The exact aetiology of blister aneurysms remains unclear although hight fluid stresses are thought to be implicated in their formation\,\cite{Gonzalez2014,  Song_Oh_Kim_Chung_Lim_Kim_Shin_2016,Nasra2021}.

Most blister aneurysms present with subarachnoid haemorrhage (95.6\%\,\cite{Nasra2021}), diagnosed initially via brain CT scans. Due to their shape and fragility only a few treatment options are viable in both surgical and endovascular fields. The choice of method is conditioned frequently by the environment and accessibility to the lesion. In absolute terms, endovascular approaches lead in 85\% of cases to a functional neurologic outcome compared to 72\% from surgical interventions\,\cite{Nasra2021}. The authors note however that the success is also conditioned by the severity of the case, which is often more elevated in surgery candidates. An improving trend when transitioning to endovascular approaches is also reported by others\,\cite{Gonzalez2014, Song_Oh_Kim_Chung_Lim_Kim_Shin_2016, Peschillo2016}, in particular for the recently introduced flow diverters. Independent studies\,\cite{Mokin2018,Zhu2018} have applied flow diverters to cover the ruptured BBAs obtaining success rates between 90 and 97\% when using a single device. Using overlapping stents and flow diverters lead to a reduced successrate of only 62\%, caused by a higher morbidity\,\cite{Zhu2018}. Occlusion rates however were similar.

This leaves room for a hypothesis worth studying in an attempt to improve patient treatment. The use of overlapping flow diverters increases the metal cover and the exclusion of the aneurysms from flow. Despite that, the occlusion rates have been observed to be similar. If we assume the increased morbidity to be a consequence of thromboembolic events sparked by a higher quantity of metal in the artery, saving material could decrease the risks for patients without impacting too much the occlusion rates\,\cite{Ji2017}. The side-wall nature of BBAs\,\cite{Papasilekas2020} promotes the flow reduction with FDS, since shear driven circulations are very effectively cut by these devices\,\cite{Augsburger2011}. For that reason, we will study the flow in two patient-specific cases on preoperative, single and double stented scenarios. We hope that a description of the hemodynamics in these, so far, successfully treated ruptured cases serves as a motivation for the community to conduct larger and statistically meaningful studies on the subject.

\subsection{Discussion}


\subsubsection*{Limitations}
Similarly to the previous study, we point out that inflow conditions were in this case generalized and can impact the results. In the case of patient 1, we modelled the PCom as a regular outflow, while a well-developed CoW was seen on MR scans. A better choice in this case would be conducting a study on the CoW to extract local flow rates and impose them as boundary conditions, as discussed in chapter 3. In both cases, we utilized the postoperative images for segmentation. In the preoperative ones, there were more perforators clearly visible (ophthalmic and anterior choroidal arteries) that could be added or modelled later for increased fidelity.
