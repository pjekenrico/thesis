\chapter{Patient Studies}\minitoc\newpage\label{chap.5}
In the previous chapters, we have dealt with the numerical principles behind CFD computations using finite elements. Then we have applied and adapted the framework to the brain arterial system, including a sensitivity study to the modelling over different extends of the vasculature. Next we studied Flow-Diverter Stents (FDS) and how to model them based on geometrical constraints. With a thorough validation in experimental and in-vivo cases, we now move towards putting the framework to use. For that we will study 3 patient cases displaying two variations of intracranial aneurysms each posing very unique challenges to the clinicians. These showcase some situations in which FDS treatment is considered the most promising tool, even though fatality rates are still high. First we will deal with the giant aneurysm case that has been subject of research in the previous chapter. The case will be thoroughly discussed considering medical and anatomical aspects before diving into the simulation of pre- nand postoperative hemodynamics. The second application case involves blister-like aneurysms, formations barely seen on CT imaging characterized by critically thin and fragile walls. Both disease types are poorly understood and require further research to improve the prospective treatment to those in need of it. With the run simulations, we put on display the framework, but most imporantly, we provide hemodynamics results on rare aneurysms and relate them to existing literature.

\section{Delayed rupture of giant aneurysms}\label{chap5:sec1}
Giant aneurysms bear several added difficulties compared to their smaller, saccular counterparts. Due to their size, they are often symptomatic, causing cerebral palsies, headaches and other neurological disturbances. Therefore, treating them is often a necessity, albeit operations are more complex and are more prone to complications\,\cite{Rouchaud2016}. Most giant aneurysms ($>$\,25\,mm) contain a sizable portion of the parent vessel and lack a well defined neck. As a consequence, clipping them surgically is often inviable. Even the deployment of coils is rarely feasible, since they require a sufficient hold to remain firmly in place. Furthermore, coils suffer from an increased risk of recanalization due to accentuated material fatigue and material compaction in these cases\,\cite{Dandapat2021}. Flow-diverter stents, although sometimes difficult to deploy in giant aneurysms, manage to avoid the aforementioned issues. Despite their overall good performance\,\cite{Kulcsar2011}, giant aneurysms still pose a non-negligible risk of delayed rupture\,\cite{Kulcsar2011,Li2019,Rouchaud2016}. Since the first observed cases in the 2010's decade, research teams have proposed several contributing factors and registered their observations. In the following, we will conduct a detailed analysis of the patient case presented to our cooperating hospital, who experienced this dramatic outcome.


\subsection{Patient medical record}
The patient is a woman born in January 1963, with no history of smoking, hypertension, nor family history of intracranial aneurysms. Vertigo along with anxiety and a strange sensation lead her to seek consultation of a neurologist. After a first consultation, she was appointed to do an MRI examination of the crane, revealing a giant intracranial aneurysm compressing the left temporal lobe\footnote{Temporal lobes: Regions of the brain situated on both halves above the ears.}. Her neurologist referred her to a neuro-interventionalist in the same month for further evaluation. A cerebral arteriography confirmed the existance of a giant aneurysm in the left supra-clinoid ICA, between the ophtalmic and communicating segments. The englobing of the parent artery by the aneurysm caused a delayed opacification of the left middle cerebral artery. The small anterior communicating artery was visible during compression of the left ICA.

Based on the presence of symptoms and a menace of rupture, endovascular treatment was proposed to the patient, to which she agreed during the following medical consultation. One week before the procedure, she began taking aspirin (75 mg/day) and prasugrel\footnote{Prasugrel: Platelet aggregation inhibitor medication.} (10 mg/day) to reduce the risk of blood clots. The procedure started with a balloon test occlusion, where a balloon was temporarily inflated in the artery to block blood flow. A guiding catheter was placed in both the right and left internal carotid arteries. A Scepter XC 4--11 balloon was positioned in the left ICA upstream of the aneurysm and inflated to stop blood flow. Contrast injection via the right catheter showed a delay of over one second in the opacification of the left cerebral veins compared to the right, indicating that the left carotid artery could not be safely occluded. Therefore, preserving and reconstructing the left ICA with a flow-diverting stent was necessary. A $3$\,mm FRED Jr flow-diverter was deployed from the left MCA (M1 segment) to the left ICA upstream of the aneurysm, immediately causing contrast stasis in the aneurysm sac. Upon waking up, the patient showed no neurological deficits and continued antiplatelet therapy. At her follow-up 1.5 months later, her clinical exam returned normal results. Six months after the procedure, a follow-up cerebral arteriography showed a patent stent\footnote{Patent (stent): Maintaining the openness and integrity of the vessel or structure where it has been placed.} with unchanged opacification of the aneurysm sac. Continued dual antiplatelet therapy was advised, with a repeat arteriography scheduled for ten months post-procedure.

Eighth months after the intervention, the patient reported a sudden headache. A brain CT scan revealed spontaneous hyperdensity in the aneurysm, suggesting thrombosis. A week later, she suffered a sudden coma, with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 4, indicating severe brain injury. A CT scan revealed subarachnoid hemorrhage, intraventricular hemorrhage, and a left temporal hematoma\footnote{Hematoma: A solid swelling of clotted blood within the tissues.}. Cerebral arteriography showed persistent aneurysm opacification, a patent stent, and active contrast leakage from the aneurysm sac's postero-inferior part. A microcatheter was placed in the aneurysm sac through the stent mesh, and the sac was occluded with coils, halting the bleeding and completely occluding the aneurysm. Sadly, the patient passed away five days after this second intervention.

\subsection{Artery topography}\label{chap5:sec1:subsec2}
\subsubsection*{Circle of Willis}
An MRA scan confirmed the present Circle of Willis (CoW) to be incomplete, missing the posterior communication ipsilateral to the aneurysm. The contralateral one, which appears to have a fetal nature, contributes most of the blood to the left posterior circulation. The anterior communication, although present, has a small size which was confirmed during a clamp test. According to these features, the structure of the CoW is categorized as a type PCP, a variant found in approximately 7\% of the population\,\cite{Hindenes2023}. Although the CoW configuration carries a 2.3 fold risk of aneurysm formation, it is not clear how it affects the rupture risk.

The aneurysm on the communicating (C7) segment of the supra-clinoidal ICA, 4\,mm before its terminal bifurcation. The Anterior Choroidal Artery (AChA) emanates from it, however its flow is barely noticeable on DSA scans, as seen on the left of \autoref{fig:stenosis}.

\subsubsection*{Tampering}
An important factor considering this case is the presence of Tampering (T) and vessel deformation around the aneurysm. The cavernous ICA has a diameter of 4.16\,mm falling in line with reported values\,\cite{Baz2021}. It experiences a continuous tampering over the ophtalmic (C6) and communicating (C7) segments leading to a nearly elliptical vessel at the proximal aneurysm neck. This atypical cross-section translates into a 32\% tampering with respect to the diameter of the cavernous ICA (see \autoref{tab:pat_geo}). Past the aneurysm, the terminal section narrows down to 2.15\,mm, equivalently to a 48\% stenosis. This value clearly falls outside the standart deviation of the recorded cases by Baz \etal\,\cite{Baz2021}.

After deployment, the proximal vessel sections remain largely unchanged. The clinoidal ICA however shrinks with diameters of 3.33\,mm, which is a 20\% tampering with respect to the cavernous ICA. The sections at the aneurysm have become nearly identical in diameter, attaining commonly found sizes. Although it is not verifyable from the imaging, these effects are likely to be caused by the mechanical forces exerted by the stent and the progressive remodelling of the artery as well.

\subsection{Aneurysm}\label{chap5:sec1:subsec3}

\subsubsection*{Morphology}
The aneurysm contained its parent vessel prior to the operation. It has a nearly round shape with a diameter of appoximately 25\,mm and a volume of 7170\,mm$^3$. In the course of the eight months after the intervention, the aneurysm underwent changes, similarly to the vasculature. Most notably, it shrunk on its lower and left sides, which rendered its shape heart-like. The FDS reshaped the parent vessel, transforming it into a circular artery with the aneurysm neck being now clearly visible (instead of the vessel traversing the aneurysm). The next rotational angiographic images stem from the hours after rupture, showing a small blood leakage at the tip of the aneurysm, as shown in \autoref{fig:stenosis2}. The rupture site is adjacent to the third ventricle, which permits an extensive movement of the aneurysm at this site.

\subsubsection*{Movement}
The displacement of the aneurysm wall induced by the cardiac pulse, could only clearly be seen after rupture. The whole aneurysm movement can be described similarly to an inflating and deflating balloon, with no noticeable displacement at the neck and a progressively bigger movement towards the tip. During systole, the rupture zone moved by an estimated 10\% of the aneurysm length. The quantitative displacement was however difficult to measure due to the rotating video angiography (see the left of \autoref{fig:wall_movement} for reference), so we propose the value rather as an order of magnitude.

During the segmentation work, it was seen that the post-rupture images were not as nitid as the preoperative ones, particularly due to the absence of saturated contrast agent in some vessels that are essential. This can be seen in \autoref{fig:stenosis2}, where the MCA is nearly hollow and only the marker wires nidicate the location and size of the vessel. Inspection of the videos taken during injection showed a remarkable response of the aneurysm to the rise in pressure caused by the ink bolus being flushed through the system. The latter is injected following medical protocol at a rate of 3\,mL/s (60\% of the mean flow rate of the ICA) for 7\,s. At systole, ink penetrates the aneurysm without visible reflux of fluids leaving the bulge. Right after systole, the aneurysm deflates, flushing out blood that turns the MCA transparent (see the right of \autoref{fig:wall_movement}). Despite being an effect only seen during the injection and therefore under increased pressure, this underscores the potential volume variation of the aneurysm and its pulsatility. When the ink injection is stopped, the reflux of the aneurysm is no longer visible, indicating a different type of fluid dynamics at play.

\subsection{Simulations}\label{chap5:sec2}
The medical images have revealed important information about the case including the topology, morphology and some of the dynamics. These informations are now enriched by hemodynamics simulations, which will help to understand flow structures, the pressure evolution and wall shearing in the aneurysm. These factors are potentially decisive in the remodelling and rupture dynamics as already discussed in previous publications on similar aneurysms\,\cite{Cebral2011, Hirato2023, Li2019}.

\subsubsection*{CFD setup}
For the CFD study, there are two 3D angiography images available, taken before the stent deployment and after rupture. Both DSA images were segmented following the guidelines described in the previous chapter and meshed with a mesh size of $h$\,=\,0.25\,mm. Due to the increased flow velocities at the stenosis, the preoperative case was run implicitly with a timestep of $\Delta t$\,=\,0.5\,ms. The convection-related instabilities at peak systole that motivate this strategy were not observed in the stented case, where semi-implicit time stepping was sufficient.

The inflow conditions were imposed through parabolic profiles according to averaged waveforms for people in the same age group as the patient\,\cite{Tanaka2006}. We assumed the inflow conditions to remain the same pre- and postoperatively\,\cite{Cebral2011}, but we acknowledge this to be a potential source of bias as already discussed by Cebral \etal\,\cite{Cebral2011}. Outlets were modelled using linear resistances as described in Chapter 3. The targeted flow was chosen to depend on the cube of the MCA and ACA diameters, meeting Murray's Law.

The aneurysm hemodynamics before the insertion of the FDS are characterized by a high velocity jet (1800\,mm/s) at the stenosis entering and rolling through the formation around a horizontal axis (see the first quadrant of \autoref{fig:hemodynamics} and \autoref{fig:hemodynamics2}). It then spreads out over the aneurysm surface, generating a large stable vortex in the bulge. As a consequence, high OSI values form on the ``eyes'' of the vortex, as seen on \autoref{fig:hemodynamics}. The WSS in these regions displays persistently an expansive properties with high divergences of the normalized WSS vectorfield. A pouch at the aneurysm's inner side stands out with high OSI and small TAWSS values, due to its irregular perfusion (see \autoref{fig:hemodynamics}).

\subsubsection*{Post-operative hemodynamics}
In the postoperative state, the FDS induces remodelling of the parent artery, which is now circular and does no longer traverse the aneurysm sack. Consequenly, the impinging jet at systole is slowed down to half of its preoperative value (800\,mm/s). The volumetric flow traversing the stent at this same instant is 38\% of the ICA's flowrate. The weakened inflow no longer holds a stable vortex, but one that travels from near the inflow towards the aneurysm's tip. The OSI experiences an increase on its path, as can be seen on the second quadrant of \autoref{fig:hemodynamics}.

The pulsatility index in the postoperative state was computed at 1.24, resembling common literature values for giant ICA aneurysms\,\cite{Larrabide2016}. The PI belongs to the rather lower spectrum of values found, which is tied to two main factors. On one side, the impingement angle of the flow and size of the IA permit the formation of large vortices that retain a large proportion of their momentum throughout the cycle. The mean bulge-averaged velocity $\bar{u}$ therefore remains high, and so does also its minimum value. The second factor is the chosen patient waveform. The averaged curve presents two closely followed peaks at systole, instead of one more pronounced one. This leads to a more regular flow pattern and thus a lower PI value.

The profiles overall vary almost linearly for the lower flowrates, but contain some fluctuations at systole. Notably, the pressure drops in the later ICA sections are remarkably high with respect to the human pressure levels. The values around 5\,kPa alone represent the range between 80--120\,mmHg, posing an interesting question not discussed in many other publications\,\cite{Cebral2011, Kizhisseri2023}. After estimating the pressure drop with linear resistances given by Poiseuille's law, we found that the obtained pressure drop is indeed the expected. Most of it is the product of the considerable tampering that the ICA experiences and possibly the fact of not modelling the flow through the smaller vessels and perforators. A search in the anatomy of the human brain revealed other possible factors that can be related to the stronger pressure drop in the main arteries. Liebeskind \etal\cite{Liebeskind2016} elaborates on the special mechanisms that the brain has to regulate its blood flow. The brain arterioles lack capillary sphincters that are elsewhere strong pressure regulators. This shifts the task towards upstream arteries, which need to adapt seamlessly to changing blood flow conditions, \eg\,during physical execise or sleep. For this purpose, brain arteries have a well-developed layer of smooth muscle cells that can widen or narrow down the vessels, thereby adjusting the flow resistance\,\cite{Liebeskind2016}. The extend of their capabilities is showcased during vasospasm, a critical form of autoregulation that can onset in during hemorrhages. There, the entire ICA may be shut by the medial contraction\,\cite{Branko2014}, posing life-threatening conditions. It could thus be possible that the brain arteries have a higher resistance to flow than other arteries, as part of their ``standby'' autoregulation capabilities. The writer underscores that this is merely a hypothesis and needs to be further investigated.

\subsubsection*{Pressure induced rupture}
In a widely known computational study in the year 2010, Cebral \etal\cite{Cebral2011} analyzed the hemodynamics of 7 FDS-treated intracranial aneurysms located at the upper ICA sections. Of those, 3 ruptured withing a week of the operation, while the remaining 4 occluded during the following months. The healed aneurysms were all middle sized excepting one giant aneurysm, whereas all ruptured belonged to the latter category. In the study, the cases were computed in preoperative and post-rupture state following similar steps as here. The pressures were leveled at the inlets of all simulations to be normotensive. They observed that under those conditions two of the three cases resulted in a pressure increase in the IA. The deployment of the FDS had opened narrow passages of the parent artery located upstream of the lesion, reducing the resistance of the flow and thus carrying higher pressures farther. In the other ruptured case, the stenosis was located past the aneurysm and thus no noticeable alteration of the pressure was reported.

While this hypothesis might be a lead in some cases\,\cite{Hirato2023}, it seems overall unsuited here. Firstly, the pressure in our case decreases post-treatment due to the location of the stenosis, similarly to other works on this subject\,\cite{Li2019}. In that sense, this aneurysm resembles the giant one that did not rupture in their study. Furthermore, we have observed a strong pulsatility of the aneurysm, which translates into a capacitance that spreads out the pressure waveform. Inevitably, the wall movement introduces further mean pressure losses due to dissipation of energy into the surrounding tissue. Lastly, the timescale of the present rupture exceeds those of Cebral \etal\cite{Cebral2011} by months, pointing towards other long term remodelling phenomena.

The considerations surrounding the pressure increase or decrease at the aneurysm depend directly on the patient and on the overall tampering of the ICA. \subsubsection*{Flow structure mediated rupture}
The last proposed theory that will be discussed is presented by Li \etal\cite{Li2019}. In their work, 10 patients with delayed aneurysm rupture were compared to 20 control patients, whose aneurysms remained stable. Simulations of the hemodynamics were carried out for both groups, including the simulations of the stents and porosity modelling of coils, when present. A large number of flow indicators, including TAWSS and OSI, were compared. Among the indicators, there was one categorical property related to the flow stability. The latter was described as, ``flow patterns that persist (do not move or change) during the cardiac cycle.'' according to a prior work from Cebral \etal\,\cite{Cebral_Mut_Weir_Putman_2010}. When a stable flow was maintained after stenting, the aneurysms were less likely to have ruptured.

In regards to our case, we can confirm to have transitioned from a ``stable'' vortex preoperatively to a more irregular flow postoperatively (shown in \autoref{fig:hemodynamics2}). This transition can also be qualitatively seen on the other publications related to delayed aneurysm rupture after FDS treatment. There is however a certain degree of vagueness in the determination of stability that hovers menacingly over the interpretation. Here, newer flow structure identifiers introduced on the analysis of the topological skeleton\,\cite{Morbiducci2020} could provide a better quantifyable measure of the flow stability. Although first efforts are being done to apply the topological skeleton to intracranial aneurysms\cite{Mazzi2020}, clear guidelines for analysis are not yet available.

In the discussion regarding ``stability'', we have observed the ``unstable'' flow to be a result of the change of shape of the vasculature and not to result from the embedded stent. Since the work of Li \etal\,\cite{Li2019}, seems to consider only the postoperative state, it is difficult to compare the results to our case. Likewise, the timescales of the ruptures are missing for the study, making a relationship with the present case somewhat impossible. A final remark from the studied publication is formulated in regards to the addition of coils. Both experimentally and clinically, it has not been proven that adding coils reduces the delayed rupture risk\,\cite{Li2019, Hirato2023}.

\section{Blister Aneurysms}
A further application to flow diverter stents is the research around blister aneurysms, also known as Blood Blister-like Aneurysms (BBAs). These formations are uncommon, highly dangerous and poorly understood. In the following section, we will provide a brief introduction to the specific properties of these aneurysms and present two patient cases that have been followed for some months at the cooperating medical institution. The aim of the study is to provide a hemodynamic analysis of the aneurysms and to discuss the potential implications of the flow diverter stent on the aneurysm's progression.


\subsection{Pathology}
Blister aneurysms are rare but distinct cerebral vascular lesions. They constitute less than 2\% of all intracranial aneurysms and are typically found on internal carotid artery, although can also be found in other locations such as the anterior communicating artery or basilar trunk\,\cite{Papasilekas2020}. Blister aneurysms are usually small, shallow, broad-based, or semi-fusiform protrusions on the arterial wall. The aneurysm wall is markedly weak, predisposing these lesions to rapid growth, rerupture, and catastrophic consequences. They are thought to result from a tear in the arterial inner wall, leading to an intramural hemorrhage and a protrusion covered only by the adventitial layer. Some studies have shown signs of dissection, such as a double lumen or arterial narrowing. An alternative theory suggests that blister aneurysms are pseudoaneurysms, essentially representing a focal defect in the arterial wall covered by an organized blood clot\,\cite{Papasilekas2020}.

Most blister aneurysms present with subarachnoid hemorrhage, diagnosed initially via brain CT scans. Due to their shape and fragility only few treatment options are viable in both surgical and endovascular fields. In a review study by Gonzalez \etal\cite{Gonzalez2014}, the authors list a total of 293 ruptured cases, of which approximately two thirds were treated surgically. The morbidity and mortarily rates were 21 and 17\%, respectively, whereas following a endovascular intervention only resulted in 3 and 11\%. Although it is difficult to extract correlations with the severity of the hemorrhage in each of the cases, endovascular treatment displays a promising trend\,\cite{Song_Oh_Kim_Chung_Lim_Kim_Shin_2016, Gonzalez2014}. Among the different techniques of the latter discipline, stenting and stent-assisted coiling have shown the best results in terms of occlusion rates and complication rates\,\cite{Gonzalez2014}. Due to the smaller sizes of cohorts, it remains difficult to provide statistically relevant percentages for the different techniques.

In most studies on the subject\,\cite{Gonzalez2014, Papasilekas2020, Song_Oh_Kim_Chung_Lim_Kim_Shin_2016}, the exact etiology of blister aneurysms remains unclear and no strong evidence support either of the proposed theories. Extracting the most possible information of the few given cases is therefore crucial for the understanding of the disease and the development of new treatment strategies. We propose a brief study of the hemodynamics of blister aneurysms, based on two patient cases that were treated with a single flow diverter after experiencing a rupture. The aim of this study is to provide an overview of the hemodynamics, which could potentially be responsible for the positive progression of the disease in these two cases.
