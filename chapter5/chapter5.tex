\chapter{Patient-specific analysis with flow-diverter treatment}\minitoc\newpage\label{chap.5}
\begin{tcolorbox}[abstract]

	In the previous chapters, we have developed the numerical framework for VMS in fluid simulations, examined the specifics of brain arterial hemodynamics, and created a tool for modeling Flow-Diverter Stents (FDS) based on geometric constraints. Leveraging the experience and tools acquired, we now apply this framework to three patient cases featuring two variations of intracranial aneurysms, each presenting unique challenges to clinicians. These cases highlight situations where FDS treatment is considered highly promising, despite the still high fatality rates. First, we address the giant aneurysm (>$,25,mm$) previously discussed. This case will be thoroughly analyzed from medical and anatomical perspectives before delving into the simulation of pre- and postoperative hemodynamics. The second case involves blister-like aneurysms, formations that are barely visible on CT imaging and are characterized by critically thin and fragile walls. Both types of aneurysms are poorly understood and necessitate further research to enhance treatment outcomes. Through our simulations, we demonstrate the capabilities of the framework and, more importantly, provide hemodynamic results for these scarcely documented aneurysms, correlating our findings with existing literature.

	Dans les chapitres précédents, nous avons travaillé sur le cadre numérique du VMS dans les simulations de fluides, étudié les spécificités de l'hémodynamique artérielle cérébrale et développé un outil pour modéliser les Stents Flow-Diverter (FDS) sur la base de contraintes géométriques. \section{Delayed Rupture of a Giant Aneurysm}
Giant aneurysms present several added challenges compared to their smaller counterparts. Due to their size, they are often symptomatic, causing cerebral palsies, headaches, and other neurological disturbances. Therefore, treating them is often necessary, though such operations are more complex and prone to complications\,\cite{Rouchaud2016}. Most giant aneurysms involve a significant portion of the parent vessel and lack a well-defined neck. As a result, surgical clipping is often unviable. Even deploying coils is rarely feasible since they require sufficient anchorage to remain firmly in place. Moreover, coils face an increased risk of recanalization due to material fatigue and compaction\,\cite{Dandapat2021}. Stent-assisted coiling has also not been found to improve the likelihood of a positive outcome\,\cite{Hirato2023, Rouchaud2016}.

Flow-diverter stents, although sometimes difficult to deploy in giant aneurysms, circumvent these issues. Despite their overall good performance\,\cite{Kulcsar2011}, giant aneurysms still pose a significant risk of delayed rupture\,\cite{Kulcsar2011, Li2019, Rouchaud2016}. Since the first observed cases in the 2010s, research teams have proposed several contributing factors and documented their observations. In the following section, we will conduct a detailed analysis of a patient case who unfortunately experienced this dramatic outcome.


\subsection{Patient Medical Records}
The patient was a woman born in January 1963, with no history of smoking, hypertension, or family history of intracranial aneurysms. She experienced vertigo, anxiety, and a strange sensation, prompting her to consult a neurologist. Following an initial consultation, she underwent an MRI scan of the brain, which revealed a giant intracranial aneurysm compressing the left temporal lobe\footnote{Temporal lobes: Regions of the brain situated on both halves above the ears.}. Her neurologist referred her to a neuro-interventionalist for further evaluation. A cerebral arteriography confirmed the presence of a giant aneurysm in the left supra-clinoid ICA, between the ophthalmic and communicating segments. The aneurysm's involvement of the parent artery caused delayed opacification of the left middle cerebral artery. The small anterior communicating artery was visible during compression of the left ICA.

Given the symptoms and risk of rupture, endovascular treatment was recommended and accepted by the patient. One week before the procedure, she began taking aspirin (75 mg/day) and prasugrel\footnote{Prasugrel: A platelet aggregation inhibitor medication.} (10 mg/day) to reduce the risk of blood clots. The procedure commenced with a balloon test occlusion, where a balloon was temporarily inflated in the artery to block blood flow. A guiding catheter was placed in the right and left internal carotid arteries. A Scepter XC 4--11 balloon was positioned in the left ICA upstream of the aneurysm and inflated to stop blood flow. Contrast injection via the right catheter showed a delay of over one second in the opacification of the left cerebral veins compared to the right, indicating that the left carotid artery could not be safely occluded. Therefore, preserving and reconstructing the left ICA with a flow-diverting stent was necessary. A $3$,mm FRED Jr flow-diverter was deployed from the left MCA (M1 segment) to the left ICA upstream of the aneurysm, immediately causing contrast stasis in the aneurysm sac. Upon waking up, the patient showed no neurological deficits and continued antiplatelet therapy. At her follow-up 1.5 months later, her clinical exam returned normal results. Six months after the procedure, a follow-up cerebral arteriography showed a patent stent\footnote{Patent (stent): Maintaining the openness and integrity of the vessel or structure where it has been placed.} with unchanged opacification of the aneurysm sac. Continued dual antiplatelet therapy was advised, with a repeat arteriography scheduled for ten months post-procedure.

Eight months after the intervention, the patient reported a sudden headache. A CT scan revealed spontaneous hyperdensity in the aneurysm, suggesting thrombosis. A week later, she suffered a sudden coma, with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 4, indicating severe brain injury. A CT scan revealed subarachnoid hemorrhage, intraventricular hemorrhage, and a left temporal hematoma\footnote{Hematoma: A solid swelling of clotted blood within the tissues.}. Cerebral arteriography showed persistent aneurysm opacification, a patent stent, and active contrast leakage from the posteroinferior part of the aneurysm sac. A microcatheter was placed in the aneurysm sac through the stent mesh, and the sac was occluded with coils, halting the bleeding and completely occluding the aneurysm. Sadly, the patient passed away five days after this second intervention.

\subsection{Arterial Anatomy}
\subsubsection*{Circle of Willis}
An MRA scan confirmed an incomplete Circle of Willis (CoW), missing the posterior communicating artery ipsilateral to the aneurysm. The contralateral posterior communicating artery, which appears to have a fetal configuration, supplies most of the blood to the left posterior circulation. The anterior communicating artery, although present, is small, as confirmed during a clamp test. Based on these features, the CoW structure is categorized as a type PCP, a variant found in approximately 7\% of the population\,\cite{Hindenes2023}. Although this CoW configuration carries a 2.3-fold increased risk of aneurysm formation, its effect on rupture risk remains unclear.

The aneurysm is located on the communicating (C7) segment of the supra-clinoidal ICA, 4,mm before its terminal bifurcation. The anterior choroidal artery (AChA) emanates from the aneurysm, though its flow is barely noticeable on DSA scans, as shown on the left of \autoref{fig:stenosis}.

\subsubsection*{Tapering}
A crucial factor in this case is the presence of tapering (T) and vessel deformation around the aneurysm. The cavernous ICA has a diameter of 4.16,mm, consistent with reported values\,\cite{Baz2021}. It experiences continuous tapering over the ophthalmic (C6) and communicating (C7) segments, leading to a nearly elliptical vessel at the proximal aneurysm neck. This atypical cross-section translates into a 32\% tapering relative to the diameter of the cavernous ICA (see \autoref{tab:pat_geo}). Beyond the aneurysm, the terminal section narrows to 2.15,mm, indicating a 48\% stenosis. This value falls outside the standard deviation of the recorded cases by Baz \textit{et al.}\,\cite{Baz2021}.

After stent deployment, the proximal vessel sections remain largely unchanged. However, the clinoidal ICA shrinks, with diameters of 3.33,mm, representing a 20\% tapering relative to the cavernous ICA. The sections at the aneurysm have become nearly identical in diameter, reaching commonly observed sizes. Although it is not verifiable from imaging, these effects are likely caused by the mechanical forces exerted by the stent and the progressive remodeling of the artery.

\subsection{Aneurysm Properties}

\subsubsection*{Morphology}
Before the operation, the aneurysm enveloped its parent vessel. It had a nearly round shape with a diameter of approximately 25,mm. Post-intervention, the stenosis diminished considerably, and the parent vessel achieved a circular shape. Furthermore, the flow-diverter stent (FDS) retracted the parent vessel from the aneurysm, creating a clearly defined neck instead of the vessel traversing the aneurysm. One week before the rupture, when the patient reported severe headaches, MR images suggested thrombus initiation in the section of the aneurysm facing the MCA.

A week later, the aneurysm ruptured, showing a large thrombosis at the same location. In the rotational DSA images, the aneurysm appeared to have half of its original volume, with missing parts in the suspected thrombosis zone. The rupture occurred at the tip of the aneurysm adjacent to the lateral ventricle\footnote{Brain ventricles: A communicating network of cavities filled with cerebrospinal fluid.}, as indicated in \autoref{fig:stenosis2}.

\subsubsection*{Movement}
The displacement of the aneurysm wall induced by the cardiac pulse became visible only after the rupture. The movement resembled an inflating and deflating balloon, with no noticeable displacement at the neck and progressively more significant movement towards the tip. At the tip, the aneurysm bordered the ventricle, a space filled with cerebrospinal fluid that allows movement. Quantitative displacement was difficult to measure due to the rotating video angiography (see the left of \autoref{fig:wall_movement} for reference).

During the segmentation process, it was observed that the post-rupture images were not as clear as the preoperative ones, particularly due to the absence of contrast dye in some essential vessels. This is evident in \autoref{fig:stenosis2}, where the MCA appears nearly hollow, with only the marker wires indicating the location and size of the vessel. Inspection of the videos taken during injection revealed a remarkable response of the aneurysm to the pressure rise caused by the contrast dye bolus being flushed through the system. The dye is injected following medical protocol at a rate of 3\,mL/s (60\% of the mean flow rate of the ICA) for 7\,s. At systole, the dye penetrates the aneurysm without visible reflux of fluids leaving the bulge. Immediately after systole, the aneurysm deflates flushing out non-saturaed blood and rendering the MCA transparent (see the right of \autoref{fig:wall_movement}). Although this effect is only observed during the injection and under increased pressure, it underscores the potential volume variation of the aneurysm and its pulsatility. When the dye injection stops, the reflux of the aneurysm is no longer visible, indicating different fluid dynamics at play.

\subsection{Simulations}

The medical images have revealed important information about the case including topology, morphology, and some dynamics. This information is now enriched by hemodynamic simulations, which will help understand flow structures, pressure evolution, and wall shear stress in the aneurysm. These factors are potentially decisive in the remodeling and rupture dynamics, as discussed in previous publications on similar aneurysms\,\cite{Cebral2011, Hirato2023, Li2019}.

\subsubsection*{CFD Setup}
For the CFD study, two 3D angiography images are available: one from before the stent deployment and one after the rupture. Both DSA images were segmented following the guidelines described in the previous chapter and meshed with a mesh size of $h$\,=\,0.25\,mm. Due to the increased flow velocities at the stenosis, the preoperative case was run implicitly with a timestep of $\Delta t$\,=\,0.5\,ms. The convection-related instabilities at peak systole that motivate this strategy were not observed in the stented case, where semi-implicit time stepping was sufficient.

The inflow conditions were imposed using parabolic profiles according to averaged waveforms for individuals in the same age group as the patient\,\cite{Tanaka2006}. We assumed the inflow conditions to remain the same pre- and postoperatively\,\cite{Cebral2011}, though we acknowledge this as a potential source of bias, as discussed by Cebral \textit{et al.}\,\cite{Cebral2011}. Outlets were modeled using linear resistances as described in Chapter 3. The targeted flow was chosen to depend on the cube of the MCA and ACA diameters, adhering to Murray's Law. Blood rheology was imposed through the modified Casson model presented in the second chapter. The hematocrit was measured at every visit, resulting in an average of 34.1\%, which was used in all computations.

\subsubsection*{FSI Setup}
Since the aneurysm demonstrated clear pulsatility, we investigated the implications of considering rigid arterial walls by comparing our results to fully coupled FSI simulations of the case. These were carried out in cooperation with Aurèle Goetz, another PhD candidate involved in the project. The fluid dynamics are solved as described in previous chapters, with the nuance that an Arbitrary Lagrangian-Eulerian (ALE) description is employed. The mesh is adapted to follow the wall motions by solving a diffusion equation as done in the work of\,\cite{HABCHI2013306, Sha20}. Arterial tissue is modeled as hyperelastic using a Neo-Hookean formulation with a Simo-Taylor volumetric model\,\cite{NeoH, SimoTaylor}. The solid dynamics equations use a mixed formulation in displacement and pressure and are also solved with a VMS-type method\,\cite{Aurele}. The coupling between the two solvers is organized in a partitioned way with an under-relaxed fixed-point algorithm\,\cite{FELIPPA198061}. Fluid stresses are applied as Neumann conditions on the solid interface, and solid dynamics yield displacement updates for the fluid. Iterations continue until convergence is achieved, with a tolerance set to $10^{-5}$\,mm on the average solid displacement. An Aitken $\Delta^2$ scheme is used for momentum acceleration, enhancing computational efficiency and convergence\,\cite{Kuttler, BREUER2012107}.

The aneurysm wall is computed as an outward extrusion of the segmented lumen. Due to the contact of the aneurysm membrane with surrounding brain tissue over most of the bulge, a rather high thickness of 0.5,mm is employed. The material properties are fitted to obtain movement in agreement with the observed displacements, with greatly diminished stiffness at the dome, where the aneurysm has been observed to pulsate mostly. For reasons of computational feasibility, the FSI case was meshed with a lower spatial and temporal resolution as shown in \autoref{tab:comp_times}.

\subsubsection*{Computation Times}
The simulation jobs were executed on 64 cores of the previously mentioned computing facility (see Chapter 3). The computation times with the different integration modalities are shown in \autoref{tab:comp_times}. The preoperative case required longer computation times due to the 2--5 subiterations per time step needed for convergence. The postoperative case, computed as a CFD simulation, was faster, requiring approximately half the time per cardiac cycle. The FSI simulation of the ruptured aneurysm took the longest time, at 50 hours per cycle, despite its lower spatial and temporal resolutions. The explicit coupling between the solid and fluid solvers, along with slower convergence rates for the solid solver, are the main reasons for this increase in computation time.

\subsubsection*{Pre-operative hemodynamics}\label{chap5:sec3:subsec1}
The aneurysm hemodynamics before the insertion of the FDS are characterized by a high-velocity jet (1.8\,m/s) at the stenosis entering and rolling through the formation around a horizontal axis (see the first quadrant of \autoref{fig:hemodynamics} and \autoref{fig:hemodynamics2}). It then spreads out over the aneurysm surface, generating a large stable vortex in the bulge. As a consequence, high OSI values form on the ``eyes'' of the vortex, as seen on \autoref{fig:hemodynamics}. The WSS in these regions displays persistently expansive properties with high divergences of the normalized WSS vector field. A pouch at the aneurysm's inner side stands out with high OSI and small TAWSS values, due to its irregular perfusion (see \autoref{fig:hemodynamics}).

\subsubsection*{Post-operative hemodynamics}
In the postoperative state, the FDS induces changes of the parent artery, which is now circular and no longer traverses the aneurysm sack. Consequently, the impinging jet at systole is slowed down to half of its preoperative value (0.8\,m/s). The volumetric flow traversing the stent at this same instant is 38\% of the ICA's flow rate, and the spatio-temporal mean bulge velocity drops to 37\,mm/s, as reported in the previous chapter. Notably, this mean velocity increases by 17\% when wall compliance is modelled. This underlines the interest of FSI simulations in assessing FDS outcome, as velocity reduction is the ultimate goal of the device. Regarding the flow strcuture, the weakened inflow no longer holds a stable vortex, but one that travels from near the inflow towards the aneurysm's tip. The OSI experiences an increase on its path, as can be seen on the second quadrant of \autoref{fig:hemodynamics}. The flow pattern of the FSI simulation resembles largely the ones from the CFD simulations. The OSI, similarly to reported observations\,\cite{Aurele}, increases moderately when simulating the effect of compliance. Other changes involving flow structure are seen, but not discussed here due to their minor impacts on the discussion. For more details, we refer to the thesis of Aurèle Goetz.

The Pulsatility Index (PI) in the postoperative state was computed at 1.24, resembling common literature values for giant ICA aneurysms\,\cite{Larrabide2016}. The PI belongs to the rather lower spectrum of values found, which is tied to two main factors. On one side, the impingement angle of the flow and size of the IA permit the formation of large vortices that retain a large proportion of their momentum throughout the cycle. The mean bulge-averaged velocity $\bar{u}$ therefore remains high, and so does also its minimum value. The second factor is the chosen patient waveform. The averaged curve presents two closely followed peaks at systole, instead of one more pronounced one. This leads to a more regular flow pattern and thus a lower PI\@.

\subsubsection*{Pressure changes}
Considered separately due to its alleged importance is the alteration of the bulge pressures between the preoperative and postoperative (ruptured) cases. To that end, we have extracted the pressure along the centerline of the ICA in all simulated cases, including the FSI computation. Figure\,\ref{fig:ctrl_p} shows the pressure drops with a colour-coded inflow volume rate. The pressures have been offset to have zero pressure at diastole in the distal section of the MCAs.

In the CFD simulations, the profiles overall vary almost linearly for the lower flow rates, except for some fluctuations at systole. Notably, the pressure drops in the later ICA sections are remarkably high with respect to the human pressure levels. For comparison, the total pressure drop from the heart to the venous system lies between 80--120\,mmHg for a normotensive person, which amouts to 10.7--16\,kPa. The question of whether these are meaningful values or if there is something erroneous in the modelling remains unaddressed despite being commonly observed\,\cite{Cebral2011, Steinman2013, Kizhisseri2023}. After estimating the pressure drop with linear Poiseuille resistances, we found that the obtained results indeed are expected. Most of it is the product of the considerable tapering that the ICA experiences and possibly the fact of not modelling the flow through the smaller vessels and perforators\footnote{Perforator: Small artery penetrating or traversing muscles or bones}. We also confirm through the FSI simulation, that the large capacitance of the aneurysm leads to a reduction of the maximal pressure by 1.5\,kPa, thanks to a flatening of the pressure waveform over a cardiac cycle (see \autoref{fig:ctrl_p}).

A search in the anatomy of the human brain revealed other possible factors that can be related to the stronger pressure drop in the main arteries. Liebeskind \etal\,\cite{Liebeskind2016} elaborates on the special mechanisms that the brain has to regulate its blood flow. The brain arterioles lack capillary sphincters that are elsewhere strong pressure regulators. This shifts the task towards upstream arteries, which need to adapt seamlessly to changing blood flow conditions, \eg\,during physical exercise or sleep. For this purpose, brain arteries have a well-developed layer of smooth muscle cells that can widen or narrow down the vessels, thereby adjusting the flow resistance\,\cite{Liebeskind2016}. The extent of their capabilities is showcased during vasospasm, a critical form of autoregulation that can onset in during haemorrhages. There, the entire ICA may be shut by the medial contraction\,\cite{Branko2014}, posing life-threatening conditions. It could thus be possible that the brain arteries have a higher resistance to flow than other arteries, as part of their ``standby'' autoregulation capabilities. The writer underscores that this is merely a hypothesis and needs to be further investigated.

Returning to the matter at hand, the pressures over the cardiac cycle show mostly a linear relationship with the flow rate, excepting the FSI simulation, where a dephase of pressure and inflow is apparent. For a clearer representation, consider the centerline pressure during systole alone displayed in \autoref{fig:ctrl_p_systole}. There, we have equalized the pressures at the inflow according to the downstream autoregulation hypothesis of Cebral \etal\,\cite{Cebral2011}. In the cavernous sections, pressure drops minimally until halfway into the clinoidal section. There, the tapering of the ICA begins, leading to a fall of 200\,Pa. At the aneurysm, the pre-treatment case sees a flat pressure profile, whereas the post-treatment case continues its nearly linear decay. At the distal end of the aneurysm, the tables turn, causing a sharp drop in the preoperative pressure of nearly 26\,mmHg due to the distal stenosis and the sudden contraction of the flow. The postoperative FSI case displays an identical behaviour to its rigid counterpart until reaching the aneurysm. At that point, a surplus of flow enters the bulge causing it to inflate. Consequently, the resistance in the vessel diminishes yielding a pressure plateau.

\subsection{Discussion}\label{chap5:sec4}
With the observed hemodynamics and the patient case description, we can attempt to relate the rupture to the proposed theories from the literature. By doing so, we can at least see if the present case fits into the general picture and if so, support one of the claims with this one case. The proposed theories relie on pressure-induced\,\cite{Cebral2011}, haemorrhage-induced \,\cite{Kulcsar2011} and lastly flow structure-induced rupture\,\cite{Li2019}.


\subsubsection*{Pressure induced rupture}
In a widely known computational study in the year 2010, Cebral \etal\,\cite{Cebral2011} analyzed the hemodynamics of 7 FDS-treated intracranial aneurysms located at the upper ICA sections. Of those, 3 ruptured within a week of the operation, while the remaining 4 occluded during the following months. The healed aneurysms were all middle-sized excepting one giant aneurysm, whereas all ruptured belonged to the latter category. In the study, the cases were computed in preoperative and post-rupture states following similar steps as here. The pressures were levelled at the inlets of all simulations to be normotensive. They observed that under those conditions two of the three cases resulted in a pressure increase in the IA. The deployment of the FDS had opened narrow passages of the parent artery located upstream of the lesion, reducing the resistance of the flow and thus carrying higher pressures farther. In the other ruptured case, the stenosis was located past the aneurysm and thus no noticeable alteration of the pressure was reported.

While this hypothesis might be a lead in some cases\,\cite{Hirato2023}, it seems overall unsuited here. Firstly, the pressure decreases post-treatment due to the location of the stenosis, similar to other works on this subject\,\cite{Li2019}. In that sense, this aneurysm resembles the giant one that did not rupture in their study. Furthermore, we have observed a strong pulsatility of the aneurysm, which translates into a capacitance that spreads out the pressure waveform. Inevitably, the wall movement introduces further mean pressure losses due to dissipation of energy into the surrounding tissue. Lastly, the timescale of the present rupture exceeds those of Cebral \etal\,\cite{Cebral2011} by months, pointing towards other long-term remodelling phenomena.


\subsubsection*{Thrombus mediated rupture}
Just months after the previous publication, Kulscar \etal\,\cite{Kulcsar2011} proposed a different mechanism for the delayed rupture of aneurysms. In their study, 13 patients with ruptured intracranial aneurysms were studied post-mortem through imaging and autopsies. The patients harboured giant-saccular and fusiform aneurysms that were treated with FDS, and in one case with stent-assisted coiling. 4 out of the 13 patients reported strong headaches prior to the rupture event, similar to our case. The ruptures were split into two categories, early ($n$\,=\,10) and late ($n$\,=\,3), with the latter taking place at least 3 months after the intervention. The latest instance of a rupture took place after 5 months, which falls short of the 8 months of our case. There, however, double anti-platelet therapy was reduced to single after three months, whereas here the patient was kept on the same medication during the whole time.

One common feature among all cases was the high degree of thrombosis in the aneurysm. Interestingly, thrombosis is generally considered as the treatment goal of FD and thus a positively perceived event. The two dissected aneurysms displayed a large degree of autolysis\footnote{Autolysis: Self-digestion of tissue.} and subsequent necrosis of the tissue. This was expressed in a strong mural thinning and the loss of smooth muscle cells and fibrous tissue. Based on these observations, the authors suggest that thrombosis might have disturbed the balance of the aneurysm wall, leading to a sped-up rupture. The size and aspect ratios ($>$\,1.6) played in thus far a role as that the thrombus forming more quickly within these aneurysms.

In the present giant aneurysm thorombosis is likely to have happened based on the hyperdensity on the MR images, although no autopsy was conducted to confirm it. It was first observed one week before the rupture and grew considerably until rupture. These observations fall in line with the disease progression outline by Kulcsar \etal\,\cite{Kulcsar2011}. What remains questionable is whether the thomrbus could unchain autolysis to the point of causing a rupture just few weeks after its initiation. In Kulcsar \etal\,\cite{Kulcsar2011}, they report one particular case in the late rupture group that after 3 months showed full patency of the aneurysm with no other apparent changes. 20 days after switching to single anti-platelet therapy the aneurysm ruptured and was largely thrombosed. If autolysis was the process leading to rupture in that case, it could be possible that little time is needed for a fatal degradation.

\subsubsection*{Flow structure-mediated rupture}
The last proposed theory that will be discussed is presented by Li \etal\cite{Li2019}. In their work, 10 patients with delayed aneurysm rupture were compared to 20 control patients, whose aneurysms remained stable. Simulations of the hemodynamics were carried out for both groups, including the simulations of the stents and porosity modelling of coils, when present. A large number of flow indicators, including TAWSS and OSI, were compared. Among the indicators, there was one categorical property related to flow stability. The latter was described as, ``flow patterns that persist (do not move or change) during the cardiac cycle.'' according to a prior work from Cebral \etal\,\cite{Cebral_Mut_Weir_Putman_2010}. When a stable flow was maintained after stenting, the aneurysms were less likely to have ruptured. The authors proposed that the structure of the flow could likewise impact the thrombus formation. By doing so, their theory could be related to the previously presented thrombus-mediated rupture, although evidence was not provided.

In regards to our case, we can confirm to have transitioned from a ``stable'' vortex preoperatively to a more irregular flow postoperatively (shown in \autoref{fig:hemodynamics2}). This transition can also be observed qualitatively in Cebral's paper\,\cite{Cebral2011}. There is however a certain degree of vagueness in the determination of stability that hovers menacingly over the interpretation. Here, newer flow structure identifiers introduced on the analysis of the topological skeleton\,\cite{Morbiducci2020} could provide a better quantifiable measure of the flow stability. Although first efforts are being made to apply the topological skeleton to intracranial aneurysms\cite{Mazzi2020}, clear guidelines for analysis are not yet available.

In the discussion regarding ``stability'', we have observed the ``unstable'' flow to be a result of the change of shape of the vasculature and not to result from the embedded stent. Since the work of Li \etal\,\cite{Li2019}, seems to consider only the postoperative state, it is difficult to compare the results to our case. Likewise, the timescales of the ruptures are not provided, making a relationship with the present case somewhat impossible. Li \etal\,\cite{Li2019} also includes the modelling of coils as a porous medium in their work. While this is undoubtly a reasonable strategy to compute mean velocities and other smooth quantities, it remains questionable whether factors like flow stability in the bulge are the same as when embedding the coils into the mesh. From a medical viewpoint, the use of coils did not influence the likelyhood of delayed aneurysm rupture, which was also confirmed elsewhere\,\cite{Kulcsar2011, Hirato2023}.

\subsubsection*{Limitations}
In the simulation of the case, no patient-specific inflowrate was available. To test the sensitivity, the preoperative case was simulated using flowrates scaled by $\pm$25\%. Although the flow patterns remained similar, we acknowledge that the nature of the waveform can impact the oscillatory components of the flow and needs to be tested further. Due to the poor quality of the postoperative images at the outlets, segmenting them was difficult and could feed back into the outflow boundary conditions. Lastly, it is important to point out that the mesh resolution at the stent was chosen following suggestions from literature\,\cite{Janiga2015,Larrabide2016} instead of convergence studies. This should be included in further work, to safely confirm the parameter choices. Furthermore, it would be interesting to study giant aneurysms using minimally dissipative solvers, such as the ones proposed by Valen-Sendstad\,\cite{Valen-Sendstad2014}, to assess solver sensitivity.


\begin{tcolorbox}[conclusions]

	After conducting a thorough analysis of the flow dynamics of the present giant aneurysm, we contrasted the observations with hypotheses on the rupture pathways found in literature. We have confirmed that under the same modelling assumptions as in the pressure-mediated rupture, no pressure increase could be observed. Thrombus-induced autolysis appears to be the most likely hypothesis, but requires more supporting data to be considered bullet-proof. Methodically, we have seen that FSI can play a crucial role in these cases, where uncertainties around the wall constitution can be overcome through imaging data. The alteration of the pressure dynamics particularly can have a strong impact on the conclusions and should be addressed when observed. Further, we point out some shortcommings of literature in this field, particularly the use of clearly defined indicators for (un-) ``stable'' flows and also the modelling of thrombus formation, to obtain a better grasp of the temporal progression of the wall weakening. With these careful considerations, researchers might ultimately provide helpful indications to clinicians, who so far, have to relie mostly on intuition when dealing with these intrincate cases.
\end{tcolorbox}

\subsection{Pathology}
Blister aneurysms are rare but distinct cerebral vascular lesions. They constitute less than 2\% of all intracranial aneurysms and are typically found on internal carotid artery, although can also be found in other locations such as the anterior communicating artery or basilar trunk\,\cite{Nasra2021, Papasilekas2020}. Blister aneurysms are usually small, shallow, broad-based, or semi-fusiform protrusions on the arterial wall. The aneurysm wall is markedly weak, predisposing these lesions to rapid growth, rerupture, and catastrophic consequences. They are thought to result from a tear in the arterial inner wall, leading to an intramural haemorrhage and a protrusion covered only by the adventitial layer. Some studies have shown signs of dissection, such as a double lumen or arterial narrowing. An alternative theory suggests that blister aneurysms are pseudoaneurysms, essentially representing a focal defect in the arterial wall covered by an organized blood clot\,\cite{Papasilekas2020}. The exact aetiology of blister aneurysms remains unclear although hight fluid stresses are thought to be implicated in their formation\,\cite{Gonzalez2014,  Song_Oh_Kim_Chung_Lim_Kim_Shin_2016,Nasra2021}.

Most blister aneurysms present with subarachnoid haemorrhage (95.6\%\,\cite{Nasra2021}), diagnosed initially via brain CT scans. Due to their shape and fragility only a few treatment options are viable in both surgical and endovascular fields. The choice of method is conditioned frequently by the environment and accessibility to the lesion. In absolute terms, endovascular approaches lead in 85\% of cases to a functional neurologic outcome compared to 72\% from surgical interventions\,\cite{Nasra2021}. The authors note however that the success is also conditioned by the severity of the case, which is often more elevated in surgery candidates. An improving trend when transitioning to endovascular approaches is also reported by others\,\cite{Gonzalez2014, Song_Oh_Kim_Chung_Lim_Kim_Shin_2016, Peschillo2016}, in particular for the recently introduced flow diverters. Independent studies\,\cite{Mokin2018,Zhu2018} have applied flow diverters to cover the ruptured BBAs obtaining success rates between 90 and 97\% when using a single device. Using overlapping stents and flow diverters lead to a reduced successrate of only 62\%, caused by a higher morbidity\,\cite{Zhu2018}. Occlusion rates however were similar.

This leaves room for a hypothesis worth studying in an attempt to improve patient treatment. The use of overlapping flow diverters increases the metal cover and the exclusion of the aneurysms from flow. Despite that, the occlusion rates have been observed to be similar. If we assume the increased morbidity to be a consequence of thromboembolic events sparked by a higher quantity of metal in the artery, saving material could decrease the risks for patients without impacting too much the occlusion rates\,\cite{Ji2017}. The side-wall nature of BBAs\,\cite{Papasilekas2020} promotes the flow reduction with FDS, since shear driven circulations are very effectively cut by these devices\,\cite{Augsburger2011}. For that reason, we will study the flow in two patient-specific cases on preoperative, single and double stented scenarios. We hope that a description of the hemodynamics in these, so far, successfully treated ruptured cases serves as a motivation for the community to conduct larger and statistically meaningful studies on the subject.

\subsection{Discussion}
The two presented patients are characterized by complex aneurysms with little margin of freedom during treatment. Their weak walls and shallow morphology restrict clinicians to use only a small subset of the methods that are usually available for handling saccular aneurysms. Although the the formations differ in nature, \ie\,the first is classified as a BBA and the second as a regular IA, both raise the question on how much metal coverage is justified to reduce the hemodynamic stress. While a reduced stress is desirable on ruptured tissue to foster healing and reduce risk of rerupture, an excessive amount can cause serious thromboembolic complications in jailed vessels. While we can certainly not answer this question with only these two patients, we can at least the hemodynamics that, so far, have kept the patients in good shape without neurological and further haemorrhages.


\subsubsection*{Stenting blister aneurysms}
The location and shape of BBA aneurysms makes them commonly subject to unidirectional, high WSS\,\cite{Peschillo2016}. In our study, we have confirmed a consistent reduction of the flow in the BBA by one-half despite a low metal coverage compared to the recommended 30\%. This result, as already noted by Augsburger \etal\,\cite{Augsburger2011}, stems from the good capabilities of FDS to cut down shear-driven flows. If a WSS reduction is assumed to be one of the determinant factors in the healing process of a rupture, the use of a single stent might be preferred. The second FDS, although once more reducing the TAWSS, achieved only a moderate absolute reduction with respect to the first stent, while nearly doubling the metal surface exposed to blood flow. The problematic feature of metallic surfaces is that they are thromboembogenic\,\cite{GEGENSCHATZSCHMID2022}, \ie\,they can easily trigger coagulation cascades forming blood clots on their surface. These can detach once a certain size is reached and travel down the arterial system causing infarction of vessels. Despite recent efforts in coating the devices\,\cite{Girdhar2018}, (dual) antiplatelet therapy remains mandatory and often prescribed for a lifetime in patients that have an implanted stent\,\cite{Dandapat2021}.

To more accurately assess this research question, it would be necessary to have an estimation of how much exposed metal bears bears a noteworthy risk. We have seen several studies assessing the thrombogenity of FDS, but our trust remains low since, all studies are financed by or supported by FD manufacturers and claim at the same time that their newly developed model is better than its competitors\,\cite{GIRBAS2024100666, Girdhar2018}.


\subsubsection*{Stenting bifurcation aneurysms}
Bifurcations belong to locations where the use of coils or WEB devices is preferred\,\cite{Dandapat2021}. When these cannot be used, due to a wide-neck formation for instance, flow diverters have to be deployed through the main artery and one of the outgoing branches. Selecting the outgoing branch is conditioned by the size of the vessels, their role in brain perfusion and how difficult the operation is. In this case, the largest outflow was chosen for two reasons, (i) it was better aligned with the parent vessel and (ii) it was more readily accessible with the catheter, permitting a safer intervention. Had the other branch been chosen, then there would be a non-negligible risk of hitting the ruptured aneurysm with the guide device. Additionally, the second M2 had a 48\% smaller radius while the chosen M2 preserved the size of the parent vessel (see \autoref{fig:blister2_angio}).

Hemodynamically, we confirm that the insertion of the FDS has helped deflecting the impinging flow away from the bulge reducing hemodynamic stress. We also executed the deployment in the other configuration. Due to the notoriously smaller vessel caliber of the second branch, the porosity of the FDS was notoriously high and only adjusted past the aneurysm neck to nominal values. Under those circumstances, the diverting effect would not have had the same impact and furthermore, we expected a redicrection into the aneurysm, making it straight ahead not recommendable. For these reasons, we did not pursue this approach any further.

The use of a second stent in this case shares the same criticism as in the previous patient. The chosen stent in this case was an XFRED device which yields a lower porosity than the Silk Vista models. This device also exerts a larger radial force that can, as was the case in the giant aneurysm, change the vessel geometry\,\cite{Maragkos2020}. The higher metal cover counterbalances the inertial flow and manages to yield similar stress reductions as in the BBA. Nevertheless, it is unclear how the difference in performance ponders with the increased risk of thromboembolism.

\subsubsection*{Limitations}
Similarly to the previous study, we point out that inflow conditions were in this case generalized and can impact the results. In the case of patient 1, we modelled the PCom as a regular outflow, while a well-developed CoW was seen on MR scans. A better choice in this case would be conducting a study on the CoW to extract local flow rates and impose them as boundary conditions, as discussed in chapter 3. In both cases, we utilized the postoperative images for segmentation. In the preoperative ones, there were more perforators clearly visible (ophthalmic and anterior choroidal arteries) that could be added or modelled later for increased fidelity.


\begin{tcolorbox}[conclusions]

	Uncoilable aneurysms, such as BBA or wide-necked aneurysms, can benefit from an FDS treatment as is the case for the presented patients and as reported in literature. The hemodynamic alterations of a single FDS have proven sufficient in treated patients until this day. The hypothetical use of a second telescopic FDS has provided limited usage with respect to the first device, making it probably unjustified in the face of the added thromboembolism risks.

	In this study, hemodynamics have proven useful in characterizing flow and measuring the impact of the devices. The deployment itself was a fundamental contributor, since it provided the porosity estimates that cannot otherwise be extracted from the deployed devices. With larger studies on cases like these, we hope that clear guidelines in treatment can be defined, helping clinicians in choosing the most favorable treatment for their patients.

\end{tcolorbox}
