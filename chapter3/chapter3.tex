After having introduced a general computation framework in the previous chapter, it is now the specific conditions of the human brain arterial flow that are going to be discussed. For this purpose, we consider first an anatomical description of the arterial network and its functions. Then angiographic imaging and the segmentation procedure are described, highlighting its relevance for subsequent simulations. The surface triangulation derived from segmentations is then used to extrude boundaries and generate computational meshes. The boundary conditions that have been shortly outlined before will then be adapted to ensure physiological conditions in the simulation domain. In a final step, the obtained information will be used to carry out several simulations of a specific type of IA, studying a new aspect of hemodynamics simulations in the brain. This chapter contains in a larger proportion passages from the publication ``\textit{Evaluating the Impact of Domain Boundaries on Hemodynamics in Intracranial Aneurysms within the Circle of Willis}''\cite{Jeken-Rico2023} that has been published during the course of the study time.

\section{The Arterial System}\label{chap3:sec1}
The cardiovascular system is a complex network responsible for the circulation of blood, delivering oxygen and nutrients to tissues, and removing waste products. The aorta, the largest artery in the body, originates from the left ventricle of the heart. It serves as the primary conduit for oxygenated blood, propelling it into systemic circulation. Structurally, the aorta is composed of three layers: the intima (inner layer), media (middle layer), and adventitia (outer layer). These layers provide both strength and elasticity, enabling the aorta to withstand and dampen the pulsatile pressure generated by cardiac contractions. The intima is lined with endothelial cells that is responsible, among others, for the biochemical interactions between the blood and arteries\cite{Kruger-Genge2019}, while the media, composed of smooth muscle cells and elastic fibers, allows the aorta to expand and contract with each heartbeat, maintaining consistent blood pressure. The adventitia, consisting of connective tissue, provides structural support and anchors the aorta to surrounding tissues. Upon leaving the aortic arch, blood is distributed to various major arteries, including the brachiocephalic\footnote{Cephalic: In or relating to the head.} trunk, the left common carotid artery, and the left subclavian\footnote{Subclavian: Below the clavicula.} artery. These arteries further branch out to supply the upper body, including the head and brain. The brachiocephalic trunk divides into the right subclavian artery and the right common carotid artery, while the common carotid arteries (right and left) ascend the neck and bifurcate into the internal (ICA) and external carotid arteries. They are the primarily supply the brain, carrying approximately 72\% of the total flux\cite{Zarrinkoob2015}. The remainder is supplied through the vertebral arteries, which run along the vertebral spine and merge into the Basilar Artery (BA). The latter, in most cases, joins the ICAs forming an anastomotic\footnote{ Anastomosis: A cross-connection between adjacent channels, tubes, fibres, or other parts of a network.} network known as the Circle of Willis (CoW), which ensures redundancy in cerebral blood supply. The ICAs enter the skull through the carotid canals, providing the anterior circulation of the brain and giving rise to several critical branches. The ophthalmic artery supplies the eyes and orbits, the anterior cerebral artery supplies the medial portions of the frontal lobes and superior medial parietal lobes, and the middle cerebral artery supplies the lateral aspects of the frontal, temporal, and parietal lobes, encompassing critical areas for motor and sensory functions. The BA bifurcates into the Posterior Cerebral Arteries (PCA), supplying the occipital lobes, the inferior parts of the temporal lobes, and the brainstem.

Arteries are uniquely structured to manage high-pressure blood flow. Elastic arteries, including the aorta and its major branches, contain a high density of elastic fibers, allowing them to stretch and recoil, buffering the pressure changes from the heart's pumping action. Muscular arteries, such as the ICAs, contain more smooth muscle than elastic fibers, regulating blood flow by vasoconstriction and vasodilation, and adjusting the distribution of blood to various regions. In most extremities, arterioles, the smallest arterial branches leading to capillaries, have a thin wall of smooth muscle that regulates blood flow into capillary beds through precapillary sphincters. This permits the body to reduce the blood perfusion in regions where it is not needed at a given moment. The brain however, due to its high metabolism, requires a consistent high supply and thus does generally not have these sphinters. As a consequence, a large portion of the arterial pressure gradient is dissipated before blood reaching the terminal branches that supply the deep regions of the brain~\cite{Liebeskind2016}.

Acknowledging the complexity of blood flow in the brain is crucial to properly evaluate properly, but also understand the remodelling effects that take place after introducing a prosthesis. The core of the research in the frame of this work will be dedicated to the study of the CoW and its adjacent arteries, since it hosts the bulk of intracranial aneurysms~\cite{Brown2014}.


\subsection{Circle of Willis}\label{chap3:sec1:subsec1}
As previously stated, the anastomotic network formed by the merging ICAs and the BA. It is believed that this redundancy is a helpful evolutionary feature that protects the brain from the consequences of the infarction of one of its main suppliers. The interconnected vessels of the CoW in that case permit the contralateral ICA or BA to balance out the missing flux. Other vascular diseases, such as stenoses\footnote{Stenosis: Abnormal narrowing of a passage in the body.}, are also alleviated by redirecting the flow and reducing the strain on the heart. The ICAs, as the main suppliers, have the largest diameter among the vessels of the CoW, measuring 3-4.5\,mm in diameter in common cases. The MCAs and ACAs, as the main branches of the ICA, have a diameter around 2.5\,mm and 2\,mm, respectively, depending on the parent arteries size~\cite{Liebeskind2016}. The BA has a proximal\footnote{Proximal: Situated nearer to the centre of the body or the point of attachment.} diameter of $3mm$ that in most cases decays quickly towards the distal section due to the large number of branches and perforators along its way.

The complete arrangement of the CoW, as shown in a sketch on \autoref{fig:cow_scheme}, is not fully developed in all humans. In fact, only an estimated 11.9\% of adults posses the textbook-type CoW, while more than 50\% miss at least one of the communicating vessels ~\cite{Hindenes2020}. Hypoplastic\footnote{Hypoplasia: Underdevelopment or incomplete development of a tissue or an organ.} vessels are commonly defined by having a diameter below a set threshold, which in the context of the CoW is often set to 1\,mm~\cite{Hindenes2020, Hindenes2023}. The type of CoW is conditions in a natural way how the flow is distributed from the suppliers. One common formation are so-called fetal PComs, in which the P1 segments of the PCAs are underdeveloped and the flow to the posterior circulation is instead provided from the ICAs through the PComs. The shape of the CoW has been shown to influence the likelihood of IA, doubling the prevalence of IA for incomplete circles with respect to complete ones~\cite{Hindenes2023}.

The anterior circulation, comprising the ICAs, MCAs and ACAs, as well as the ACom, contains 85\% of the lesions~\cite{Brown2014} and is therefore the most common target of operations. Due to the asymptomatic nature of most unruptured IA, they are usually found during routine checks or unrelated screenings. The main tool for analysing the disease are angiography images obtained either from Computed Tomography (CT) or Magnetic Resonance (MR) scans. Both modalities encompass benefits and disadvantages that are also important when segmenting the patient's lumen.

\subsection{Medical Imaging}\label{chap3:sec1:subsec2}
Arterial angiography\footnote{Angiography: Visualisation of vessels.} highlights the structure of vessels in a designated location of the body. CT angiography involves using ionising radiation to visualise the blood vessels in the brain. A contrast dye, usually iodine-based, is administered through a catheter placed in a vein of an extremity. As the dye travels through the bloodstream, the CT scanner takes a series of detailed cross-sectional images in which the radiopaque ink and the bones stand out from the nearly transparent remaining tissue. The resulting images have a spatial resolution between 0.6 and 1\,mm and are most commonly used in diagnosis~\cite{Hacein-Bey2011}. Higher resolutions can be obtained with a different CT modality, the Digital Subtraction Angiography (DSA). There, the contrast agent is directly flushed into the target artery upstream of the region of interest, providing superior resolutions of 0.2-0.3\,mm~\cite{Hacein-Bey2011}. Additionally, a second take without contrast agent is subtracted from the main take to remove static structures, such as bones or coils. The scan can likewise be executed by rotating the recording components of the scanner during the capture time. The images recorded during the $240^{\circ}$ sweep are then reconstructed into one image cube with isotropic resolutions around 0.15--0.2\,mm.

The second modality of angiography imaging is Time-Of-Flight (TOF) MR tomography\footnote{Tomography: A technique for displaying a representation of a cross section through a human body or other solid object.}. TOF MR leverages the movement of blood to produce high-contrast images without the need for contrast agents, which is particularly beneficial for patients with allergies or renal impairments. This method exploits the differences in magnetic properties between stationary tissue and flowing blood to create the images. In a typical MRI scan, repeated radio pulses saturate the protons in stationary tissues, making them appear darker. However, in TOF MR, as blood flows into the imaging slice, it hasn't been exposed to these pulses. This means the protons in the flowing blood are not saturated and retain their signal, appearing bright on the final image. In contrast, the stationary tissue within the imaging slice has been exposed to repeated pulses and therefore appears darker. This difference in signal intensity creates a natural contrast between the bright blood vessels and the darker surrounding tissues, enabling clear and detailed visualisation of the blood vessels (see \autoref{fig:TOFMR}). The resolutions of TOF MR depend fundamentally on the intensity of the magnetic field that can be generated. Common 3\,T machines yield anisotropic resolutions of $0.42\times0.42\times1.5$\,mm, where the coarse side corresponds to the axis in which the scanner sweeps. Newly introduced 7\,T machines offer higher resolutions of up to 0.3\,mm that can even be exploited to extract flow velocity profiles through a technique labelled 4D Flow MRI~\cite{Berg2014}. This technique emerged more than a decade ago and has been an active research field. While the direct use of the scanner to extract flow profiles is attractive, low resolutions and artifacts, combined with long measurement times prevent this technique from being used in day-to-day clinical practice. In the \autoref{chap3:sec2}, it will be further discussed due to its role in computing inflow boundary condition values. MR angiography, as already mentioned, profits from being a non-invasive technique that can simultaneously highlight all sections of the CoW, unlike DSA, making it a widely used diagnosis tool.

During medical interventions, CT scans are the only imaging device used, due to its capacity to provide real time images in which radiopaque devices, such as endovascular coils, are clearly seen.

\subsection{Vessel Segmentation}\label{chap3:sec1:subsec3}
The images obtained during the scans are quickly segmented by radiologists to measure possible malformations and plan interventions. In the process, attention is given to the region of interest and anatomy knowledge is put to use to differentiate between imaging artifacts and vasculature features. When the segmentations ought to be used in hemodynamics simulations, a larger portion of the vasculature has to be processed. This assures the integrity of the simulation in regards to the base geometry it runs on. It has been speculated that inconsistent indications on risk estimators for IA among different research teams might be caused due to differing segmentation techniques. In an effort to clarify this question, Berg and Vo\ss\,\etal~\cite{Berg2018, voss2019} opened a challenge in which the 26 participating teams would first segment three DSA images containing a total of five IA. The results were then analysed based on imaging metrics and hemodynamics indicators from simulations. The authors concluded that the largest variations among groups could be an important issue, possibly leading to diverging hypothesis. For this reason, the segmentation bias is retained as an important factor in the patient-specific cases treated within this thesis.

To obtain the mask marking the arterial vasculature, \href{https://www.slicer.org/}{3D Slicer} has been used. The open-source program provides handy tools to navigate and manipulate medical image data. The latter is provided by medical institutions, under the terms of both ethics and cooperation agreements, in the normed DICOM format. In an initial step, the domain of interest is defined and removed from the remainder of the image. Subsequently, minor smoothing and gradient filtering operations can be run to remove background noise and more properly delineate the lumen. Bones and implants, which are commonly found in 3D rotational angiography images, are be removed manually or with some dedicated semi-supervised tools, before engaging with the segmentation of the vessels. The radiopaque ink in the case of DSA creates a smooth intensity gradient at the lumen that requires the person segmenting the vessel to set an appropriate cut threshold. In the case of TOF MR, the signal may vary with the in-plane flow velocity, requiring likewise a careful handling. As a user who has not undergone medical training, confirmation was always sought from the cooperating clinicians before concluding a segmentation. One common medical practice is to remove small and hypoplastic vessels from the segmentation due to the small implications and more error prone delineation. This is commonly adopted for vessels of diameter below 1\,mm.

At some point of the segmentation it is required to limit the extension of the vessels surrounding the diseased tissue. This in fact presents an interesting question that is not fully described in literature, particularly in regards to communicating vessels. There the flow direction is not necessarily clear \apriori, as it might depend on the overall flows of the circuit. This interesting question has been the seed for the first publication that has been carried out during the course of this work. Before however engaging in more depth on that question, a few more topics will be introduced that will explain keypoints in modelling physiological hemodynamics.



\section{Hemodynamics Simulations}\label{chap3:sec2}

\subsection{Mesh Generation}\label{chap3:sec2:subsec:1}
The segmentation yields a binary mask identifying vessels and background. Using a resolution-dependent smoothing factor, the surface of the bodies is meshes with triangles. Hereby, the meshing algorithm provided by Slicer does not distinguish between walls and the cut inlets and outlets. Therefore, the mesh has to be manually opened with planar cuts. For carrying out this operation, \href{https://www.paraview.org/}{Paraview} and \href{https://vtk.org/}{vtk} can be used, which additionally permit re-triangulating the mesh to remove any quadrilateral patches originating from the cuts. The location of the inlet cut is best set on a straight segment of the parent vessel, for instance the cervical portion of the ICA, which stretches for more than 30mm in a straight untapered manner\cite{Vijaywargiya2017}. This alleviates the fact of modelling the inlet velocity profiles through a simplified parabolic or plug profile~\cite{Castro2006, Hodis2015}. The tortuous vessels proximal of the IA then bend and shape the flow into a physiological shape again reducing the modelling errors~\cite{Berg2019}. Additionally, it is advisable to extrude the open mesh along its open boundaries for several reasons. It further reduces inflow modelling errors and allows mapping an irregular cross-section to a circular profile. The latter are especially important when parabolic or Womersley profiles are to be used, since they rely on circular pipes. On the outlets side the extrusions reduce the risk of reflux into the systems, simplifying the CFD modelling overall.

\paragraph{Extrusion of inlets and outlets} are generated with transfinite interpolations between the original inlet and a target circle. The target profile is generated by fitting a circle to the original cross-section and shifting it along the inlet's outward normal. The concept is sketched in \autoref{fig:transfinite}.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.4\linewidth]{chapter3/Figures/Meshing/extrusion_scheme.pdf}
	\caption{Sketch of the interpolation between the vessel cross-section (in the back) and the circular profile (front).}
	\label{fig:transfinite}
\end{figure}
The parametrization of the extruded circle is based on the least-squares fit of a circle on the original, irregular boundary. To this end, every node and edge belonging to a boundary patch is fetched. The nodal positions of the given patch are shifted by the mean position towards the origin and registered in a matrix $X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times3}$, a singular value decomposition can be run on $X$ to obtain the surface normal.
\begin{equation*}
	X = U\Sigma V^*, \quad \text{with}\quad U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},\, \Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times3},\, V^*\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times3}
\end{equation*}
The vector corresponding to the smallest singular value in matrix $V^*$ provides the surface normal i.e. $\vec{n} = V_{3,j}$. It can then be used to rotate the boundary cross-section onto a 2D plane using the Rodrigues' rotation:
\begin{equation}
	M_{rot} = sI + \sqrt{1-s^2} [\vec{k}]_\times + (1-s) \vec{k}\otimes\vec{k}
	\label{eq:rodri}
\end{equation}
Where $[\cdot]_\times$ indicates the skew-symmetric matrix spanned by $\cdot$ and $\vec{k}$ and $s$ are defined by:
\begin{equation}
	\vec{k} = \vec{n}\times \vec{e}_z,\qquad s = \vec{n}\cdot \vec{e}_z
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[htb]
	\centering
	\input{chapter3/Figures/Meshing/Rotation}
	\caption{Projection of the cut onto a 2D plane in the reference coordinate system $\xi,\,\eta$.}
\end{figure}
Note, that all the nodes are by construction on a plane, thus making the third component of the rotated zero. The cross-section in the $(\xi, \eta)$ plane can now be fitted with a simple least-squares algorithm to a circle:
\begin{equation}
	(\xi-\xi_0)^2 + (\eta-\eta_0)^2 = r^2
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	[\xi_i\quad \eta_i\quad 1]\,\vec{c} = \xi_i^2 + \eta_i^2
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	\vec{c}_* = \argmin{\vec{c}\in \mathbb{R}^3} \norm{A\vec{c}-\vec{b}}^2
\end{equation}
The centre coordinates $\xi_0, \eta_0$ then are rotated and translated back to the $(x,y,z)$ space and used to parameterise a circle with the computed radius $r$. The circle is shifted in outward normal direction $\vec{n}$ for a desired distance. If the cut is not perpendicular to the vessel direction, it is also possible to correct the extrusion direction by the average normal of the edges at the cross-section. The nodal coordinates are matched between the circle and the cross-section based on their minimal euclidean distance. With a step wise generation of nodes and a consequent meshing, the desired mesh extension is obtained (see \autoref{fig:extrusion}). The surface mesh provides a scaffold for a full 3D volumetric mesh in which the simulations can be run.

To generate a tetrahedra-based mesh, the open source meshing library \href{https://gmsh.info/}{gmsh} is used. It supports the input of open \textit{stl}\footnote{STL: Stereolithography files composed of triangles in 3D.} files and generates high quality meshes exploiting elaborated conformal mappings. Aside from the meshing of the interior, it is also possible to generate structured boundary layers along the vessel walls. The preimplemented algorithms detect open boundaries and consider them in this process.

\paragraph{Boundary layers} play a critical role in the computation of many fluid dynamics problems. The provide a better resolution of the phenomena occurring close to walls, where velocity gradients are high and in some cases vortices and other structures can be formed and detach. In the case of hemodynamics, turbulence related effects are not in the main spotlight. Even though studies~\cite{Valen-Sendstad2011,Jain2016} have devoted their time studying turbulence, its effects are, if present, overcast by segmentation variability and boundary condition modelling errors. Instead boundary layers play an important role in the computation of walls remodelling indicators, such as the previously introduced WSS. These quantities have been shown to be susceptible to the layer thickness and element arrangement. In Janiga \etal~\cite{Janiga2013_mesh}, simulations were carried out using finite volume solvers on different meshes of the same geometry. In that context, using structured boundary layers resulted in a more reproducible WSS computation across meshes. This is not straightforwardly expandable towards finite elements, for two main reasons. Finite volumes profit from regularly aligned cells, but handle tetrahedral meshes rather poorly in comparison. Due to the intrinsic formulation of flux balances across cells, finite volumes can handle polyhedra without large changes, whereas finite elements change radically if more complex element types are considered. On the counter side, FE can increase their order of convergence by using these higher order elements while paying for a higher computation cost. While this explanation surely falls short to properly evaluate the implications on the structure of boundary layers, it is intended to show that one-to-one translations of findings between papers are to be carefully considered. Since the context of this thesis does not include higher order elements, their influence on the boundary layer resolution could not be measured. This stays however as an interesting research topic for follow-up studies. To leverage the possible bias in the WSS computation with linear elements, boundary layers of consistent size should be used.

In gmsh, the wall Boundary Layers (BL) are generated as an inward extrusion of the surface mesh. The resulting prism elements are then split into tetrahedra. The sizes of the boundary layers can commonly be estimated through by targeting a non-dimensional wall distance $y^+ < 1$:
\begin{equation}
	h_{BL} = \frac{y^+\mu}{\rho u_*}
\end{equation}
There $u_*$ is the friction velocity, which can be computed through the Schlichting skin-friction correlation:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
		u_* = u_\infty  \frac{\left(2\log_{10}{(\Rey)} -0.65\right)^{-1.15} }{\sqrt{2}}
	\end{split}
	\label{eqn:friction_velocity}
\end{equation}
The Reynolds number in the model \autoref{eqn:friction_velocity} is defined on a plate over which the flow sweeps. In our context, it remains difficult to estimate for equivalent of the length unit in such a \Rey number. If the diameter of vessels is considered, maximal \Rey\,values span roughly between 250 and 750. In reference work~\cite{Jain2016, Valen-Sendstad2011} \Rey\,numbers between 200 and 697 were estimated. Using values in this reference with overestimated inflow velocities of  $u_{\infty}=$\,0.75\,m/s, one obtains minimal boundary layer sizes of $\approx$ 0.05\,mm. With the commonly adopted expansion factor 1.2, the minimal size can be progressively expanded inwards.

\subsection{Convergence Analysis}
Hemodynamics simulations intrinsically depend on the choice of resolutions in both temporal and spatial domains. To not repeat the same analysis over and over in this thesis, one illustrative case will be presented below for which both aspects have been considered. The geometry is part of the publication work that is contained in this chapter and was published during the course of these studies. Details about case that are not of immediate relevance here are given in the next section \autoref{chap3:sec3} for the sake of clarity.\\

\subsubsection{Spatial Resolution}
To identify appropriate choices of mesh sizes, computations can be run for different mesh resolutions. Based on the variation of primary variables or other flow indicators, it is possible to devise minimal resolution criteria. In cases of brain hemodynamics simulations, both the flow distribution through the network, as well as a high resolution image of the flow inside the aneurysm dome are important. To properly assess the mesh convergence, the first half will be dedicated to finding a reasonable resolution in the core of the vessels and the second half will be dedicated to the bulge. Doing splitting the analysis, we hope to have a clearer view on the implications of mesh resolution. This has been tested on one of the cases that forms part of the study that is contained by this chapter (case C). In a first step, meshes have been generated with mesh sizes:
$$h\in\{0.1, 0.15, 0.2, 0.25, 0.3\}\,\text{mm}$$
that englobe popular choices in IA literature. The aneurysms itself was kept at a fixed resolution of $h=0.1$\,mm, to study it separately. The mesh contained 3 boundary layers with a minimal size of $0.05$\,mm that stretched across the entire geometry. The convergence study was run considering the second of two cardiac cycles run at a constant time step of $\Delta t= 0.001$\,s. Semi-implicit schemes and non-Newtonian viscosity according to the Carreau-Yasuda law were used.

It can be seen that the differences among the resolutions are overall small and of quantitative nature, particularly, for mesh sizes below $0.2$\,mm. The velocity fields were linearly interpolated from all meshes to the coarsest mesh for a global comparison. Taking the most refined simulation as a reference, the $L_2$ norm of the velocity error was computed in the whole domain and integrated over the cardiac cycle and the space. This yielded errors (in mm/s) of:
\[\varepsilon_{0.3} = 4.937,\quad \varepsilon_{0.25} = 3.559, \quad\varepsilon_{0.2} = 2.414, \quad\varepsilon_{0.15} = 1.194\]
While the average errors, confirm no large dissent among acceptable resolutions, it is inconclusive on the best choice among them. Instead, the previously considered comparison (see \autoref{fig:conv_space}), suggest a mesh size of $h=0.2$\,mm as a good candidate.

The vessels retain the chosen $h=0.2$\,mm and all other simulation parameters are kept intact. From the obtained simulations, similar small changes in the primary variables could be observed. To consider a new facet of the important indicators, both the Oscillatory Shear Index (OSI) and the Time-Averaged Wall Shear Stress (TAWSS) have been computed over the aneurysm bulge.

The integrated errors with respect to the finest resolution have been recorded along with the local TAWSS error obtained from the last cardiac cycle in \autoref{fig:osi_tawss}. The errors in this case have been computed by mapping the OSI and TAWSS fields from the finer meshes to the coarsest one using nearest neighbour interpolation. This interpolator, even though sub-optimal, manages to avoid a large number of undefined values which otherwise follow from the use of a smooth interpolation on meshes with a slight mismatch of the surfaces. Since the indicators (OSI and TAWSS) are restricted to the boundaries by their intrinsic nature, it is easy for nodes to be slightly shifted outside the intersections of the convex hulls spanned by the meshes. Any linear or higher order interpolation fails there and yields invalid results. Nearest-neighbour interpolation however is impervious to these issues at the cost of a larger interpolation error. The obtained results show little deviations in TAWSS whose values are withing the range of 0.5--2\,\pa\,for the bulge. The maximal deviations are found on the neck sections of the aneurysm, where due to larger velocity gradients the WSS differs more notably (see right of \autoref{fig:osi_tawss}). Similar results hold for the OSI over the bulge. The relationship between the different errors is not arranged in a decreasing order. This can either be the result of perturbations due to the interpolation method or errors induced by the granularity of the segmentation. The geometries were segmented and smoothed following the previously described steps. The mesh size of the surface model exported from 3D Slicer lies around an estimated 0.1-0.15\,mm based on the angiography image resolution. One option is smoothing and refining the segmentation surface mesh, another one is simply avoiding diminute element sizes below 0.1\,mm. From \autoref{fig:osi_tawss} it is concluded that a separate refinement of the aneurysm might not be necessary. \subsubsection{Temporal Resolution}
Time step size is in general set to match a unit CFL number in the simulations. One reason for this is maintaining an equilibrium between the single components of the momentum stabilization parameter, which depends on both spatial and temporal resolutions. Having a too small time step causes the stabilization to become homogeneous and small, \ie~bounded to the time step size. On one side, this minimises the added numerical diffusion. On the other it increases the risk of losing stability. To find time step sizes that are within reasonable limits, all hemodynamics simulations of this study have been tested with different time steps $\Delta t = \{ 0.0001, 0.0005, 0.001, 0.0025, 0.005, 0.01\}$\,s. It has been observed that for nearly all computations $\Delta t = 0.0001$\,s lead to stability issues. These would also occur in some cases when using the next larger step $\Delta t = 0.0005$\,s, but not for $\Delta t = 0.001$\,s and above.

The time scheme also contributes to the accumulation of errors and was therefore analyzed separately with a fixed contribution in the stabilization parameters of $\Delta t = 0.001$\,s. The time steps chosen are $\Delta t = \{ 0.0005, 0.001, 0.0025, 0.005, 0.01\}$\,s, and represent common choices found in literature~\cite{Berg2019_challenge}. The velocity is plotted at systole over the same line as in the previous case in \autoref{fig:conv_time}.

\subsection{Risk Indicators}
The objective of these carefully considered convergence studies is to ensure a proper computation of flow indicators. Most of them, as stated before, depend on the shearing exerted on the vessel walls. In its most basic representation the WSS is expressed as:
\begin{equation}
	\vec{\tau}_{wss}  = \vec{n} \times \left[ (\sigma\cdot\vec{n})\times \vec{n}\right]
	\label{eq:wss_def}
\end{equation}
From an equation point of view, the WSS ($\tau_{wss}$) is a projection of the normal stresses onto the tangential plane of the vessel. To see the visual derivation of it see \autoref{fig:wss_theory}.
\begin{figure}[ht]
	\centering
	\input{chapter3/Figures/Meshing/WSS}
	\caption{WSS derivation.}
	\label{fig:wss_theory}
\end{figure}
In literature, it is common to find a different, scalar definition of WSS, \autoref{eq:wss_def_2}, which in the case of incompressibility corresponds exactly to the $L_2$ norm of $\tau_{wss}$.
\begin{equation}
	\norm{\vec{\tau}_{wss}}_2 = \mu\dot{\gamma}
	\label{eq:wss_def_2}
\end{equation}
This second definition often profits from the precomputed shear rate $\dot{\gamma}$ that is used for non-Newtonian flows and does not require computing the surface normals. When using the coarse scale solution of the VMS stabilized Navier-Stokes however, using this formulation can be indeed problematic, yielding undesired high WSS contributions. The reason for this lies in the fact that the coarse scale velocities are in general not divergence free, but equal a certain modelled subscale divergence. To provide a better picture of this, consider without loss of generality a surface patch like the one shown in \autoref{fig:wss_theory}, where the normal direction is $\vec{n}=\vec{e}_x$. The no-slip condition at walls enforces that the derivatives in tangential directions are zero, i.e. $\partial_y\vec{u}=0$ and $\partial_z\vec{u}=0$. Assume, that the fluid is not divergence-free by a factor $\epsilon$:
\begin{equation}
	\nabla \cdot \vec{u} = \partial_x u_x = \epsilon
\end{equation}
Then the original definition of WSS \eqref{eq:wss_def} still yields the expected contribution, since it filters out $\partial_x u_x$ in the first cross product operation
\begin{equation}
	\norm{\vec{n} \times \left[ (\sigma\cdot\vec{n})\times \vec{n}\right]}_2 = \mu\sqrt{ (\partial_x u_y)^2 + (\partial_x u_z)^2},
\end{equation}
whereas the shear rate definition carries the said divergence into the WSS:
\begin{equation}
	\mu\dot{\gamma} = \mu\sqrt{ \epsilon^2 + (\partial_x u_y)^2 + {(\partial_x u_z)}^2}
\end{equation}
Under these circumstances, it is recommended to use the vectorial definition of the WSS in place of the commonly used shear rate-dependent expression. Apart from alleviating mass continuity errors, it provides a resourceful quantity to measure direction in shearing, which is not possible otherwise. In fact, many relevant indicators, such as the Oscillatory Shear Stress (OSI)~\cite{Meng2014}, build upon vectorial wall shearing. For these reasons, the vectorial formulation is preserved. The first directly derived quantity of interest is the Time-Averaged WSS (TAWSS), expressing how large the exerted WSS is in an area on average (see \autoref{eqn:tawss}).
\begin{equation}
	\text{TAWSS} = \frac{1}{T}\int_T \norm{\vec{\tau}_{wss}}_2 \,dt
	\label{eqn:tawss}
\end{equation}
The mentioned OSI quantifies the oscillating character of the WSS. If it retains a consistent direction throughout a cardiac cycle, the OSI is zero. Alterations of its directions yield a growing OSI up to 0.5, its limit value (see \autoref{eqn:osi}).
\begin{equation}
	\text{OSI} = \frac{1}{2}\left[ 1 - \frac{\norm{\int_T \vec{\tau}_{wss} \,dt}_2}{\int_T \norm{\vec{\tau}_{wss}}_2 \,dt} \right]
	\label{eqn:osi}
\end{equation}
Another family of indicators has been developed around the stability of WSS~\cite{Morbiducci2020, Mazzi2020}, \ie~the structure around points of vanishing WSS, as well as contracting and expanding manifolds. The latter analysis, related to the study of dynamical systems, identifies whether a vessel patch is stretched, compressed or twisted by shearing forces. While stability of WSS has not been yet assessed in the same depth as other remodelling indicators related to IA, it will be considered in the analysis of a giant aneurysm as an informative quantity. More precisely, the Jacobian of the vectorial WSS will be computed and its eigenvalues will be analyzed following classifications of stability points.
\begin{equation}
	\vec{\lambda}_{wss} = \text{eig}\left(\nabla \vec{\tau}_{wss}\right)
\end{equation}
The derivatives of the WSS require in theory second derivatives, which for first order linear elements are 0. We overcome this limitation by reinterpolating the velocity gradients onto the nodes using a Clément-type operator.

A proper computation of the flow indicators depends on the numerical aspects of the computation, but also on the physiological compatibility between the simulated case and the real phenomena occurring in the human body. One keystone is imposing Boundary Conditions (BCs) that resemble reasonably well, the flow at the segments delimiting the computation domain. These belong to either inlets or outlets and need to be considered separately, due to their differing implications and determining factors.


\subsection{Inlet Boundary Conditions}\label{chap3:sec2:subsec:2}
The in-going mass flow depends on several patient-specific characteristics, such as age, physical state and the presence of cardiovascular complications, \eg~hypertension. In principal, it is possible to record the blood flow velocity in the larger arteries leading to the brain, \ie~ICAs, using either Doppler-Ultrasound velocimetry or 4D flow MRI.

\paragraph{Doppler-Ultrasound velocimetry} measures the velocity of blood flow or other moving structures within the body using the Doppler effect. The technique involves a transducer that emits high-frequency ultrasound waves into the body. When these waves encounter moving objects, like red blood cells, they are reflected back to the transducer with a change in frequency. This frequency shift, known as the Doppler shift, is directly proportional to the velocity of the moving object. By analysing the frequency changes of the reflected waves, the ultrasound system can calculate and display the speed and direction of blood flow. This technique is widely used when analysing stenosis and other blockages in the upper body and limbs. In the crane however, applying Doppler-Ultrasound is more complicated due to the bony structures in its way. Also, the signal can be perturbed by any other larger vessel that lays between the target artery and the transducer.

\paragraph{4D Flow MRI} utilises, as the name suggest, an MRI scanner to retrieve estimates about the flow velocity in one or several planes. For each of the planes, the scanner retrieves the signal over several cardiac cycles to filter out noise. For that reason, patients need to remain still for an extended period of time, making it a seldomly used technique in medical the medical field. In studies where 4D flow is used, resolutions have been reported to be around 1/3 of the ones obtained in TOF MRI using the same device~\cite{Berg2014}. Due to the reduced resolution, mass and momentum conservation are not assured and neither is the quality of the estimations in recirculation zones. Nevertheless, the technique provides an unbiased profile of the fluxes in the brain that doesn't rely on assumptions related to boundary conditions of blood rheology. Much work is invested lately into the evaluation and development of 4D flow MRI using Machine Learning to enhance the estimations and reduce scanning times~\cite{Harloff2013, Rutkowski2021, Cherry2022}.

\paragraph{The influence of the inflow waveform}
Since neither of the options are generally available, studies usually fall back onto generalised waveforms obtained from large populations. Ideally, these generalised curves are given for separate groups, \eg~age groups, to better fit specific characteristics\cite{Hoi2011}. Several publications have devoted time to analysing the discrepancies between using generalised curves and patient-specific ones. In 2014 Ahn \etal~\cite{I.G.H.2014} and Ahn \etal~\cite{Ahn2014} conducted independent studies with partially contradicting outcomes. In the prior a small population was the basis of simulations using different inflow waveforms with the same volume found in literature. The authors found only significant alterations in the OSI magnitudes. On the other hand Jansen~\cite{I.G.H.2014} found that in the 36 treated patient cases, for which 2D phase-contrast MR recordings were available, substantial differences were the result of different inflow waveforms. In the last, and largest study by Li \etal~\cite{Li2020} 148 patients were considered. Transcranial Doppler sonography recordings were taken of the patients and used to run simulations of the IA in their brain arterial system. The same simulations, as in the previous studies, were compared to ones run with generalised profiles. The study concluded that even though significant differences in the flow could be witnessed, they were not sufficiently relevant to influence the rupture risk assessment. The latter study strikes a middle ground between its two predecessors and keeps a certain degree of confidence in the use of generalised data. It is also important to note that the cardiac output is a quantity subject to many alterations in the body, such as the state of activity, anemic events and environmental changes. In that sense, it is nearly impossible to strike a "perfect" result, underlining that hemodynamic simulations are an approximation of the complex and transient phenomena in the brain.

Considering the presented studies and under the absence of direct measurements for the patients considered in this work, generalized curves are adopted. A waveform of the ICA obtained in the cooperating medical institution was taken as a baseline, since age and other informations were not present. However, the MR scans revealed the whole structure of the CoW which permitted scaling the waveform according to the indications from~\cite{Tanaka2006}. In the cited work, 105 healthy, young individuals are examined in regards to the flow rates in the ICAs and BA considering posterior or anterior hypoplasia. This permitted generating indications for the flow split among suppliers in the absence of a complete CoW. An example for the volume flow curve alongside another age-based curve~\cite{Hoi2011} are given in \autoref{fig:waveforms}.
\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{chapter3/Figures/Waveforms/waveform_comparison.pdf}
	\caption[Generalized Inflow Waveforms]{Two waveforms $\dot{Q}_1$~\cite{Tanaka2006} and $\dot{Q}_2$~\cite{Hoi2011} used when the structure of the CoW is known and when the individual is older, respectively. 	\label{fig:waveforms}

The volumetric flow can be imposed with varying degrees of complexity. The first, and most simple is using a plug profile over the inlet. If a sufficiently long portion of vasculature, ideally containing turns~\cite{Hodis2015}, is used, this method may be applied~\cite{Berg2019}. Its main advantage is that it does not require a circular inlet to be implemented and can instead be applied to any surface. Another more realistic approach is the use of a parabolic profile:
\begin{equation}
	\vec{u}_{in} = -\vec{n}_{in}\frac{2\dot{Q}}{\pi R^2}\left(1-\frac{r^2}{R^2}\right)
\end{equation}
The profile bases on a fully developed Poiseuille flow, \ie~for a stationary viscous flow in a circular pipe. Therefore, it needs to be applied on circular inlet of radius $R$, where $r$ is the distance to the circle's center. Note that the normal vector $\vec{n}_{in}$ is defined outwards, like all other outflows, and thus requires to be inverted for the profile here. This profile is due to its smooth progression and simple implementation one of the most frequent choices in IA literature~\cite{Vignon-Clementel2006, Lynch2022}. The last approach found in common IA literature is based on theory of oscillating blood flow described by Womersley in 1957~\cite{Womersley1957}. Here, not only the magnitude, but also the inflow profile vary across time, mimicking the behaviour of a viscous fluid in a circular pipe under oscillating inflow pressures. While arguably being the most physiologically compatible flow of the presented methods, it requires some more implementation steps as well as circular profiles, much like the parabolic profile. For the present work, Womersley profiles have not been used due to a negligible impact on IA hemodynamics~\cite{Berg2015} while implicating an increase in complexity of the simulations.


\subsection{Outlet Boundary Conditions}\label{chap3:sec2:subsec:4}
Outlet boundary conditions in the brain arterial system are overall difficult to model and calibrate. Contrary to, the aorta and its branches, the vessels branching off the CoW are small and measuring flow velocities in them is nearly impossible. The main difference with respect to the inflow, is that in general there are many more outgoing vessels at different heights of the vascular tree. Therefore, it is necessary to devise a strategy to split the flow correctly throughout the sinks of the system.

\paragraph{Zero-pressure outlet conditions} are the most common choice in IA literature~\cite{Berg2019_challenge, Valen-Sendstad2018}. This effectively renders the flow distribution proportional to the resistance along the paths to each vessel. Aside from the fact that no physiological law supports this BC, it implies that the flow split becomes dependent on the length of the outlet extrusions or the outlet cut-offs, and the choice of viscosity model. This can be seen by simply modelling the flow in a network with a 0D model, much like an electrical circuit. If a ground is applied at each end, the sole factor directing the flow through the network is the resistance along each path. However, it is expected that the dominant contribution to the resistance stems actually from the smaller vascular bed of arterioles and capillaries found past the limits of our computation models. This poses a direct contradiction with the zero-pressure law, raising criticism from many\cite{Berg2019_challenge, Chnafa2018, Chnafa2017, Vignon-Clementel2006}. Another indirect drawback, is that the overall pressures in the system are not adapted to the ones found in reality. While this does not directly affect CFD simulations, it does yield utterly wrong results when wall compliance is considered. A considerable leap in development was struck in 2006 by Vignon-Clementel who developed a reduced-order model for the outlets.

\paragraph{Reduced-order outlet model} extends the regular 3D domain by 1D domains in which simplified versions of the Navier-Stokes equations are solved analytically. As a result from the derivation, the pressure is set as a function of the current volumetric flow times a resistance. In the most simple execution, the resistance is a constant $R$, much like in an established Poiseuille flow.
\begin{equation}
	p_{out} = R\int_{\Gamma_{out}} \vec{u} \cdot \vec{n}\,dA
	\label{eqn:pqr}
\end{equation}
If vessel compliance is considered, the resulting pressure is no longer linearly dependent of the volume flow, but is subject to an impedance $Z(t)$. The impedance models the shift of the outgoing flow that is caused by the capacity of arteries to elastically deform due to a sudden rise in pressure.
\begin{equation}
	p_{out} = \frac{1}{T} \int_{t-\tau}^{t}Z(t-\tau) \int_{\Gamma_{out}} \vec{u} \cdot \vec{n}\,dA\,d\tau
\end{equation}
The latter BC is commonly referred to as a 2-element Windkessel model. The denomination \textit{Windkessel} stems stems from German and means wind kettle. It was used to describe water circuits with integrated air chambers that acted as a dampening volume absorbing shocks from the abrupt changes of flow in the system. In the context of the heart, having compliance outlet conditions is seen as a critical requirement. There, as described above, arteries absorb the shocks emitted by the heart contractions and smooth them out to ensure a continuous flow. In brain per contrary, the arteries lack much of the elasticity and instead have more developed smooth muscle cells in the media\cite{Liebeskind2016}. This renders compliance small, which can be seen in dynamic CT images. Note that the same does not necessarily apply to diseased aneurysm tissue, as is known from literature and will be shown in the last chapter of this thesis. In summary, from the presented boundary conditions it is assumed that a linear resistance together with the volume flow rate can provide an effective way of imposing outflow pressures. The remaining question to solve is how to determine this resistance.

\paragraph{The Principle of Minimum Work} also called Murray's Law, describing a natural law of branchings can be exploited to estimate the split. In the course of evolution, humans have developed an optimized branching pattern in arteries that minimizes the overall maintenance $P_s$ and transport cost $P_t$~\cite{Helthuis2019, Keelan2021}. One can think of the maintainance cost as a penalization to having just few large vessels, since blood needs to be pushed through capillaries to effectively exchange nutrition and waste products. One way of expressing the transport cost is through Poiseuilles law:
\begin{equation}
	P_t = \dot{Q}\Delta p = \frac{8\mu l \dot{Q}^2}{\pi r^4}
\end{equation}
The storage work grows proportionally to the volume of blood held by an idealised vessel, which is multiplied by an volumetric cost $\lambda$, usually set as $\lambda = 648$\,J/($m^3$s)~\cite{Keelan2019}.
\begin{equation}
	P_s=\lambda V = \lambda l\pi r^2
\end{equation}
To retrieve the optimal radius, the minimizer in \eqref{eq:minim_murray} is derived with respect to $r$. Note, that the sum of terms with a dependency $r^{-4}$ and $r^{2}$ has one local minimum for $r>0$.
\begin{align}
	\min_r P_t + P_s = \min_r \left(\frac{8\mu l \dot{Q}^2}{\pi r^4} + \lambda l\pi r^2\right)\label{eq:minim_murray} \\[0.2em]
	\frac{\partial}{\partial r}(P_t + P_s) = -\frac{32\mu l \dot{Q}^2}{\pi r^5} + 2\lambda l\pi r = 0                 \\[0.2em]
	\dot{Q} = r^3\sqrt{\frac{\lambda\pi^2}{16\mu}}
\end{align}
For a given bifurcation with an inflow $\dot{Q}_{in}$ and several outflows $\dot{Q}_{i}$, Murray's law is exploited using the flux balance:
\begin{align}
	\frac{\dot{Q}_{1}}{\dot{Q}_{2}} & = \frac{r_1^3}{r_2^3}                                                                            \\
	\dot{Q}_{in}                    & = \sum_i \dot{Q}_{i} = 1 + \frac{r_2^3}{r_1^3}\dot{Q}_{1} + \frac{r_3^3}{r_1^3}\dot{Q}_{1} + ... \\
	                                & =\frac{\sum_i r_i^3}{r_1^3}\dot{Q}_{1}
\end{align}
For any outlet $k$ and a general exponent $n$, Murray's law fixes average flow over a cardiac cycle as:
\begin{equation}
	\dot{Q}_{k} =\frac{r_k^n}{\sum_i r_i^n}\dot{Q}_{in}
	\label{eqn:basic_murray}
\end{equation}
Under the previously provided assumptions, Murray's law establishes a cubic proportionality between the steady-state volumetric flow and the vessel radius. Varying assumptions on flow properties or changing the dimensionality of the problem results in other exponents, e.g. 2D Poiseulle flow has $n=5/2$, effects of turbulence and compliance yield $n \approx 2$~\cite{Keelan2021}. Murray's law has been confirmed experimentally by exhaustive \textit{post-mortem} examinations of human brains by Helthuis \etal~\cite{Helthuis2019}. By measuring the luminal radius in large series of bifurcations it was confirmed that an exponent close to 3 rules the branching patterns. Depending on each individual and the overall size of the bifurcation that is examined, the coefficient may vary between $n=2$ and $n=3.5$. In simulated hemodynamics literature, index 3 is a common choice since it bases on the same assumptions than plain resistance boundary conditions, making it a consistent choice~\cite{Marsden2008}. Several publications have studied the impact of the exponent 2 and 3 for IA simulations~\cite{Saalfeld2019, Chnafa2018}, confirming that $n=3$ promotes flow in larger vessels with respect to $n=2$. Nevertheless, since no measurement data was available, neither of the choices could be prioritised over the other one.

Another studied aspect of the law is its locality, or in other words, what is considered one bifurcation. In \autoref{eqn:basic_murray} all outflows of a system were considered at a time, but one may arguably consider the mass balance individually at each bifurcation instead, as done in~\cite{Chnafa2018}. In case of a perfectly Murray-compliant network, both approaches yield the same outcome. When Murray's law is not met the outcomes vary. Consider for instance an unrealistic network, in which all vessels have the same diameter. In that case the flow drastically increases in proximal outlets and diminishes exponentially on distal parts based on the number of bifurcations on its path.

One particular issue arising from this local Murray's split concerns communicating vessels. These vessels, as described before, serve the purpose of linking the anterior and posterior circulations, providing alternative pathways for the blood in case of ischaemia\footnote{Ischaemia: Inadequate blood supply to an organ or part of the body.}~\cite{Liebeskind2016}. There, the local law fails in any case, since depending from which direction the bifurcations are swept through, the communicating vessels will have either one direction or another one. It is also problematic to delimit the vascular network at such a vessel, since their flow direction can even be inverted between individuals~\cite{Zhang2016}. Hence, Murray's law as well as plain zero-stress boundary conditions are likely to alter results considerably in that scenario. To our surprise, even though 60--70\% of all IA at the CoW are situated at communicating arteries~\cite{Hacein-Bey2011}, no previous publications (to the authors best knowledge) has mentioned or discussed this question. Most works studying IA at the carotid arteries do not describe the general topology of the CoW and the absence or hypoplasia of communicating segments. There are cases defining the PCom as a regular system outlet~\cite{Chnafa2017, Chnafa2018} and others neglecting it~\cite{Castro2006, Dennis2017}. This motivates the contribution in which we attempt evaluate and estimate the implications of using Murray's law in patient cases with IA at posterior communicating arteries.

\subsection{Case-specific Methods}
At the time of the study the rheology was modelled through the Carreau model with generalized values~\cite{MBehr2005, Xiang2012} in the absence of the patient's hematocrit values. The law is described by \autoref{eq:carreau}. The constants $\rho\,$\,=\,1056\,kg/m$^3$, $\mu_0$\,=\,0.0456\pas, $\mu_\infty$\,=\,0.0032\pas, $\lambda$\,=\,10.03\,s, $n$\,=\,0.344 and the shear rate $\dot{\gamma}$ were employed~\cite{Gambaruto2011}.
\begin{equation}
	\mu(\dot{\gamma}) = \mu_\infty + (\mu_0 - \mu_\infty)\left( 1+(\lambda\dot{\gamma})^2 \right)^{(n-1)/2}
	\label{eq:carreau}
\end{equation}
Inflow conditions are described with the help of a generalized volumetric flow curve ($\dot{Q}_1$ from \autoref{fig:waveforms}) which has been scaled and split among the supplying arteries. More precisely, in case A each ICA contributes 44\% of the total volumetric flow while the BA carries only 12\% due to its small size~\cite{Tanaka2006}. Cases B and C follow a split of 40\%-20\% between each ICA and the BA. The flow rate $Q$ is imposed via parabolic velocity profiles at the base of the arterial system. The walls are considered fully rigid and are thus set through no-slip conditions. As previously explained, the boundary conditions are set using the linear resistance models (\autoref{eqn:pqr}) and the target volumetric flow is set though Murray's law with exponent $n=3$. The resistances needed to map the targeted flow rate to pressures are computed in an iterative process. A naive gradient descend algorithm has been implemented to regularly adapt resistances to minimize the per-cardiac-cycle volume flow error. This procedure was executed on the 9 simulations for the study, achieving volume flows within 5\% of the targeted quantity. An example for the outcome of the fitting can be seen in \autoref{fig:flow_rates}. To wash out the initial transient and establish a periodic solution, 3 cardiac cycles have been computed for each case due to the complexity of the flow in the \textit{Full} simulations. The last cardiac cycle was used in the analysis of the results.

\subsubsection{Global flow distribution}
The overall flow distribution can be visually followed from the velocity magnitudes shown in \autoref{fig:volume}. Patient A stands out due to slower flow velocities despite similar tributary flows, which is a direct consequence from having overall larger vessels compared to B and C. The plot highlights regions of high flow speeds as for instance in the terminal ICA bifurcation. The highly inertia driven flow turns mostly into the MCAs, which both due to their size and the Murray exponent (n=3), carry the largest outflow. The BA, when present, supplies the posterior circulation of the brain without directly irrigating the anterior circulation. Therefore, the flux traversing the PCom is strictly unidirectional carrying blood away from the ICA towards the PCA. Patient B and C feed, according to our models, the distal PCA mostly (Patient C) or exclusively (Patient B) through the PCom due to their small or fetal P1 segments.

\subsection[Study Discussion]{Discussion}
The resulting flow in the CoW resembles literature values~\cite{Zarrinkoob2015} with a tendency to overestimate the flow through the MCAs. Using an exponent of (n=2) in Murray's law reduces the discrepancy for the flow in the MCA at the cost of overestimating the flows in smaller vessels, such as the OA. The changes in the flow through the ACA between the different computational domain extensions are a direct consequence of Murray's law being applied on vessels of varying diameter. The proximal ACA (A1) has, in all cases, a larger radius than its post-communicating segment (A2), consequently leading to different flow rates. However, this apparent inconsistency is caused by perforator vessels around the ACom, such as the Recurrent Artery of Heubner, which, due to their small size, cannot be properly simulated. Experimental studies~\cite{Zarrinkoob2015} support the observed decrease in flow rates. In our cases, applying Murray's law has shown a better agreement in ACA and MCA flowrates with the said study when setting the domain boundary in the A1 segment.

The further analysis of the hemodynamics in the IA has shown differences that exceed expectations, comparable to those seen in simulation challenges~\cite{Berg2018, Berg2019, Valen-Sendstad2018}. The \textit{Simplified} strategy lacks physical reasoning since all of the patient geometries in this study exhibit PCom arteries. Simulations confirm that neglecting these significantly alters the intrasaccular hemodynamics, confirming the initial intuition. Without further exploration of this strategy, we conclude that it is unsuitable for the presented vasculatures. The \textit{Middle} extension, commonly found in literature, presents more similarities with \textit{Full} CoW simulations. However, moderate changes in hemodynamics sometimes result in magnified alterations in WSS. These differences may lead to varied correlations between flow dynamics and the aneurysm evolution~\cite{Valen-Sendstad2014}. Causes for these observations included changed boundary flow rates on one side and the lack of interactions with the opposing hemisphere and the posterior circulation on the other.

The additional blood suppliers in the case of \textit{Full} simulations, such as the neighbouring ICA and the BA, are participants in causing blood to traverse communicating vessels into other circulation segments. Although most of the interhemispheric flow was observed at the ACom, hence distal to the aneurysm fundus, the influence of the inflow streams cannot be generally disregarded from being important~\cite{Castro2006}. In our study, equal flow rates for the ICAs were imposed, aligning with a popular choice shown in large scale studies~\cite{Valen-Sendstad2018} due to a lack of measurement data. As stated before, this is irrefutably one of the biases taken into consideration that could be lifted by measurements in follow-up work. The second factor causing altered results between the \textit{Full} CoW and \textit{Middle} simulations is, as stated, the interaction between both anterior and posterior circulations. By not imposing a boundary midway in a posterior communicating artery, more slack is left for the flow to unravel its natural characteristics thereby providing a more physiological hemodynamic profile. Examples of this, among others, are pressure drops at posterior arterial junctions and bifurcations that effectively alter upstream pressure distributions and are of notable relevance~\cite{Mynard2015, Chnafa2017}.

The flow in these communicating vessels holds significant medical importance, given that they host 60--70\% of all IAs on the Circle of Willis, which itself constitutes 85\% of all IAs~\cite{Hacein11}. While the role of CFD in diagnosing and analyzing IA growth and remodeling processes remains uncertain, it is crucial to acknowledge that modeling choices significantly impact the hemodynamics of communicating vessels~\cite{Zhang2016}. Moreover, statistical evidence indicates that the absence or hypoplasia of certain communicating segments is associated with a higher likelihood of developing IAs~\cite{Hindenes2023, Feng2023}. As such, it becomes imperative to continually evaluate and refine CFD models to capture the hemodynamics of the CoW more effectively. One promising outlook is the use of perfusion imaging~\cite{Zhang2016, Schollenberger2021}. Thanks to a quantification of the perfusion of different brain parts, the flow into different sections of the brain can be mapped through a brain atlas, giving direct feedback about the flow traversing the outlets of the CoW. While this method is still in its infancy and requires an elaborate MRI procedure, it holds the potential to provide a more accurate boundary condition for CFD simulations.


\section{Conclusion}
In this chapter, we have presented the brain's anatomy and how to translate its complex hemodynamics into a numerical framework, including details of mesh generation and choice of boundary conditions. Based on the latter subject, we have assessed the implications of Murray's law on the hemodynamics of intracranial aneurysms when communicating vessels are in play. We have shown that substatial differences can arise when Murray's law is applied to a vessel network considering various extensions. Unfortunately, since no groud truth was available, no preferred domain extension could be recommended.

The obtained results lead us to recommend to take into account the overall anatomy of each patient and consider possible implications of the flow from contralateral or posterior circulations. By confirming hypoplasia or absence of communicating segments, studies can further reinforce their choice of BCs or motivate a sensitity study otherwise. Information about the presence of communicating arteries can be obtained from MR angiography, which is a standard procedure in the diagnosis of IAs.
