\chapter{Numerical Modelling of Hemodynamics}
\minitoc\newpage\label{chap.2}
In the following chapter, the incompressible, single-phase Navier-Stokes equations are introduced as the backbone to the hemodynamics simulations that are run. The equations are derived from the conservation of mass and momentum and are further reformulated in a weak sense. The obtained formulation is then extended with a Variational Multiscale-type Method (VMS) to stabilize the schemes in face of same-order test and trial functions for pressure and velocities. This additionally controls the otherwise ocurrring spurious oscillations caused by convection dominated flows, such as the present ones. The methodology is explained and validated on a series of conventional test cases in 2D and 3D. The chapter is concluded with a discussion on the numerical results and the implications of the findings.
\section{Governing Physics}\label{chap2:sec1}
The Navier-Stokes equations stem, as mentioned before from the conservation of mass and momentum in an open and bounded domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb {R}^{n}$ in which no material enters or leaves. For the former quantity, the conservation can be expressed through:
\begin{equation}
	\frac{d}{dt} \int_{\Omega(t)} \rho dV = 0,
\end{equation}
imposing that the integral of over the density in the control volume does not change.

\subsection{Reynolds Transport Theorem}\label{chap2:sec1:subsec1}
The derivative over the time-dependent domain can be simplified thanks to the Reynolds transport theorem, named after its discoverer Osborne Reynolds.

\begin{theorem}[Reynolds Transport Theorem]
	Let $\vec{f}(t, \vec{x})$ be any conservation quantity, then the Reynolds transport theorem states that for a velocity field $\vec{u}$, the following holds:
	\begin{equation}
		\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega (t)}\vec {f} \,dV=\int_{\Omega (t)}{\frac{\partial \vec {f} }{\partial t}} + \nabla\cdot(\vec{u}\otimes\vec{f}) \,dV
	\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}

	\begin{equation}
		\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega (t)}\vec {f}(t, \vec{x}) \,dV = \lim_{\Delta t\to 0} \frac{1}{\Delta t} \left(\int_{\Omega (t+\Delta t, \vec{x})}\vec{f}(t+\Delta t, \vec{x}) \,dV-\int_{\Omega (t)}\vec{f}(t, \vec{x}) \,dV\right)
	\end{equation}
	To drag the derivative into the integral it is necessary to reformulate the problem in terms of a initial position $\vec{X}$ and a mapping $\vec{x}=\vec{\phi}(t, \vec{X})$. With the corresponding derivative $\nabla\vec{\phi} = F(t, \vec{X})$ and the Jacobian $J = \det F$, the integral can be rewritten written in terms of the initial domain $\Omega(0)$ and the contents can therefore be merged:

	\begin{align}
		\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega(t)}\vec{f}\,dV & = \lim_{\Delta t\to 0} \frac{1}{\Delta t} \int_{\Omega (0)}\left[(\vec{f}\circ\vec{\phi})J\right](t+\Delta t, \vec{X}) - \left[(\vec{f}\circ\vec{\phi})J\right](t, \vec{X})\,dV \\
		                                        & = \int_{\Omega (0)} \left[J\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\vec{f}\circ\vec{\phi}) + (\vec{f}\circ\vec{\phi})\frac{\partial J}{\partial t}\right](t, \vec{X})\,dV
	\end{align}
	To simplify the derivative of the Jacobian, recall Jacobi's formula.
	\begin{equation}
		\frac{\partial J}{\partial t} = J\tr\left(F^{-1}\frac{\partial F}{\partial t}\right)= J\tr\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\vec{x}}\frac{\partial \vec{\phi}}{\partial t}\right) = J(\nabla\cdot\vec{u})
	\end{equation}
	Now the integral can be written back in terms of a time-dependent domain.
	\begin{equation}
		\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega (t)}\vec {f} \,dV = \int_{\Omega (t)}\frac{\partial\vec{f}}{\partial t} + (\vec{u}\cdot\nabla)\vec{f} + \vec{f}(\nabla\cdot\vec{u})\,dV = \int_{\Omega (t)}\frac{\partial\vec{f}}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(\vec{u}\otimes\vec{f})\,dV
	\end{equation}
\end{proof}
From now on all functions are taken to be time-dependent, as well as dependent on a location $\vec{x}$ in an eulerian framework, unless stated otherwise. The explicit dependencies are omitted for the sake of brevity. Also, the derivatives in time will be denoted by $\partial_{t}$.

\subsection{Conservation Equations}\label{chap2:sec1:subsec2}
Using the transport theorem, the conservation of mass can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}
	\int_\Omega \partial_{t}\rho + \nabla\cdot(\rho\vec{u})\,dV = 0
	\label{eq:massconservation_int}
\end{equation}
The present integral form can be reduced to a differential form with a short proof by contradiction. Suppose that the content of the integral from \autoref{eq:massconservation_int} is not zero in $\Omega$, then the integral would be adding positive and negative contributions over the domain. However, since the choice of $\Omega$ is arbitrary, the integral formulation has to hold for any subdomain of $\Omega$, including those in which the integrand is non-zero. With the contradiction, the integrand has to be zero in $\Omega$ and the differential form is obtained:
\begin{equation}
	\partial_{t}\rho + \nabla\cdot(\rho\vec{u}) = 0
	\label{eq:massconservation}
\end{equation}
In the same manner, the conservation of momentum is derived from preserving the volume-specific momentum $\rho\vec{u}$, which is modulated by body forces, such as the gravity, and stresses. The latter are the result of the interactions between the fluid particles and are expressed through the stress tensor $\sigma$. The conservation of momentum is then:
\begin{equation}
	\partial_t \rho\vec{u} + \nabla\cdot(\rho\vec{u}\otimes\vec{u}) = \nabla\cdot\sigma + \rho\vec{g}
	\label{eq:momentumconservation_conservative}
\end{equation}
The conservative formulation shown in \autoref{eq:momentumconservation_conservative} can be rewritten into the convective form by using the product rule and the conservation of mass.
\begin{equation}
	\rho\partial_t\vec{u} + \rho(\vec{u}\cdot\nabla)\vec{u} = \nabla\cdot\sigma + \rho\vec{g}
	\label{eq:momentumconservation_convective}
\end{equation}
The stress tensor $\sigma$ is taken to be the:\\
\begin{equation}
	\sigma =-pI+\lambda(\nabla \cdot \vec{u})I+ 2\mu \varepsilon(\vec{u})
	\label{eq:stress}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	\varepsilon(\vec{u}) = \frac{1}{2}(\nabla \vec{u}+\nabla \vec{u}^T)
	\label{eq:rate_of_strain}
\end{equation}

\begin{table}[ht]
	\centering
	\begin{tabular}{c|c|c}
		$p$       & Pressure          & \pa  \\
		$\lambda$ & Second viscosity  & \pas \\
		$\mu$     & Dynamic viscosity & \pas \\
	\end{tabular}
	\caption{Physical quantities of stress tensor.}
\end{table}
As will be more thoroughly discussed in the coming section, blood is incompressible at physiological pressures and temperatures, implying $\rho=const$. Therefore, one may proceed with the simplification of the momentum and mass equations:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
		\rho\left(\partial_t\vec{u} + (\vec{u}\cdot\nabla)\vec{u} - \vec{g}\right) &= -\nabla p + \nabla \cdot 2\mu \varepsilon(\vec{u})\\
		\nabla\cdot \vec{u} &= 0
	\end{split}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Boundary Conditions}\label{chap2:sec1:subsec3}
Boundary conditions have been identified by several publications and computing challenges as sensitive components of hemodynamics simulations~\cite{Berg2019, Valen-Sendstad2018}. For that reason, Chapter 3 dedicates a full section to inlet and outlet BCs, providing both measurement-based techniques and heuristics that depend on the vascular topology. Additionally, the modelling effects of using rigid walls versus compliant ones will be shown based on existing literature. In Chapter 4, more detail will be provided on the modelling of endoluminal devices. For now, they will be just shortly introduced in order to carry on with the derivation of the numerical framework.\\

The domain boundaries for segments of the brain arterial system are divided into four groups: inlets $\Gamma_{in}$, outlets $\Gamma_{out}$, walls $\Gamma_{w}$ and endoluminal devices $\Gamma_{fd}$. In general terms, the inflow conditions, \ie~the blood supplied to the portion of the vasculature that is simulated, is imposed through a time-varying velocity profile. In mathematical terms speaking, it is expressed as:
\begin{equation}
	\vec{u} = \vec{u}_{in}, \quad (\vec{x}, t) \in \Gamma_{in} \times (0,T]
\end{equation}
Outflow conditions on the other hand are related to the flow split caused by the resistance and capacitance of the distal vasculature. The split can be either modelled as an imposed volumetric flow or a normal stress. For now, we will stick to the latter:
\begin{equation}
	\sigma \cdot \vec{n} = \vec{h}, \quad (\vec{x}, t) \in \Gamma_{out} \times (0,T]
\end{equation}
Arterial walls and endoluminal devices are modelled as rigid, no-slip boundaries. Therefore, in an eulerian framework, the velocity at the vessel wall and the interface of any implanted device will be dictated by:
\begin{equation}
	\vec{u} = 0, \quad (\vec{x}, t) \in \Gamma_{w}\cup\Gamma_{fd}  \times (0,T]
\end{equation}
The boundary conditions presented so far can be divided into two groups, essential on $\Gamma_{d}$ and natural on $\Gamma_{n}$. These categories are relevant in Finite Element Analysis (FEA), since their imposition leads to different formulations of the problem. Therefore, inflow, walls and any endoluminal devices are essential, while the outflow is natural.\\

The initial conditions to the simulations can be set by previously solving a steady state simulation at the inflow rate of interest. Other approaches, involve solving a potential flow or simply setting the initial solution to zero. For any of the choices, it is required to run simulations for several cardiac cycles to ensure that the initial transient is washed out and a periodic state has been reached.
\begin{equation}
	\vec{u} = \vec{u}_0, \quad \vec{x} \in \Omega,\,t=0
\end{equation}


\subsection{Blood Rheology}\label{chap2:sec1:subsec4}
In the preceding section, the Navier-Stokes equations were silently introduced to describe blood as a single-phase flow. Blood however is a suspension consisting to approximately 45\% of cellular components, such as red (erythrocytes), white blood cells (leukocytes), and platelets. The remaining 55\% is blood plasma, a yellowish liquid composed of water, electrolytes, proteins, glucose, lipids, hormones and carbon dioxide. The high fraction of nearly incompressible fluids in blood at physiological conditions justifies modelling it as an incompressible medium at densities slightly above the ones of water ($\rho=1050kg/m^3$ at $310K$)~\cite{Suzuki2021}. Blood plasma exhibits a nearly Newtonian character~\cite{Nader2019} with dynamics viscosities only decreasing by about $0.04\mpas$ between a resting state ($\mu_0 = 1.27\mpas$) and a highly sheared one ($\mu_C =$ 1.23\mpas)~\cite{Rodrigues2022}. The cellular components however, especially the Red Blood Cells (RBCs), have a greater impact on the bulk viscosity of the emulsion. RBCs are characterized as biconcave disks of $8\mu$ diameter in a resting state~\cite{Nader2019, Valen-Sendstad2011}. The cells enclose a hemoglobin solution, the oxygen carrying protein, with a highly deformable membrane. When subjected to shearing forces, the RBCs are capable of reshaping to the point of fitting through capilaries, whose diameter (5$\mu m$) lies well below the size of RBCs~\cite{Nader2019}. Apart from their capabilities of strong deformations and alignment, the RBCs have the tendency to arrange in stacks due to reversible protein interactions when the flow characteristics permit it. These stacks form mostly, during low shearing and dissolve gradually when exposed to higher shearing, giving blood the characteristic shear-thinning behaviour. The stacks, or also called ``rouleaux'', pose a larger impediment to flow, thus yielding a larger friction than when the RBCs are scattered in the blood stream~\cite{Nader2019}. The alingment and reshaping of RBCs likewise influences the macroscopic flow of blood in ways that are not fully understood yet. Thesse effects are naturally scaled by the volume fraction of RBCs in the blood, also called the hematocrit, which commonly dwells around 44\% depending on sex and levels of hydration among others.

In face of the complexity of the blood rheology, a thixotropic and visco-elastic fluid, it is required to narrow down the range of accounted effects to the physically relevant ones in our environment. The latter is given by the main arterial system comprising vessels of diameters above approximately 1\,$mm$. There, the much larger channel size compared to RBCs and other cells permits the modelling of blood as a single phase fluid. The Fåhræus-Lindqvist effect~\cite{fareus1931} describing the dependency of the apparent viscosity on the vessel diameter can therefore be disregarded, since they are only relevant in vessels with diameters below 0.3mm. The flow in arteries is subject to larger shear rates $\dot{\gamma}$ than in arterioles, capilaries or veins overall, allowing us to focus on the behaviour of blood for $\dot{\gamma}>1s^{-1}$. In this regime, blood displays notable shear-thinning effects until $\dot{\gamma}<700s^{-1}$ from which it is usually seens as a Newtonian fluid~\cite{Fung1993}. One of many models that can cope with this behaviour is the well-known Casson model \autoref{eqn:casson}, in which $\tau_y$ representes the yield stress and $\mu_C$ the Casson viscosity.
\begin{equation}
	\label{eqn:casson}
	\mu = \frac{{\left(\sqrt{\tau_y} + \sqrt{\dot{\gamma}\mu_C}\right)}^2}{\dot{\gamma}}
\end{equation}
Another version of the Casson model was introduced to avoid a division by zero during numerical tests. It is called the modified Casson and it provides a simple ramp to the shear rate using a new model parameter $m>100s$~\cite{pham1994,Suzuki2021}.
\begin{equation}
	\label{eqn:modified_casson}
	\mu = {\left( \sqrt{\mu_C} + \sqrt{\tau_y\frac{(1-\exp{(-m\dot{\gamma})})}{\dot{\gamma}}} \right)}^2
\end{equation}
Apart from $m$, the values for $\tau_y$ and $\mu_C$ have been obtained during rheological tests in several publications, considering dependencies on the constitution of blood in each of the cases. One illustrative recent one~\cite{Suzuki2021}, provides the following values for the Casson model depending on the HTC (as \%) fitted on a total of 33 healthy patients:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
		\mu_C &= (0.073\cdot\text{HTC} + 0.599) \mpas\\
		\tau_y &= (0.888\cdot\text{HTC} - 23.753) \mpa
	\end{split}
\end{equation}
The curves were fit using whole blood at $310K$ on patients with IA\@. The HTC values found in the study range from 32\% to 44\%, and should therefore be taken with consideration for larger concentrations. In \autoref{fig:casson_model}, three examples using several HTC values (43\% \- common, 36\% \- lower threshold for women and 50\% \- upper threshold for men).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{chapter2/Figures/Plot_Visco/casson_model.pdf}
	\caption{Casson model for blood viscosity including the expectable shear-rate in the human brain arteries, as well as the Newtonian glycerol/water solution that is used as an analog during experiments.}
	\label{fig:casson_model}
\end{figure}
One source of uncertainty surrounding the modelling of blood, as suggested in~\cite{Suzuki2021}, is the effect of the anticoagulant, \eg\,heparin, needed for performing the measurements. While the anticoagulant can be more easily characterized, its effect on the complex fluid blood remains hard to describe with certainty.




\section{Finite Element Analysis for the incompressible Navier-Stokes Equations}\label{chap2:sec2}

To properly understand the content of the following section, it is important to get familiar with some basics required Finite Element Analysis (FEA) for those who are not yet familiar with them. Contrary to finite differences and volumes, where the strong form of the equations can be used to start the discretization, finite elements base themselves on a weak formulation of the problem.


As a reminder, the Sobolev space $H^1(\Omega)$ is the space of functions that belong to the space of square integrable functions $L^2(\Omega)$ and whose first derivatives are also square integrable. In more mathematical terms this can be written as:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
		H^1(\Omega) &= \left\{ v \in L^2(\Omega) \mid \nabla v \in L^2(\Omega) \right\}\\
		L^2(\Omega) &= \left\{ v \mid \int_{\Omega} v^2 \,dV < \infty \right\}
	\end{split}
\end{equation}


\subsection{Stability Issues}\label{chap2:sec2:subsec1}
One common question for all those starting to work with FE discretizations of the Navier-Stokes equations is why do most schemes include some sort of stabilization. The reason is twofold involving (i) first the compatibilities between the interpolations of velocity and pressures and secondly (ii) the effects of convection dominated flows. We will start by providing an ilustrative example of the first problem arising in the particular case of a P1-P1 coupling.

\subsection{Stable Interpolations in mixed problems}\label{chap2:sec2:subsec1:subsubsec1}
For showing possible problems arising from using the same order interpolations for velocities and pressures, we will consider the following simple example. Let us consider a 2D domain $\Omega\subset \mathbb{R}^2$ with a square shape and a uniform mesh of splitted rectangular elements (see \autoref{fig:LBB_Violation}). On that domain, it is sought to solve the Stokes problem with homogeneous boundary conditions:

\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
		\nabla p - \nabla\cdot2\mu\varepsilon(\vec{u}) &= \vec{f}\\
		\nabla \cdot \vec{u} &= 0
	\end{split}, \quad \vec{x} \in \Omega
	\label{eqn:short_stokes}
\end{equation}

Since the Stokes problem admits any constant being added to the pressure, it is said to be unique up to a constant. Therefore, to close the system an auxiliary condition is added constraining the integral constant to fulfill that the volume integral of the pressure is zero:
\begin{equation}
	\int_{\Omega} p \,dV = 0
	\label{eqn:auxiliary}
\end{equation}

The weak formulation of the problem is obtained by multiplying the equations with discrete test functions $\vec{v}_h$ and $q_h$ and integrating over $\Omega$. The domain itself has a valid partition $\Tau_h$ into triangular elements $K$ on which piecewise linear functions are defined following the classical $\mathbb{P}_1$-Lagrange elements.
\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
		\int_\Omega \vec{v}_h\cdot\nabla p_h - \vec{v}_h\cdot\left[\nabla\cdot2\mu\varepsilon(\vec{u}_h)\right] \,dV = \int_\Omega \vec{f}\,dV, \quad \vec{v}_h \in V_h&\\
		\int_\Omega q_h\nabla \cdot \vec{u}_h\,dV = 0, \quad q_h \in Q_h&\\
		V_h = {\{v_h \in H^1(\Omega) \mid v_{k|K} \in \mathbb{P}_1, \forall K\in \Tau_h\}}&^2\\
		Q_h = {\{q_h \in L^2(\Omega) \mid q_{k|K} \in \mathbb{P}_1, \forall K\in \Tau_h\}}&\\
	\end{split}\label{eqn:weak_stokes}
\end{equation}
Using the Gau\ss-divergence theorem and the homogeneous boundary conditions, this problem can be rewritten into:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
		\int_\Omega 2\mu\varepsilon(\vec{u}):\varepsilon(\vec{v})\,dV - \int_\Omega p\nabla\cdot\vec{v}\,dV &= \int_\Omega \vec{f}\cdot\vec{v}\,dV\\
		\int_\Omega q\nabla \cdot \vec{u}\,dV &= 0
	\end{split}\label{eqn:weak_stokes2}
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
	\centering
	\input{chapter2/Figures/LBB_Violation/Figure}
	\caption{2D unit square decomposed into triangular elements. The pressures }
	\label{fig:LBB_Violation}
\end{figure}

Assume now that $\hat{p}_h$ as shown in \autoref{fig:LBB_Violation} satisfies the relationship:
\begin{equation}
	\int_{\Omega} \hat{p}_h\nabla\cdot\vec{v}_h\,dV = 0, \quad \forall \vec{v}_h \in V_h
	\label{eqn:auxiliary2}
\end{equation}
Then it is clear that the solutions to the pressure of \autoref{eqn:weak_stokes2} are not unique, since any real-valued multiple of $\hat{p}_h$ would also satisfy \autoref{eqn:auxiliary2}. In other words, the pressure is not uniquely determined by the velocity field, since for any $\vec{v}_h$ the term vanishes. This can be easily verified by checking the veracity of $\hat{p}_h$ as a possible solution. First, it is clear that $\hat{p}_h\in Q_h$ and that the condition formulated in \autoref{eqn:auxiliary} is satisfied in all $K\in\Tau_h$.

\begin{equation}
	\int_{\Omega} \hat{p}_h \,dV = \sum_{K\in\Tau_h}	\int_{\Omega} \hat{p}_{h,K}\,dV = \sum_{K\in\Tau_h} (-1+0+1) * \Delta_K = 0
\end{equation}

Then, it remains showing that the proposed $\hat{p}_h$ is indeed in the kernel:

\begin{align}
	\int_{\Omega} \hat{p}_h\nabla\cdot\vec{v}_h\,dV & = \sum_{K\in\Tau_h}	\int_{\Omega} \hat{p}_{h,K}\nabla\cdot\vec{v}_{h,K}\,dV                                         \\
	                                                & = \sum_{K\in\Tau_h}\nabla\cdot\vec{v}_{h,K} \int_{\Omega} \hat{p}_{h,K}\,dV = 0, \quad \forall \vec{v}_{h} \in V_h
\end{align}
Therefore, we can see that for the given mesh there is no uniqueness to the solutions, posing a major problem. This phenomenon is due to the inherent compatibility of equal order interpolations on some meshes, although it is not necessary, that for all triangulations this pressure problem arises. This is more formally expressed through the Inf-Sup condition, also called the Ladyzhenskaya-Babuska-Brezzi (LBB) condition. The LBB condition is a necessary and sufficient condition for the stability of the mixed finite element method for the Stokes and other problems that take a similar shape. The LBB condition is met if there exist a mesh independent factor $\beta > 0$ such that the following inequality holds:

\begin{equation}
	\exists \beta > 0:\quad \inf_{q_h\in Q_h} \sup_{\vec{v}_h\in V_h} \frac{\int_{\Omega} q_h\nabla\cdot\vec{v}_h\,dV}{\norm{q_h}_{L^2}\norm{\vec{v}_h}_{H^1}} \geq \beta > 0
	\label{eqn:LBB}
\end{equation}

The condition \autoref{eqn:LBB} is satisfied inconditionally for some pairings, such as the Taylor-Hood (P2-P1), but clearly not for P1-P1 pairings. The stabilization techniques introduced later however mitigate this inherent issue.

\subsection{Convection dominated processes}\label{chap2:sec2:subsec1:subsubsec2}
Compared to the Stokes problem, the Navier-Stokes equations introduce the complexity of a non-linear convection term $(\vec{u}\cdot\nabla)\vec{u}$. In physical terms speaking it describes how certain information is carried by a velocity field throughout the domain. To understand the challenges it involves it suffices again to consider a much simpler example. Let us consider the stationary advection-diffusion equation in 1D for any scalar field $\varphi$ and a small constant diffusion coefficient $\varepsilon$. Take $u$ to be a constant positive velocity.
\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
		u \partial_x \varphi - \varepsilon\partial_{xx}\varphi= f\\
		\varphi(\Gamma_0) = \varphi(\Gamma_1) = 0
	\end{split}
	\label{eqn:advection_diffusion}
\end{equation}
For a better interpretation take $\varphi$ as to be a temperature that is convected through a pipe to transport heat between sources and sinks given by $f$. The boundaries are set to the reference temperature $0$. Here again we compute the weak formulation:
\begin{equation}
	\int_{\Omega} u\psi\partial_x\varphi + \varepsilon\partial_x\varphi\partial_x\psi\,dx = \int_{\Omega} f\psi\,dx, \quad \forall \psi\in H^1_0(\Omega)
	\label{eqn:weak_advection_diffusion}
\end{equation}
The bilinear operator $a(\cdot,\cdot)$ of \autoref{eqn:weak_advection_diffusion} can be written as:
\begin{equation}
	a(\varphi, \psi) = \int_{\Omega} u\psi\partial_x\varphi + \varepsilon\partial_x\varphi\partial_x\psi\,dx
	\label{eqn:bilinear_advection_diffusion}
\end{equation}
With this bilinear form, we are going to show that for the standart Galerkin discretization some necessary criteria for existence and uniqueness are not met when $\varepsilon\rightarrow0$. Before continuing, the Lax-Milgram theorem is stated:

\begin{theorem}[Lax-Milgram]
	Let $V$ be a Hilbert space and $a(\cdot,\cdot)$ a bilinear form on $V$ that satisfies:
	\begin{align*}
		\text{Continuity:} & \quad \exists C\in \mathbb{R}^+ : |a(u,v)| \leq C\norm{u}_V\norm{v}_V, \quad \forall u,v\in V \\
		\text{Coercivity:} & \quad \exists \alpha\in \mathbb{R}^+ : a(u,u) \geq \alpha\norm{u}_V^2, \quad \forall u\in V
	\end{align*}

	Then there exists a unique solution $u\in V$ for a bounded linear form $f(v)$ to the variational problem:
	\begin{equation*}
		a(u, v) = f(v), \quad \forall v\in V
	\end{equation*}
\end{theorem}
In the convection-diffusion equation, the coercivity condition reads as followed:
\begin{align}
	a(\varphi, \varphi) & = \int_{\Omega} u\varphi\partial_x\varphi + \varepsilon{(\partial_x\varphi)}^2\,dx                                 \\
	                    & = \int_{\Omega} \frac{u}{2}\partial_x\varphi^2 + \varepsilon{(\partial_x\varphi)}^2\,dx                            \\
	                    & = \frac{u}{2}{\left[\varphi^2\right]}^{\Gamma_1}_{\Gamma_0} + \int_{\Omega} \varepsilon{(\partial_x\varphi)}^2\,dx \\
	                    & = \varepsilon \norm{\varphi}^2_{H^1(\Omega)}
\end{align}
Therefore, for $\varepsilon\rightarrow0$ or in other words, for pure convection the coercivity condition is not met and uniqueness and existence cannot be guaranteed. This phenomenon can also be observed in finite differences when central schemes are used for the space discretization of convective terms. In fact, a discretization with central finite differences of the convection-diffusion problem will lead to the same linear system with first order Lagrange elements. For that reason, the FEA approach is oftentimes seen as a central scheme. In finite difference discretizations, the problem is usually tackled by exchanging higher order central schemes by upwind schemes. These, as the name suggests, employ the data found upwind of the flow velocity to discretize spatial gradients.
\begin{equation}
	\partial_x\varphi =
	\begin{cases} \frac{\varphi(x) - \varphi(x-\Delta x)}{\Delta x}, \quad u > 0 \\
		\frac{\varphi(x+\Delta x) - \varphi(x)}{\Delta x}, \quad \text{else}
	\end{cases} +\mathcal{O}(\Delta x)
\end{equation}
An interesting note is, that these schemes can also be interpreted as central differences with added diffusion terms. Consider for example the case $u>0$:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
		\partial_x\varphi &= \frac{\varphi(x+\Delta x) - \varphi(x-\Delta x)}{2\Delta x} + \mathcal{O}(\Delta x^2)\\ &\approx \frac{\varphi(x) - \varphi(x-\Delta x)}{\Delta x} + \frac{\Delta x}{2}\left(\frac{\varphi(x+\Delta x) - 2\varphi(x) + \varphi(x - \Delta x)}{\Delta x^2}\right)\\
		&= \frac{\varphi(x) - \varphi(x-\Delta x)}{\Delta x}  + \mathcal{O}(\Delta x)\\
		\partial_{x}^{UD}\varphi &= \partial_{x}\varphi - \varepsilon\partial_{xx}\varphi, \quad \varepsilon = \frac{\Delta x}{2}
	\end{split}
	\label{eqn:upwind}
\end{equation}
Thus, a first order upwinding scheme can be seen as a central scheme with an added diffusion term of the order $\varepsilon$ to satisfy the coercivity constraint. Luckily, the VMS-type stabilization that we will employ later does also tackle this problem. While the compatibility constraint is aleviated through a relaxation of the mass balance, the convection phenomena are handled by a resulting shift in the test function. This shift, as will be seen later, is proportional to the convective velocity and acts in a similar way to a flux delimiter.

\subsection{Variational Multiscale Method}\label{chap2:sec2:subsec2}
One strategy that allows for a stable appoximation of the solution of the Navier-Stokes equations for same order Lagrange elements under the presence of large convection terms is the Variational Multiscale method (VMS). More precisely, one can generate a VMS-type discretization of the Navier-Stokes equations by splitting the solution into a resolved large scales $\vec{U}_h$ and a subgrid contributions $\vec{U}'$. This idea shares many similarities with other turbulence models used in finite difference and finite volume methods such as the Large Eddy Simulations (LES) or the Reynolds Averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS). In fact, it can be shown, that the stabilization technique that is going to be introduced can be seen an LES approach~\cite{Hughes2017}.

For the derivation of the VMS-type method that is going to be used in the hemodynamics simulations farther below, the problem in its strong form is going to be rewritten. The variables, $\vec{u}$ and $p$ are bundled into a single vector $\vec{U} = {[\vec{u}, p]}^T$. We introduce also the Navier-Stokes operator $\mathcal{L}$ and the right-hand side $\vec{F}$.
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{L}(\vec{u}_{conv}, \vec{U}) = \begin{bmatrix}
		\rho\partial_t \vec{u} + \rho(\vec{u}_{conv}\cdot\nabla)\vec{u} + \nabla p - \nabla\cdot2\mu\varepsilon(\vec{u}) \\
		\nabla \cdot \vec{u}
	\end{bmatrix}, \qquad
	\vec{F} = \begin{bmatrix}
		\rho\vec{g} \\
		0
	\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

Therefore, the strong form of the Navier-Stokes Equations  can be equally written as:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
		\mathcal{L}(\vec{u}_{conv}, \vec{U}) &= \vec{F}, \quad (\vec{x}, t) \in \Omega \times (0,T]\\
		\vec{u} &= \vec{u}_0, \quad \vec{x} \in \Omega, t=0\\
		\sigma \cdot \vec{n} &= \vec{h}, \quad (\vec{x}, t) \in \Gamma_{n} \times (0,T]\\
		\vec{u} &= \vec{u}_d, \quad (\vec{x}, t) \in \Gamma_{d} \times (0,T]
	\end{split}
	\label{eqn:short_strong}
\end{equation}
The weak form of the Navier-Stokes equations can be obtained by multiplying the strong form \autoref{eqn:short_strong} with tests functions $\vec{V} = {[\vec{w}, q]}^T$ contained in the Sobolev space $H^1(\Omega)$.

The multiplication of \autoref{eqn:short_strong} with the test functions $\vec{V}$ is subsequently integrated over $\Omega$.
\begin{equation}
	\int_{\Omega} \vec{V} \cdot \mathcal{L}(\vec{u}_{conv}, \vec{U}) \,dV = \int_{\Omega} \vec{V} \cdot \vec{F} \,dV, \quad \forall \vec{V} \in {[H^1(\Omega)]}^d
\end{equation}





VMS-type methods now introduce a decomposition of the solution into a resolved part $\vec{U}_h$ and a subgrid part $\vec{U}'$. The coarse scales are solved on the existing mesh with, in our case linear elements, while the subgrid scales are the (potentially) infinite-dimensional and will be modelled instead. The same decomposition is applied to the test functions $\vec{V}$. The function spaces

\begin{equation}
	\vec{U} = \vec{U}_h + \vec{U}', \qquad \vec{U} \in V_h =: \left\{ \vec{u}, p \in H^1(\Omega) \mid \vec{u} = \vec{u}_d\,\text{on}\,\Gamma_{d} \right\}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	\vec{V} = \vec{V}_h + \vec{V}'
\end{equation}

		\vec{U}_h, \vec{V}_h \in \vec{W_h} \oplus \vec{W'} =: \vec W\\
		\vec{W} \backslash \vec{W_h} = \vec{W'}
	\end{split}
\end{equation}
