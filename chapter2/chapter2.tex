\chapter{Title of the Chapter 2}
\minitoc\newpage\label{chap.2}
In the following chapter, the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations are introduced as the backbone to the hemodynamics simulations that are run. The equations are derived from the conservation of mass and momentum and are further reformulated in a weak sense. The obtained formulation is then extended with a Variational Multiscale-type Method (VMS) to stabilize the schemes in face of same-order test and trial functions for pressure and velocities. This additionally controls the otherwise ocurrring spurious oscillations caused by convection dominated flows, such as the present ones. The methodology is explained and validated on a series of conventional test cases in 2D and 3D. The chapter is concluded with a discussion on the numerical results and the implications of the findings.
\section{Governing Physics}\label{chap2:sec1}
The Navier-Stokes equations stem, as mentioned before from the conservation of mass and momentum in an open and bounded domain $\Omega \subset \mathbb {R}^{n}$ in which no material enters or leaves. For the former quantity, the conservation can be expressed through:
\begin{equation}
	\frac{d}{dt} \int_{\Omega(t)} \rho dV = 0,
\end{equation}
imposing that the integral of over the density in the control volume does not change. The derivative over the time-dependent domain can be simplified thanks to the Reynolds transport theorem, named after its discoverer Osborne Reynolds.

\begin{theorem}[Reynolds Transport Theorem]
	Let $\vec{f}(t, \vec{x})$ be any conservation quantity, then the Reynolds transport theorem states that for a velocity field $\vec{u}$, the following holds:
	\begin{equation}
		\frac {d}{dt}\int _{\Omega (t)}\vec {f} \,dV=\int _{\Omega (t)}{\frac {\partial \vec {f} }{\partial t}} + \nabla\cdot(\vec{u}\otimes\vec{f}) \,dV
	\end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}

	\begin{equation}
		\frac {d}{dt}\int _{\Omega (t)}\vec {f}(t, \vec{x}) \,dV = \lim _{\Delta t\to 0} \frac{1}{\Delta t} \left(\int _{\Omega (t+\Delta t, \vec{x})}\vec{f}(t+\Delta t, \vec{x}) \,dV-\int _{\Omega (t)}\vec{f}(t, \vec{x}) \,dV\right)
	\end{equation}
	To drag the derivative into the integral it is necessary to reformulate the problem in terms of a initial position $\vec{X}$ and a mapping $\vec{x}=\vec{\phi}(t, \vec{X})$. With the corresponding derivative $\nabla\vec{\phi} = F(t, \vec{X})$ and the Jacobian $J = \det F$, the integral can be rewritten written in terms of the initial domain $\Omega(0)$ and the contents can therefore be merged:

	\begin{align}
		\frac {d}{dt}\int _{\Omega (t)}\vec {f} \,dV & = \lim _{\Delta t\to 0} \frac{1}{\Delta t} \int _{\Omega (0)}\left[(\vec{f}\circ\vec{\phi})J\right](t+\Delta t, \vec{X}) - \left[(\vec{f}\circ\vec{\phi})J\right](t, \vec{X})\,dV \\
		                                             & = \int _{\Omega (0)} \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[(\vec{f}\circ\vec{\phi})J\right](t, \vec{X})\,dV                                                                            \\
		                                             & = \int _{\Omega (0)} \left[J\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\vec{f}\circ\vec{\phi}) + (\vec{f}\circ\vec{\phi})\frac{\partial J}{\partial t}\right](t, \vec{X})\,dV
	\end{align}
	To simplify the derivative of the Jacobian, recall Jacobi's formula.
	\begin{equation}
		\frac{\partial J}{\partial t} = J\tr\left(F^{-1}\frac{\partial F}{\partial t}\right)= J\tr\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\vec{x}}\frac{\partial \vec{\phi}}{\partial t}\right) = J(\nabla\cdot\vec{u})
	\end{equation}
	Now the integral can be written back in terms of a time-dependent domain.
	\begin{equation}
		\frac {d}{dt}\int _{\Omega (t)}\vec {f} \,dV = \int _{\Omega (t)}\frac{\partial\vec{f}}{\partial t} + (\vec{u}\cdot\nabla)\vec{f} + \vec{f}(\nabla\cdot\vec{u})\,dV = \int _{\Omega (t)}\frac{\partial\vec{f}}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(\vec{u}\otimes\vec{f})\,dV
	\end{equation}
\end{proof}
From now on all functions are taken to be time-dependent, as well as dependent on a location $\vec{x}$ in an eulerian framework, unless stated otherwise. The explicit dependencies are omitted for the sake of brevity. Also, the derivatives in time will be denoted by $\partial_{t}$.

Using the transport theorem, the conservation of mass can be expressed as:
\begin{equation}
	\int_\Omega \partial_{t}\rho + \nabla\cdot(\rho\vec{u})\,dV = 0
	\label{eq:massconservation_int}
\end{equation}
The present integral form can be reduced to a differential form with a short proof by contradiction. Suppose that the content of the integral from \autoref{eq:massconservation_int} is not zero in $\Omega$, then the integral would be adding positive and negative contributions over the domain. However, since the choice of $\Omega$ is arbitrary, the integral formulation has to hold for any subdomain of $\Omega$, including those in which the integrand is non-zero. With the contradiction, the integrand has to be zero in $\Omega$ and the differential form is obtained:
\begin{equation}
	\partial_{t}\rho + \nabla\cdot(\rho\vec{u}) = 0
	\label{eq:massconservation}
\end{equation}
In the same manner, the conservation of momentum is derived from preserving the volume-specific momentum $\rho\vec{u}$, which is modulated by body forces, such as the gravity, and stresses. The latter are the result of the interactions between the fluid particles and are expressed through the stress tensor $\sigma$. The conservation of momentum is then:
\begin{equation}
	\partial_t \rho\vec{u} + \nabla\cdot(\rho\vec{u}\otimes\vec{u}) = \nabla\cdot\sigma + \rho\vec{g}
	\label{eq:momentumconservation_conservative}
\end{equation}
The conservative formulation shown in \autoref{eq:momentumconservation_conservative} can be rewritten into the convective form by using the product rule and the conservation of mass.
\begin{equation}
	\rho\partial_t\vec{u} + \rho(\vec{u}\cdot\nabla)\vec{u} = \nabla\cdot\sigma + \rho\vec{g}
	\label{eq:momentumconservation_convective}
\end{equation}
The stress tensor $\sigma$ is taken to be the:\\
\begin{equation}
	\vec{\sigma}=-pI+\left(\lambda-\frac{2}{3}\mu\right)(\nabla \cdot \vec{u})I+\mu (\nabla \vec{u}+\nabla \vec{u}^T)
	\label{eq:stress}
\end{equation}

\begin{table}[ht]
	\begin{center}
		\begin{tabular}{c|c|c}
			$p$       & Pressure          & $N/m^2$   \\
			$\lambda$ & Bulk viscosity    & $kg/(ms)$ \\
			$\mu$     & Dynamic viscosity & $kg/(ms)$ \\
		\end{tabular}
	\end{center}
	\caption{Physical quantities of stress tensor.}
\end{table}
Blood, the medium to be described in the thesis, is formed by around 80\% water\cite{Beilin1966} and thus shares similar densities of around 1056$kg/m^3$ and incompressibility properties. Therefore, the mass conservation~\eqref{eq:massconservation} can be simplified to:
\begin{equation}
	\nabla\cdot \vec{u} = 0,
\end{equation}
also known as the continuity equation. Applying the continuity equation to the stress tensor~\eqref{eq:stress} cancels the bulk viscosity, related to the stress of compressing a medium. Inserting the stress tensor~\eqref{eq:stress} into the momentum equation~\eqref{eq:momentumconservation_convective} brings us to the final strong form of the Navier-Stokes equations:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{split}
		\rho\partial_t\vec{u} + \rho(\vec{u}\cdot\nabla)\vec{u} + \nabla p - \nabla \cdot \mu (\nabla \vec{u}+\nabla \vec{u}^T) &= \rho\vec{g}\\
		\nabla\cdot \vec{u} &= 0
	\end{split}
\end{equation}
